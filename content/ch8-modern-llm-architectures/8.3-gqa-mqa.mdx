## 1. Why this session exists

The KV-cache analysis of [6.14](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.14-kv-cache) ended with a numerical wall: for LLaMA-7B at a $128k$-token context, the KV cache is $67$ GB per sequence in fp16 — larger than the model parameters themselves. The cache is dominated by the count $2 \cdot L \cdot h \cdot d_h \cdot T$, and the only freely-tunable factor among those (without changing the model's hidden size $d = h d_h$, the depth $L$, or the served context $T$) is the number of *distinct* K and V projections per layer.

The structural observation behind grouped-query attention (GQA) and multi-query attention (MQA): there is no rule that says each query head must have its *own* $W_K$ and $W_V$. We can share K and V across groups of query heads, paying a small quality cost and getting a *linear* reduction in KV-cache size and in decode-step memory bandwidth.

This session derives the construction, counts the savings, and places GQA / MQA as the standard choice in 2024+ frontier dense models (Llama-3, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma). MLA, the more aggressive 2024 alternative used by DeepSeek, is the subject of §8.5.

## 2. The design space: from MHA to MQA

Standard multi-head attention (MHA, [6.4](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.4-multi-head-attention)) has $h$ query heads, $h$ key heads, and $h$ value heads — one of each per head index $\ell \in \{1, \dots, h\}$. Multi-query attention (MQA, Shazeer 2019<FN n={2} />, [arXiv:1911.02150](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02150)) shares **one** K head and **one** V head across all $h$ query heads. Grouped-query attention (GQA, Ainslie et al. 2023, [arXiv:2305.13245](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13245)) generalises: partition the $h$ query heads into $g$ groups of $h/g$ heads each, and use **one** K and **one** V projection per group, shared across the queries in that group.

Parameterise by the number of *KV groups* $g \in \{1, 2, \dots, h\}$:

| Variant | $g$ | K, V heads | Comment |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| MQA | $1$ | $1$ each | Most aggressive sharing |
| GQA-2 | $2$ | $2$ each | Two K, two V across all query heads |
| GQA-$g$ | $g$ | $g$ each | $g$ K-heads, $g$ V-heads |
| MHA | $h$ | $h$ each | No sharing — recover standard MHA |

GQA-$h$ is MHA; GQA-$1$ is MQA. So the choice is a single integer $g$.

### 2.1 The forward pass, formally

Fix hidden size $d$, query heads $h$, group count $g \mid h$, per-head dimension $d_h = d/h$. Define the projection matrices:

$$
W_Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}, \qquad W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times g d_h}, \qquad W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times g d_h}, \qquad W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}.
$$

The $W_Q$ matrix has the same shape as in MHA; the $W_K$ and $W_V$ matrices have width $g d_h$ instead of $h d_h = d$, i.e. they output $g$ (not $h$) K-heads. Given $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}$:

$$
Q = X W_Q \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times h d_h}, \quad K = X W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times g d_h}, \quad V = X W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times g d_h}.
$$

Reshape: $Q \to T \times h \times d_h$, $K \to T \times g \times d_h$, $V \to T \times g \times d_h$. Now per query head $\ell \in \{1, \dots, h\}$ we need to pick *which* K, V to use. Group head $\ell$ to KV group

$$
\boxed{\ \text{group}(\ell) = \left\lceil \frac{\ell \cdot g}{h} \right\rceil \in \{1, \dots, g\}, \ }
$$

i.e. the first $h/g$ query heads use K, V group $1$; the next $h/g$ query heads use group $2$; etc. Per query head, apply scaled dot-product attention ([6.2](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.2-scaled-dot-product-attention)) with the *shared* K and V of its group:

$$
\text{head}_\ell = \text{Attn}\!\left( Q_{:, \ell, :}, \ K_{:, \text{group}(\ell), :}, \ V_{:, \text{group}(\ell), :} \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_h}.
$$

Concatenate the $h$ heads, project: $\text{GQA}(X) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h) W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}$. The output shape matches MHA exactly. So GQA is a drop-in replacement for MHA at the level of input/output signatures; the change is interior to MHA.

## 3. KV-cache shrink: the headline win

The KV-cache equation of [6.14 §4](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.14-kv-cache) was

$$
\#\text{bytes}_\text{KV} = 2 \cdot \text{bpe} \cdot L \cdot h \cdot d_h \cdot T \cdot \text{batch},
$$

where $\text{bpe}$ is bytes per element. With GQA-$g$, the K, V tensors per layer have only $g$ (not $h$) heads. So

$$
\boxed{\ \#\text{bytes}_\text{KV}^\text{GQA} = 2 \cdot \text{bpe} \cdot L \cdot g \cdot d_h \cdot T \cdot \text{batch}. \ }
$$

The cache shrinks by factor $h/g$. For MQA ($g = 1$), this is $h\times$ — a $32\times$ reduction for $h = 32$. For GQA-$8$ in a $h = 64$ model, this is $8\times$.

Numerical example. LLaMA-3-70B uses GQA-$8$ with $h = 64$, $L = 80$, $d = 8192$, $d_h = 128$. At $T = 32k$:

$$
\text{KV (MHA)} = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 80 \cdot 64 \cdot 128 \cdot 32768 = 86 \text{ GB},
$$

$$
\text{KV (GQA-8)} = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 80 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 32768 = 11 \text{ GB}.
$$

The MHA version would not fit on an 80 GB H100 even for batch size 1; the GQA-$8$ version leaves $\sim 70$ GB for model weights and activations. This is the *operational* reason for GQA's adoption: it makes long-context serving with batch parallelism feasible on standard 80 GB accelerators.

### 3.1 Compute count and parameter count

Parameter count of the attention sub-layer, with $W_Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, $W_K, W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times g d_h}$, $W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$:

$$
\#\theta_\text{GQA} = d^2 + 2 d \cdot g d_h + d^2 = 2 d^2 + 2 d^2 \cdot \frac{g}{h} = 2 d^2 \left( 1 + \frac{g}{h} \right).
$$

For $g = h$ (MHA) this is $4 d^2$ — the value of [6.4 §3](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.4-multi-head-attention). For $g = 1$ (MQA) it is $2 d^2 + 2 d^2 / h \approx 2 d^2$ — about half of MHA. Per-layer parameter savings are modest; the dominant win is the KV cache.

FLOPs per training-time forward pass per layer (compare [6.12](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.12-compute-and-memory)):

- **Projections** ($Q, K, V, $ output): $2 d^2 T + 2 \cdot 2 d^2 (g/h) T + 2 d^2 T \approx 4 d^2 T (1 + g/(2h))$. For MQA this is $\approx 4 d^2 T$ instead of $8 d^2 T$ — half.
- **Score matmul** $Q K^\top / \sqrt{d_h}$ per head: $2 T^2 d_h$ per head, $h$ heads $= 2 T^2 d$. Independent of $g$.
- **Mix with $V$**: $2 T^2 d$. Independent of $g$.

So the dominant FLOPs (the $T^2 d$ terms) are the same for MQA, GQA, and MHA at training time. Decode-time FLOPs are dominated by the projections, which do shrink slightly with $g$. But the headline number is the KV-cache memory and the *bandwidth* to read it during decode.

### 3.2 Decode bandwidth, the operational metric

At decode, per generated token, we read $K \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times g d_h}$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times g d_h}$ from HBM, perform a single-query attention of shape $1 \times T$, and discard most of the multiplied-out result. The bandwidth cost per layer per step is

$$
\#\text{bytes-read}^\text{KV}_\text{step} = 2 \cdot \text{bpe} \cdot g \cdot d_h \cdot T = 2 \cdot \text{bpe} \cdot d \cdot T \cdot (g/h).
$$

For MQA, $g/h = 1/h$, so decode-step KV bandwidth drops by $h\times$. Combined with the (model-parameter-fixed) bandwidth to read the layer's weights, this gives a clean wall-clock decode speedup that scales with $h/g$ until the parameter-reading bandwidth dominates. For LLaMA-3-70B ($h = 64, g = 8$), the empirical decode-step speedup vs the hypothetical MHA-70B is $\sim 5$–$8\times$ depending on context length and batch.

## 4. The quality cost

The harder question: how much quality do we lose by tying K, V across query heads?

Ainslie et al. (2023)<FN n={1} /> measured this empirically by *uptraining* MHA checkpoints into GQA and MQA. The recipe:

1. Take an existing MHA-trained checkpoint with $h$ K and V heads.
2. **Mean-pool** the $h$ K-heads down to $g$ K-heads (and same for V): each group's K is the average of the $h/g$ original K's it absorbs.
3. Continue training for a small fraction ($\sim 5\%$) of the original training budget with the GQA mask in place.

Result: with $g = 8$, post-uptraining quality is within $\sim 0.1$ perplexity of the original MHA checkpoint on standard benchmarks. With $g = 1$ (MQA), quality drops a measurable $0.3$–$0.7$ perplexity. The interpretation: collapsing all heads to a single K, V pair loses some of the per-head specialisation observed in trained MHA models ([6.4 §4](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.4-multi-head-attention)), but $g = 8$ still has enough specialisation to recover most of the quality.

This is the empirical sweet spot the field converged on:

| Model | $h$ | $g$ | Variant |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| LLaMA-1, LLaMA-2-7B | 32 | 32 | MHA |
| LLaMA-2-34B / 70B | 56 / 64 | 8 | GQA-$8$ |
| LLaMA-3 (all sizes ≥ 8B) | 32 / 64 | 8 | GQA-$8$ |
| Mistral-7B | 32 | 8 | GQA-$8$ |
| Qwen-2 | 32 / 64 | 8 | GQA-$8$ |
| Gemma-2 | 32 / 48 | 8 / 16 | GQA-$8$ / GQA-$16$ |
| Falcon-180B | 64 | 1 | MQA |
| PaLM (early) | — | 1 | MQA |

**GQA-$8$ is the de facto 2024–2026 default** for dense decoder-only models. MQA still appears in a few large dense models (Falcon-180B, PaLM); the empirical view is that at very large $h$, even $g = 1$ has enough effective capacity, but the $g = 8$ point gives a quality-cost insurance policy with little additional cache.

<Aside type="note">
**Why $g = 8$ specifically?** Three coincident reasons. (1) Empirically, the perplexity gap to MHA closes between $g = 4$ and $g = 8$ in Ainslie et al.'s uptraining curves and saturates thereafter. (2) A typical tensor-parallel split of MHA shards the query heads across $\text{TP-size}$ devices ([7.9 §2](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.9-tensor-pipeline-parallelism)); $g = 8$ aligns with common TP sizes of 8 (one DGX node), so each TP rank handles one group's K, V locally. (3) $h/g = 8$ keeps each KV group serving exactly one query "team" of $h/g$ heads, which empirically retains most of the head specialisation.
</Aside>

## 5. Uptraining vs from-scratch

A model can be either trained from scratch with GQA or uptrained from an MHA checkpoint. Three observations.

**(a) From-scratch training is no harder than MHA training.** Same losses, same recipes, same hyperparameters. The only change is the per-layer projection shapes. LLaMA-3 was trained from scratch with GQA-$8$; no pre-existing MHA checkpoint was involved.

**(b) Uptraining from MHA works and is cheap.** The mean-pool initialisation closes most of the gap with $\sim 5\%$ of original training compute. The technique is useful when an organisation already has an MHA model and wants to shrink its KV cache without retraining from scratch — LLaMA-2-70B's GQA variant was produced this way.

**(c) The MHA → GQA transition is unidirectional.** Once trained with $g$ groups, the model has internalised the group structure; you cannot recover the lost K, V diversity by "unpooling" the projections. This matters for serving: a model trained with $g = 8$ cannot be served with $g = 64$ to recover MHA quality at inference time.

## 6. Where GQA fits with the rest of Chapter 8

GQA is one of two attention-side KV-cache shrinkers; MLA (§8.5) is the other. They differ in their structural device:

- **GQA** shrinks the *number* of K, V heads — fewer projections, but each is full per-head-dimension.
- **MLA** shrinks the *dimension* of K, V via a low-rank latent — the *number* of heads is preserved (in the $Q$-side count), but K and V are stored compressed and decompressed at attention time.

They are not mutually exclusive (one could imagine GQA combined with MLA), but in practice no model uses both: GQA is the simpler, more widely-adopted option in dense Western LLMs, and MLA is DeepSeek's choice for their V2/V3/R1 lineage. We will derive MLA carefully in §8.5 to compare.

The other Chapter 8 sessions are orthogonal to the GQA/MLA choice: SwiGLU (§8.4) touches the FFN, MoE (§8.6) replaces the FFN with a router-plus-experts, Mamba (§8.7) replaces attention entirely with an SSM, hybrids (§8.8) mix Mamba and attention layers. Frontier dense models in 2026 typically combine GQA-$8$ + SwiGLU + RMSNorm + RoPE inside the block diagram of [8.1 §6](/session/ch8-modern-llm-architectures/8.1-decoder-only).

## 7. What we keep going forward

Three takeaways for the rest of the chapter:

1. **GQA-$g$ is parameterised by one integer.** Replace $h$ K-heads and $h$ V-heads with $g$ K-heads and $g$ V-heads; tie groups of $h/g$ query heads to each KV group. Default $g = 8$.

2. **The shrink is exactly $h/g$ in KV cache and decode-step KV bandwidth.** That is the operational reason it became the 2024+ default.

3. **Quality cost is small** ($\lesssim 0.1$ perplexity) at $g = 8$ for dense models in the $7$B–$70$B range, after either from-scratch training or $\sim 5\%$-budget uptraining from MHA.

GQA is the first of three KV-cache treatments in Chapter 8. The next, SwiGLU (§8.4), shifts to the FFN side; MLA (§8.5) returns to attention with a more aggressive compression.
