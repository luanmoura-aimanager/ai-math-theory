## 1. Why this session exists

Chapter 9 closed the alignment loop: with SFT, RLHF or DPO, GRPO, and inference-time selection, we have a *policy* $\pi_\theta$ that gives us, for every prefix $x_{<t}$, a next-token distribution

$$
\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{<t}) \in \Delta^{|V|-1}.
$$

Chapter 10 opens the question of how to *use* that distribution at deployment time: given the per-token distribution, how do we produce a complete sequence?

There are two families. **Deterministic search** picks tokens by some rule — argmax (greedy) or beam-restricted argmax (beam search). **Stochastic sampling** draws from a (transformed) distribution — covered in [10.2](/session/ch10-inference-decoding/10.2-sampling-temperature-top-k-top-p).

This session does the deterministic family carefully. The headline conclusion is uncomfortable: **beam search, the workhorse of pre-Transformer NMT, hurts open-ended LLM generation**. Understanding *why* sets up everything else in this chapter.<FN n={1} />

---

## 2. The autoregressive decoding problem

Recall the autoregressive language model factorisation ([5.3](/session/ch5-sequence-modeling/5.3-language-modeling-objective)):

$$
p_\theta(y_{1:T} \mid x) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{T} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
$$

Decoding is the optimisation problem

$$
y^\star \;=\; \arg\max_{y_{1:T},\, T \le T_{\max}} \; \log p_\theta(y_{1:T} \mid x).
$$

This search space has size $|V|^{T_{\max}}$ — for $|V|=32\text{k}$ and $T_{\max}=2048$, roughly $10^{9{,}300}$ candidate sequences. Exact optimisation is hopeless. Every practical decoder is an approximation.

Two design axes:

1. **Greedy vs. lookahead.** At each step, commit to the token that looks best locally, or maintain alternatives and re-evaluate after seeing more context.
2. **Deterministic vs. stochastic.** Pick by argmax, or sample from a distribution.

Greedy and beam search are both deterministic; greedy is no-lookahead, beam search is bounded-lookahead.

---

## 3. Greedy decoding

The simplest decoder: at each step, take the token of maximum probability.

```
Greedy(x):
    y_1 = argmax_v π_θ(v | x)
    for t = 2, ..., T_max:
        y_t = argmax_v π_θ(v | x, y_{<t})
        if y_t = <EOS>: break
    return y_{1:t}
```

### 3.1 Cost

One model forward per token, $T$ tokens total. With the KV cache ([6.14](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.14-kv-cache)), each step costs $\Theta(d_\text{model}^2 + d_\text{model} \cdot T)$ FLOPs and reuses cached keys/values for the prefix.

$$
\boxed{\;\text{FLOPs}_\text{greedy} \;\approx\; T \cdot \big( 2 N + d_\text{model} \cdot T \big), \;\;\text{memory} = O(N + d_\text{model} \cdot T)\;}
$$

where $N$ is the parameter count (the $2N$ factor is the standard "one fwd ≈ 2N FLOPs per token" rule from [7.3](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.3-forward-pass-at-scale)). The $d_\text{model} \cdot T$ term is attention against the cached prefix.

### 3.2 What greedy gets wrong

Greedy maximises the local conditional $\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$ at each step, but this is *not* the joint argmax. Two failure modes.

**Pathological loops.** If the model assigns high probability to a token that, once emitted, makes itself most probable again, the decoder enters a loop ("the the the …" or "I think I think I think …"). This is endemic in undertrained models and in regions of context space the model hasn't seen.

**Premature commitment.** A high-probability token at step $t$ may force the model into a low-probability region later. The classic toy:

$$
\pi(y_1 = A) = 0.6, \;\;\; \pi(y_1 = B) = 0.4.
$$

Greedy picks $A$. But suppose $\pi(y_2 = z \mid A) = 0.1$ and $\pi(y_2 = z \mid B) = 0.9$. Then

$$
p(A, z) = 0.06, \quad p(B, z) = 0.36.
$$

Greedy's sequence $A,z$ has joint probability $6\%$; the better sequence $B,z$ has $36\%$ but greedy never explores it.

This is the motivation for *lookahead* — beam search.

## 4. Beam search

Maintain a set of $k$ partial hypotheses (the "beam") and at each step expand all of them, keeping only the top-$k$ by joint log-prob.

```
BeamSearch(x, k):
    B_0 = { (∅, 0.0) }
    for t = 1, ..., T_max:
        candidates = []
        for (y, s) in B_{t-1}:
            if y ends in <EOS>: candidates.append((y, s)); continue
            for v in V:
                candidates.append((y ⊕ v, s + log π_θ(v | x, y)))
        B_t = top-k candidates by score
        if all B_t end in <EOS>: break
    return argmax B_T by score
```

### 4.1 Cost

Each step expands $k$ live hypotheses against $|V|$ next tokens, then prunes to $k$. Forward passes: $k$ per step, $T$ steps, so $k T$ total. KV cache must be maintained *per beam* (each hypothesis has its own continuation):

$$
\boxed{\;\text{FLOPs}_\text{beam} \approx k \cdot T \cdot (2N + d_\text{model} \cdot T), \;\;\text{memory} = O(k \cdot (N + d_\text{model} \cdot T))\;}
$$

So beam is $k\times$ greedy in both axes. Typical $k = 4$–$8$ for NMT; beyond ~$10$ the marginal quality gain flattens for almost all tasks.

### 4.2 Length normalisation

The naive score $\log p(y_{1:T}) = \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid \cdot)$ accumulates a *negative* number per step (since $\log p \le 0$). Longer sequences always score worse. Beam search prefers short sequences pathologically.

The standard fix is **length-normalised scoring**:

$$
\boxed{\;s_\alpha(y_{1:T}) \;=\; \frac{1}{T^\alpha} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\;}
$$

with $\alpha \in [0.6, 1.0]$. $\alpha = 1$ is the per-token log-likelihood; smaller $\alpha$ penalises length less. NMT systems typically use $\alpha \approx 0.7$.

<Aside type="note">
**Why $\alpha < 1$ helps.** With $\alpha = 1$, the model's bias is exactly cancelled (long and short are scored on the same per-token basis). But in NMT, the *source* dictates target length and pure per-token scoring still under-generates. $\alpha < 1$ rewards completing the translation rather than truncating. The 0.6–0.7 range was tuned empirically by Wu et al. (Google NMT, 2016) and persists.
</Aside>

### 4.3 Coverage penalties

NMT systems also add a *coverage* term — a reward for translating each source token at least once, computed from attention weights. This is specific to encoder-decoder MT and doesn't transfer to decoder-only LLMs (no source to cover).

## 5. The beam search curse

In Sequence-to-Sequence MT, beam-$k$ with $k=4$–$8$ reliably outperforms greedy by 1–2 BLEU. In *open-ended* generation — story continuation, dialogue, instruction-following — beam search *underperforms* greedy and dramatically underperforms stochastic sampling.

This counterintuitive result is the "beam search curse". Holtzman et al. ([2019<FN n={2} />, arXiv:1904.09751](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09751)) documented it: as $k$ increases, generated text becomes *more* repetitive, *less* diverse, and *lower* quality (by human evaluation) — even though the joint log-likelihood goes up.

### 5.1 Diagnosis: argmax of a calibrated LM is degenerate

The next-token distribution of a well-trained LM has heavy tail: many plausible continuations have small but nonzero probability. The argmax sequence — the *single most likely* string — is highly atypical of the distribution. It is short, generic, and repetitive, because those properties accumulate small probability advantages step after step.

Formally, consider a unigram model with $\pi(\text{the}) = 0.05$, $\pi(\text{a}) = 0.04$, etc. The argmax sequence is "the the the the the…". Probability: $0.05^T$. The *typical* sequence under the model is something like "The cat sat on the mat", which has lower per-step probability but is far more plausible.

This is the *typical set* phenomenon from information theory: for sources with entropy $H$, almost all probability mass is on sequences of approximate per-token log-prob $-H$, which is *not* the argmax. Beam search drives towards the mode of $p(y_{1:T})$; sampling stays in the typical set.

### 5.2 The plot from Holtzman et al.

Beam-$k$ generated text exhibits **degenerate repetition**: after some tokens, the same phrase begins to repeat verbatim. The probability of the *repeated* phrase actually *increases* with each repetition — the model becomes more confident in repetition the more it repeats — so beam search locks in.

Empirically, the curse is sharpest for $k \ge 4$ on open-ended generation. Even greedy ($k = 1$) exhibits some repetition; the cure is to introduce *stochasticity* (Cap 10.2) or *repetition penalties* (post-hoc score adjustments that downweight tokens recently emitted).

## 6. When beam search is still right

Beam search isn't dead. Three settings where it works:

1. **Machine translation.** Source dictates target. Output length is bounded by source length; faithfulness matters more than diversity. Beam-4 with length-normalised scoring remains state of the art for NMT, including modern neural systems.
2. **Constrained generation.** Tasks where the output must match a schema (JSON, SQL, code with syntactic constraints) benefit from beam search combined with a constraint-aware scoring function that prunes invalid prefixes.
3. **Short, factual responses.** "Yes/no" answers, single-word completions, classification framed as generation. The output is short enough that the typical-set vs. mode distinction barely applies.

For everything else — open-ended dialogue, instruction-following, code, creative writing — sampling-based decoding (Cap 10.2) wins.

<Aside type="history">
The transition is visible in the literature: GNMT (Wu et al., 2016)<FN n={3} /> used beam-8 with length normalisation; T5 (2019) defaulted to beam search; GPT-2 (2019) defaulted to top-$k$ sampling; GPT-3 onwards almost never reports beam-search numbers for open-ended tasks. By 2026, beam search persists in the HuggingFace `generate()` API mostly for backwards compatibility and translation benchmarks.
</Aside>

## 7. Diverse beam search and group beam search

A line of work tried to fix beam search by *forcing diversity* across beams.

**Diverse Beam Search** (Vijayakumar et al., 2016, [arXiv:1610.02424](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.02424)) partitions the $k$ beams into $G$ groups and applies a *dissimilarity penalty* between groups:

$$
s_g(y_{1:T}) \;=\; \frac{1}{T^\alpha}\sum_{t} \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid \cdot) \;-\; \lambda \sum_{g' < g} \mathrm{sim}(y, y^{(g')}).
$$

In practice this is rarely used today; the moment you accept *some* diversity, plain sampling with temperature/top-p is simpler and equally effective.

## 8. Implementation notes

A few details that matter for a correct implementation:

- **EOS handling.** A hypothesis ending in `<EOS>` is *finished*; it should not be expanded further, but its score still competes with live hypotheses in the top-$k$ selection. Otherwise long, fluent-but-pointless beams crowd out short, complete ones.
- **KV cache management.** Each beam needs its own KV cache. When beam $i$ is pruned, its cache memory is freed; when beam $j$ branches into two top-$k$ continuations (rare but possible), the cache must be *cloned*. Production beam implementations therefore maintain a *tree* of caches with reference counting.
- **Batching.** Beam search batches naturally: the $k$ beams form a batch of size $k$, and tokens within a batch are independent. This is a free $k\times$ speedup on a GPU — relevant for NMT serving but not for the LLM case where $k=1$ (greedy) dominates.

---

## 9. What we keep going forward

Three things.

1. **Greedy decoding** is the simplest baseline: argmax at each step, $T$ forwards total, fast and deterministic, but vulnerable to loops and premature commitment.
2. **Beam search** maintains $k$ hypotheses with lookahead; costs $k\times$ greedy in compute and memory; needs length normalisation; remains the right choice for machine translation and constrained generation.
3. **The beam search curse**: on open-ended generation, the *argmax of a calibrated LM* is degenerate (repetitive, generic), because most probability mass sits in the typical set, not at the mode. Sampling — Cap 10.2 — is the cure.

The rest of this chapter takes the sampling-based decoding for granted and turns to *engineering*: KV-cache details (10.3), speculative decoding (10.4), FlashAttention (10.5), quantisation (10.6), test-time scaling (10.7), continuous batching (10.8), agentic decoding (10.9).
