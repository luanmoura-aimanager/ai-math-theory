## 1. The minimum vocabulary

A *random variable* $X$ is a measurable function from a sample space
$\Omega$ to $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{R}^d$). In ML we almost never
construct $\Omega$ explicitly — what we manipulate are the induced
distributions $p(x)$. Two kinds matter:

- **Discrete:** $X$ takes values in a countable set; $p(x)$ is a
  probability mass function with $\sum_x p(x) = 1$.
- **Continuous:** $X$ takes values in $\mathbb{R}^d$; $p(x)$ is a
  probability density function with $\int p(x)\,dx = 1$. Density values
  $p(x)$ are *not* probabilities — only integrals over sets are.

The distinction is fundamental but the formulas to follow have parallel
shapes; we'll write them side by side.

## 2. Expectation

The expectation of a function $g(X)$ under $p$:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{p}[g(X)] \;=\;
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\sum_x g(x)\, p(x) & \text{discrete}, \\[6pt]
\displaystyle\int g(x)\, p(x)\, dx & \text{continuous}.
\end{cases}
$$

Three properties that we use without comment from here on:

- **Linearity.** $\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]$
  for any $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, *whether or not $X$ and $Y$ are
  independent*. Linearity is the single most useful identity in this
  course.
- **Constant.** $\mathbb{E}[c] = c$.
- **Independence ⇒ factorisation.** If $X \perp Y$, then
  $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y]$. The converse is false.

ML example: the *empirical risk* on a training set of size $m$ is

$$
\hat R(\theta) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \ell(x_i, y_i; \theta)
\;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\hat p}[\ell(X, Y; \theta)],
$$

where $\hat p$ is the empirical distribution placing mass $1/m$ on each
training point. Expectation under the empirical distribution and "mean
over the training set" are the *same object*. This will matter when we
re-derive the cross-entropy loss as a sample-average MLE in §0.7.

## 3. Variance and standard deviation

$$
\mathrm{Var}[X] \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\big].
$$

Expanding the square and using linearity gives the formula we'll use
constantly:

$$
\boxed{\;\mathrm{Var}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2.\;}
$$

Derivation, no skipped steps. Let $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Var}[X]
&= \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2] \\
&= \mathbb{E}[X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2] \\
&= \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X] + \mu^2 \\
&= \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2 \\
&= \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Standard deviation: $\sigma_X = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[X]}$.

Two properties:

- **Scaling.** $\mathrm{Var}[aX + b] = a^2\,\mathrm{Var}[X]$. The shift
  $b$ drops out (variance is translation-invariant); the scale gets
  squared (variance scales like length-squared).
- **Independence ⇒ additivity.** If $X \perp Y$, $\mathrm{Var}[X + Y] =
  \mathrm{Var}[X] + \mathrm{Var}[Y]$. *Crucial* for understanding why
  batch-norm and layer-norm work the way they do (Ch. 3).

## 4. Covariance and correlation

For two scalar random variables,

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])\big]
\;=\; \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y].
$$

Correlation is the unit-free version: $\rho_{XY} = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) /
(\sigma_X \sigma_Y) \in [-1, 1]$.

For a vector random variable $X \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with mean
$\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$, the *covariance matrix* is

$$
\Sigma \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mu)(X-\mu)^\top\big] \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.
$$

By construction $\Sigma$ is symmetric positive-semidefinite. The
diagonal contains the per-component variances; off-diagonals are
pairwise covariances. This is the object PCA diagonalises and that
Gaussian likelihoods invert.

## 5. The law of total expectation (tower property)

For any random variables $X, Y$,

$$
\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}[Y] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_X\!\big[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\big].\;}
$$

In words: average over $Y$ given $X$, then average that conditional
expectation over $X$. The two-step average equals the one-step average.

Quick proof (continuous case):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_X[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]]
&= \int p(x) \left( \int y \, p(y \mid x)\, dy \right) dx \\
&= \int\!\!\int y \, p(x)\, p(y \mid x)\, dy\, dx \\
&= \int\!\!\int y \, p(x, y)\, dy\, dx \\
&= \mathbb{E}[Y].
\end{aligned}
$$

ML example: the noise term in a regression model is often given as
$\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0$. The tower property then gives
$\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0$ unconditionally — but the converse is not
true. This distinction is at the heart of why ordinary least squares
gives an unbiased estimator under the conditional-mean-zero assumption.

## 6. Why this matters going forward

Almost every loss in ML is an expectation. Almost every regularisation
trick is a variance manipulation. Cross-entropy (§0.7) is an
expectation. KL divergence (§0.6) is an expectation. Dropout
(Ch. 3) is a variance argument. Attention scores (Ch. 6) are
normalised so that they form a probability distribution and have a
well-defined expectation over values.

Keep linearity of expectation as your reflex move; it solves more
problems than any other identity in this course.
