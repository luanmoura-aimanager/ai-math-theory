## 1. Joints, marginals, and conditionals

Given a joint distribution $p(x, y)$ on a pair $(X, Y)$:

- The **marginal** of $X$ is obtained by summing/integrating $Y$ out:
  $$
  p(x) = \sum_y p(x, y) \quad\text{or}\quad \int p(x, y)\, dy.
  $$
  Marginalisation is *the* universal operation for getting rid of a
  variable you don't care about right now.

- The **conditional** of $Y$ given $X = x$ is the joint normalised by
  the marginal:
  $$
  p(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)}, \qquad \text{defined when } p(x) > 0.
  $$
  Conditioning is *the* universal operation for asking "what do I know
  about $Y$ once I've observed $X$?".

These two are dual: marginalising forgets, conditioning updates.

## 2. The product rule (chain rule of probability)

Rearranging the definition of conditional probability gives the *product
rule*:

$$
\boxed{\;p(x, y) = p(x)\,p(y \mid x) = p(y)\,p(x \mid y).\;}
$$

The two factorisations exist because we can condition in either
direction. The identity itself is trivial — but equating the two
right-hand sides is exactly Bayes' rule.

## 3. Bayes' rule

Equate the two factorisations of the joint:

$$
p(x)\,p(y \mid x) = p(y)\,p(x \mid y),
$$

and solve for the conditional we typically *want* in inference:

$$
\boxed{\;p(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid y)\, p(y)}{p(x)}.\;}
$$

The denominator $p(x)$ is just normalisation; expanded via
marginalisation,

$$
p(x) = \sum_{y'} p(x \mid y')\, p(y'),
$$

it is the prior-weighted average likelihood of observing $x$ across all
hypotheses. The four named pieces:

- $p(y)$ — **prior**, what you believed about $Y$ before seeing data.
- $p(x \mid y)$ — **likelihood**, the model's probability of generating
  $x$ given hypothesis $y$.
- $p(y \mid x)$ — **posterior**, updated belief about $Y$ after
  observing $x$.
- $p(x)$ — **evidence** / marginal likelihood, a normaliser.

In ML the model is usually parametrised by $\theta$ and we write
$p(x \mid \theta)$ for the likelihood; $\theta$ replaces $y$ in the
formula and prior/posterior become priors/posteriors over parameters.

## 4. The binary classifier $p(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma(w^\top x)$

This is the model behind logistic regression and behind the output layer
of every binary deep classifier. The self-test in the roadmap asks: what
is the implicit generative story?

The answer is *not* "we drew $x$ from some Gaussian and applied a
deterministic rule". The model is more honest than that: it directly
specifies the posterior $p(y \mid x)$ and says nothing at all about
$p(x)$. This is the **discriminative** stance: model
$p(y \mid x)$ directly; don't bother modelling how $x$ came to be.

Compare with the **generative** stance, which would model $p(x \mid y)$
and $p(y)$, then derive the posterior by Bayes' rule:

$$
p(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid y)\, p(y)}{p(x)}.
$$

For example, if we assumed $X \mid Y = y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_y,
\Sigma)$ (Gaussian class-conditionals with shared covariance) and a
Bernoulli prior over $Y$, then a few lines of algebra (which we don't
need today) give

$$
p(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma(w^\top x + b)
$$

for specific $w$ and $b$ derived from $\mu_0, \mu_1, \Sigma$, and the
prior. The logistic-regression *functional form* is consistent with a
Gaussian generative story — but logistic regression itself doesn't
commit to that story.

The point: $p(y \mid x) = \sigma(w^\top x)$ is *the same conditional* as
the one Bayes' rule would produce from a class-conditional Gaussian
generative model, but logistic regression learns $w$ directly from the
data without ever estimating the Gaussian parameters. Discriminative
training is often more sample-efficient when all you need is the
decision rule.

<Aside type="note">
Most of deep learning, including LLMs, is discriminative — even when the
output looks generative. A language model assigns
$p(x_{t+1} \mid x_{1:t})$: that's a conditional, learned by maximum
likelihood on a corpus, with no explicit generative process for $x_{1:t}$
itself. The chain rule of probability stitches the conditionals into a
joint, but the joint is *induced* by the conditionals, not directly
modelled.
</Aside>

## 5. Independence and conditional independence

$X$ and $Y$ are **independent** ($X \perp Y$) iff

$$
p(x, y) = p(x)\, p(y) \quad \text{for all } x, y,
$$

equivalently $p(y \mid x) = p(y)$ for all $x$ with $p(x) > 0$. Observing
$X$ tells you nothing new about $Y$.

$X$ and $Y$ are **conditionally independent given $Z$**
($X \perp Y \mid Z$) iff

$$
p(x, y \mid z) = p(x \mid z)\, p(y \mid z) \quad \text{for all } z.
$$

Conditional independence is the structural primitive behind graphical
models, the naive-Bayes classifier (assumes features are independent
given the label), and the layered structure of probabilistic deep models
later on. Worth keeping in mind even when you don't draw a graph.

## 6. Where this is going

Bayes' rule is the lens through which we will read MLE, MAP, variational
inference, and the discriminative-versus-generative distinction that
runs all the way through the course. Cross-entropy as a loss (§0.7) is
MLE on a discriminative model. The score function in policy gradient
(Ch. 9) is the gradient of a log-likelihood. The "likelihood" of an
LLM output token is the conditional next-token probability. Same
machinery, used many times.
