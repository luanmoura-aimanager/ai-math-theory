## 1. Why this session exists

All of *backpropagation* is exactly one thing: the chain rule applied to
a directed acyclic graph (DAG). If you leave this session knowing how to
write the chain rule as a *sum over paths in the graph*, all of Chapter 2
(MLPs and backprop) becomes bookkeeping rather than mystery.

You already know the scalar version from Calc I. What we'll do here is
lift it first to several intermediate variables, then to general DAGs,
and finally walk through the canonical example pen-in-hand.

## 2. Refresher: scalar chain rule

If $y = f(g(x))$ and we set $u = g(x)$, then $y = f(u)$ and

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\,\frac{du}{dx}.
$$

Conceptually, a small perturbation $\Delta x$ produces $\Delta u \approx
g'(x)\,\Delta x$, which in turn produces $\Delta y \approx
f'(u)\,\Delta u = f'(u)\,g'(x)\,\Delta x$. In the limit, the ratio
$\Delta y/\Delta x$ is the product of the two local derivatives.

Warm-up example: $y = \sin(x^2)$, set $u = x^2$, $y = \sin(u)$, so

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \cos(u) \cdot 2x = 2x\cos(x^2).
$$

Nothing new. But notice the structure: *we multiply the local derivative
at every link in the chain*. This structure is the only thing that
survives the generalisation.

## 3. Multivariable with one scalar input

Suppose now that $y$ depends on $n$ intermediates $u_1, \dots, u_n$, and
that each $u_i$ depends on $x$:

$$
y = h(u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n), \qquad u_i = g_i(x).
$$

A perturbation $\Delta x$ now propagates through $n$ parallel paths —
one per $u_i$ — and the effects add linearly to first order:

$$
\Delta y \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n}
\frac{\partial h}{\partial u_i}(u)\,\Delta u_i
= \sum_{i=1}^{n}
\frac{\partial h}{\partial u_i}(u)\,g_i'(x)\,\Delta x.
$$

Dividing by $\Delta x$ and taking the limit,

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{dy}{dx} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}
\frac{\partial y}{\partial u_i}\,\frac{du_i}{dx}.\;}
$$

This is the *multivariable chain rule with one input*. Note its shape:
a sum over links, each link carrying a product of local derivatives.
This is more than coincidence; it's the special case of a much more
general identity, coming next.

## 4. DAGs and the sum over paths

In any deep learning model, the output $L$ (say, the loss) is computed
from an input $x$ through a potentially complicated sequence of
intermediate operations. We represent this computation as a *directed
acyclic graph*: each node is a variable (scalar, vector, or tensor), and
each directed edge $u \to v$ indicates that $v$ is a direct function of
$u$ (and possibly of other variables).

The question is: how does $L$ depend on $x$ overall? In the scalar case
the answer is the *sum over paths*:

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}
= \sum_{\substack{\text{directed paths} \\ p:\, x \to L}}
\;\prod_{\text{edge } u \to v \,\in\, p}
\frac{\partial v}{\partial u}.\;}
$$

In words: to find how $L$ responds to $x$, walk *every* directed path in
the DAG from $x$ to $L$; along each path, multiply the local partials
along its edges; sum the results.

The proof is an easy induction on graph depth. For a node $v$ fed
directly by $u_1, \dots, u_k$,

$$
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}
= \sum_{i=1}^{k}
\frac{\partial v}{\partial u_i}\,\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x},
$$

and expanding each $\partial u_i/\partial x$ recursively until reaching
$x$, each term in the final expansion corresponds to one path $x \to
\cdots \to v$. Applying this with $v = L$ gives the identity.

<Aside type="note">
The sum-over-paths is the *declarative* form of the chain rule: it tells
you what the result is, without prescribing a computation order. *Forward
mode* and *reverse mode* are two different strategies for organising the
sum to compute it efficiently — reverse mode is what PyTorch's
`backward()` implements, and it is asymptotically cheaper when the output
is scalar (a loss) and the input is a vector of many parameters. More on
this in Chapter 2.
</Aside>

<Aside type="history">
"Sum over paths in a graph, with a product over edges" should ring a
bell for anyone who has seen the Feynman path integral: there the
quantum amplitude of going from $A$ to $B$ is a sum over all possible
trajectories, each weighted by $e^{iS/\hbar}$. Here each path's "weight"
is the product of local derivatives. The analogy is not cosmetic: both
reflect the same combinatorial structure of linearising compositions.
</Aside>

## 5. The computational graph as a data structure

In practice, a deep learning framework builds the DAG in two phases:

1. **Forward pass.** Evaluate each node in topological order from input
   $x$ to output $L$, storing the intermediate values $u_i$ (because
   they appear in the local derivatives).
2. **Backward pass.** Walk the graph in reverse topological order,
   accumulating $\partial L/\partial u_i$ at each node by summing
   contributions from its children. When it reaches $x$, you have
   $\partial L/\partial x$ ready.

Each edge carries *one local partial* (scalar, vector, or matrix
depending on the node types); each node is responsible for summing the
contributions arriving from its children. That's it.

The advantage of the graph framing over memorising chain-rule formulas
is scalability: the same algorithm handles linear chains, branches,
joins (multiple paths meeting at the same node), and any DAG, with no
special cases.

## 6. Worked example: $f(x) = \sin(x^2)\cdot e^{x}$

Computing $\partial f/\partial x$ two ways: (a) by the direct scalar
chain rule, as a check, and (b) by the sum over paths in the DAG, to
internalise the method that will scale to deep networks.

### 6.1 Identifying intermediate nodes

Decompose the function into elementary operations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= x^2, \\
b &= \sin(a), \\
c &= e^{x}, \\
f &= b\cdot c.
\end{aligned}
$$

$x$ appears in *two* places in the original formula — once
inside $\sin(x^2)$ and once in $e^x$. This will become a branch in the
DAG.

### 6.3 Local derivatives (one per edge)

For each edge $u \to v$ of the DAG, compute $\partial v/\partial u$
alone, treating the other parents of $v$ as constants:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial a}{\partial x} &= 2x, &
\frac{\partial b}{\partial a} &= \cos(a), \\[2pt]
\frac{\partial c}{\partial x} &= e^{x}, &
\frac{\partial f}{\partial b} &= c, \\[2pt]
\frac{\partial f}{\partial c} &= b. & &
\end{aligned}
$$

Five local derivatives, one per edge. Each is trivial in isolation —
that's why the method scales.

### 6.4 The paths from $x$ to $f$

Inspecting the DAG, there are exactly two directed paths:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{Path 1:}\quad & x \to a \to b \to f, \\
\textbf{Path 2:}\quad & x \to c \to f.
\end{aligned}
$$

### 6.5 Contribution of each path

The rule: multiply the local derivatives along the edges of the path.

Path 1:

$$
\Big(\frac{\partial a}{\partial x}\Big)
\Big(\frac{\partial b}{\partial a}\Big)
\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial b}\Big)
= (2x)\,\big(\cos(a)\big)\,(c)
= 2x\,\cos(x^2)\,e^{x}.
$$

Path 2:

$$
\Big(\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}\Big)
\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial c}\Big)
= (e^{x})\,(b)
= e^{x}\,\sin(x^2).
$$

### 6.6 Final sum

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
= 2x\,\cos(x^2)\,e^{x} \;+\; \sin(x^2)\,e^{x}
= e^{x}\big[\,2x\cos(x^2) + \sin(x^2)\,\big].\;}
$$

### 6.7 Sanity check via direct scalar chain rule

To validate, differentiate by the product rule and the classical chain
rule. With $f(x) = \sin(x^2)\cdot e^{x}$ and $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{df}{dx}
&= \frac{d}{dx}[\sin(x^2)] \cdot e^{x}
  \;+\; \sin(x^2) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}[e^{x}] \\
&= 2x\cos(x^2) \cdot e^{x} \;+\; \sin(x^2)\cdot e^{x} \\
&= e^{x}\big[\,2x\cos(x^2) + \sin(x^2)\,\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

Identical. The sum-over-paths is not a different technique — it is the
*same* chain rule, reorganised to make the combinatorial structure
explicit when a variable shows up in multiple places.

## 7. Why this is already (almost) backprop

Look again at §6.5. To compute Path 1's contribution, we walked the
edges *in the direction $x \to f$* (forward mode). But notice that we
could equally have walked *in the direction $f \to x$* (reverse mode)
and obtained the same product — multiplication is commutative. What
changes is the *order in which partial products are accumulated*.

In reverse mode, we start with $\partial f/\partial f = 1$ at the
terminal node and walk backwards, multiplying by each edge's local
partial and *accumulating the sum* at nodes where paths meet. The
result at $x$ is exactly $\partial f/\partial x$.

The advantage of reverse mode when there are many inputs and one output
(the case for every ML loss function): a single backward pass computes
$\partial L/\partial \theta_j$ for *every* parameter $\theta_j$
simultaneously. Cost is proportional to the number of edges, not to the
number of parameters. This is the asymptotic win that makes training
networks with billions of parameters tractable.

We'll formalise the algorithm in Chapter 2. For now it's enough to know
that *backprop is sum-over-paths, computed from output to input*.
