## 1. Why this session exists

Most of what we've derived so far is mathematically clean and numerically *dangerous*. The cross-entropy of a softmax (0.7), the KL divergence (0.6), the gradient of the log-likelihood (0.7) — every one of them is the composition

$$
\log \,\Bigl(\sum_i \exp(\text{something})\Bigr),
$$

which overflows the moment any single logit gets large. We need a small trick (log-sum-exp) and a small amount of floating-point fluency (fp32 vs fp16 vs bf16) to make this work at the scale of a real network.

This is the last session of Chapter 0. After it, every loss we write will use the stable form, and we'll know why training in bf16 became standard.<FN ns={[1, 2]} />

---

## 2. The two failure modes: overflow and underflow

A floating-point number stores a sign, an exponent, and a mantissa. Two characteristic numbers matter:

- **Max finite value** — anything larger gets clamped to $\pm \infty$.
- **Min positive normal value** — anything smaller positive underflows (to a subnormal, eventually to $0$).

For our three formats:

- **fp32 (single precision)**: max $\approx 3.4 \times 10^{38}$, min positive normal $\approx 1.2 \times 10^{-38}$, relative precision $\approx 6 \times 10^{-8}$.
- **fp16 (IEEE half precision)**: max $\approx 65504$, min positive normal $\approx 6.1 \times 10^{-5}$, relative precision $\approx 10^{-3}$.
- **bf16 (brain float)**: max $\approx 3.4 \times 10^{38}$, min positive normal $\approx 1.2 \times 10^{-38}$, relative precision $\approx 8 \times 10^{-3}$.

So in fp32, $\exp(x)$ overflows for $x > \ln(3.4 \times 10^{38}) \approx 88.7$. In fp16 it overflows already for $x > \ln(65504) \approx 11.09$. Underflow of $\exp(-x)$ happens at $x > 88.7$ in fp32 and $x > 9.7$ in fp16.

Both directions bite during training. A logit of value $20$ inside a classifier — completely ordinary, just a confident prediction — overflows fp16 if you naively exponentiate it.

## 3. The log-sum-exp identity

We want to compute

$$
L(x) = \log \sum_{i=1}^N e^{x_i}, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}^N,
$$

robustly. The naive recipe (exponentiate, sum, log) is the dangerous one. The fix is one line of algebra.

Let $m = \max_i x_i$. Then

$$
\sum_i e^{x_i}
= \sum_i e^{m + (x_i - m)}
= e^m \sum_i e^{x_i - m}.
$$

Taking the log,

$$
\boxed{\
\log \sum_i e^{x_i} \;=\; \max_i x_i \;+\; \log \sum_i e^{x_i - \max_i x_i}.
\ }
$$

Why this is stable:

- Every shifted exponent $x_i - m$ is $\le 0$, so $e^{x_i - m} \in (0, 1]$ — no overflow.
- At least one term has $x_i - m = 0$, contributing $e^0 = 1$ to the sum, so the argument of the outer log is $\ge 1$ — no $\log 0$.
- The only "large" number, the global maximum $m$, is added back outside the log, where it stays in the safe linear regime.

Algebraically this is the same expression we started with. Numerically it is night and day. *Always* compute log-sum-exp this way.

## 4. Stable softmax and the fused softmax + cross-entropy

**Softmax.** Defined entry-wise as

$$
\operatorname{softmax}(x)_i = \frac{e^{x_i}}{\sum_j e^{x_j}}.
$$

A small but important invariance:

**Lemma (shift invariance).** For any scalar $c$, $\operatorname{softmax}(x + c \mathbf{1}) = \operatorname{softmax}(x)$.

*Proof.* $\dfrac{e^{x_i + c}}{\sum_j e^{x_j + c}} = \dfrac{e^c\, e^{x_i}}{e^c \sum_j e^{x_j}} = \dfrac{e^{x_i}}{\sum_j e^{x_j}}.$ $\blacksquare$

This lets us pick $c = -m$ with $m = \max_i x_i$, giving the **numerically stable softmax**:

$$
\operatorname{softmax}(x)_i = \frac{e^{x_i - m}}{\sum_j e^{x_j - m}}.
$$

Now every exponent in numerator and denominator is in $(0, 1]$, and the denominator is $\ge 1$.

**Cross-entropy on logits.** For target class $y$ and logits $x \in \mathbb{R}^N$, the per-example cross-entropy loss is

$$
\ell(x, y) = -\log \operatorname{softmax}(x)_y = -x_y + \log \sum_j e^{x_j}.
$$

Substituting log-sum-exp,

$$
\boxed{\
\ell(x, y) \;=\; -x_y \;+\; m \;+\; \log \sum_j e^{x_j - m},
\quad m = \max_j x_j.
\ }
$$

This is what `F.cross_entropy(logits, target)` in PyTorch computes — as a **single fused op** that takes logits, not probabilities, and never forms the softmax explicitly. Doing so by hand (probabilities first, then `.log()`) is the most common cause of NaNs in custom losses.

<Aside type="warning">
Two patterns to avoid:

1. `loss = -log(softmax(x))[y]` — applies log *after* forming probabilities. Any logit large enough to dominate sums of others numerically gives a probability that rounds to $1.0$, and other classes round to $0.0$, then `log(0) = -inf`.
2. `loss = -log(P)` where `P` was computed with a separate `softmax` call.

Always call the cross-entropy directly on the logits.
</Aside>

**Gradient sanity check.** From 0.7 we already know

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial x_i} = \operatorname{softmax}(x)_i - \mathbb{1}[i = y].
$$

This expression is itself stable: it's a difference of two values in $[0, 1]$. The implementation needs the *stable* softmax above.

## 5. Floating-point formats compared

Three formats, all 32 or 16 bits, all IEEE-flavoured but distributed differently.

**fp32.** $1$ sign + $8$ exponent + $23$ mantissa bits. The reliable baseline. Big enough dynamic range that overflow rarely happens at training time; precise enough that accumulating gradients over millions of steps doesn't drift.

**fp16.** $1 + 5 + 10$. Half the bits, but only $5$ exponent bits — so the *range* shrinks dramatically: positive normals lie roughly between $6 \times 10^{-5}$ and $6.5 \times 10^4$. The mantissa is generous ($10$ bits, $\sim 10^{-3}$ relative precision) but the range is the limiting factor. Used heavily for inference; needs **loss scaling** for training.

**bf16 (brain float).** $1 + 8 + 7$. Same exponent layout as fp32, so the same dynamic range. The mantissa is only $7$ bits, giving $\sim 8 \times 10^{-3}$ relative precision — noisier than fp16. But noise the optimiser can average over; underflow it cannot.

A cartoon comparison:

- **fp32**: precise, wide range.
- **fp16**: precise, narrow range. Underflows aggressively on small gradients.
- **bf16**: noisy, wide range. Almost a drop-in replacement for fp32 in deep nets.

For training large language models, bf16 is the modern default — TPUs from the start, NVIDIA Ampere (A100) and later, AMD MI200+, etc. The reason is the dynamic range: gradients during pre-training of a Transformer regularly span $10^{-10}$ to $10^{1}$, which fp16 cannot hold.

## 6. Mixed-precision training (a forward look)

Modern training rarely uses one precision throughout. The standard recipe ("mixed precision"):

1. Keep a **master copy** of the weights in fp32.
2. Cast a working copy to **bf16** (or fp16) for the forward and backward passes.
3. Accumulate the loss in **fp32**.
4. Cast gradients back to fp32 for the optimiser step.

Memory and bandwidth halve for activations and weights during the forward/backward pass; the precision-sensitive optimiser state stays in fp32.

For fp16, an extra trick is needed: **loss scaling**. Multiply the scalar loss by some $S > 1$ before calling `.backward()`. Every gradient is then $S$ times larger in fp16, lifted out of the underflow region. After backward, divide gradients by $S$ before stepping the optimiser. PyTorch's `torch.cuda.amp.GradScaler` automates this with adaptive scale selection.

bf16 usually does not need loss scaling — its dynamic range already covers what fp32 sees.

<Aside type="history">
The bfloat16 format was designed at Google for the first generation of TPUs (around 2017). The motivating observation: in deep nets, the *exponent* width matters more than the *mantissa* width because gradients span many orders of magnitude during training. Throwing away mantissa bits to keep the fp32 exponent layout turned out to be a near-free swap — the model just sees a little more quantisation noise, which the optimiser absorbs.
</Aside>

## 7. Takeaways

1. **Never** compute $\log \sum_i e^{x_i}$ naively. Subtract the max first.
2. **Never** compute cross-entropy as $-\log \operatorname{softmax}(x)_y$ in two steps; use the fused form on logits.
3. **fp16** for inference and for layers known to live in a small dynamic range. **bf16** for training large models. **fp32** for the optimiser state and the master weights.
4. Mixed-precision training: fp32 master copy, bf16 (or fp16-with-loss-scaling) compute. Roughly $2\times$ throughput on modern GPUs at essentially the same final accuracy.

With this in hand, Chapter 0 is done. Chapter 1 starts with the perceptron, the linear classifier, and the first end-to-end optimisation problem we'll write down — and we'll use the stable softmax + cross-entropy from §4 the moment we generalise to multi-class.
