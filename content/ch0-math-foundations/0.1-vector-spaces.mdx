## 1. Where the data lives

In ML, the underlying object of any data point is a vector in a
finite-dimensional real vector space $V = \mathbb{R}^d$. A 28×28
grayscale image becomes a vector with $d = 784$ components; a sequence
of $T$ tokens in a language model, once embedded, becomes a matrix in
$\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_\text{model}}$, but each row is a vector in
$\mathbb{R}^{d_\text{model}}$.

The choice of $\mathbb{R}^d$ is not innocent — it commits us to three
things at once: additive structure ($x + y \in V$), scalar multiplication
($\lambda x \in V$), and the existence of a canonical inner product, which
we'll now derive.

## 2. Inner product via basis expansions

Fix a basis $\{e_1, \dots, e_d\}$ of $V$. Every vector has a unique
expansion

$$
x = \sum_{i=1}^{d} x_i \, e_i, \qquad x_i \in \mathbb{R}.
$$

Suppose $V$ is equipped with a symmetric positive-definite bilinear form
$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : V \times V \to \mathbb{R}$. By
bilinearity, for $y = \sum_j y_j e_j$,

$$
\langle x, y \rangle
= \Big\langle \sum_i x_i e_i,\, \sum_j y_j e_j \Big\rangle
= \sum_{i,j} x_i\, y_j\, \langle e_i, e_j \rangle.
$$

When the basis is orthonormal, $\langle e_i, e_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$,
and the double sum collapses:

$$
\boxed{\;\langle x, y \rangle = \sum_i x_i y_i = x^\top y.\;}
$$

This is the form that shows up in every ML derivation: inner product is
row-times-column multiplication.

## 3. Induced norm and angle

The norm induced by the inner product is

$$
\|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x, x \rangle} = \Big( \sum_i x_i^2 \Big)^{1/2}.
$$

And the angle $\theta$ between $x$ and $y$ is defined implicitly by

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\langle x, y \rangle}{\|x\|\, \|y\|}.
$$

The *cosine similarity* used in semantic search and embedding
normalisation is exactly this ratio. When vectors are pre-normalised to
unit norm, $\cos\theta = x^\top y$ — that's it.

## 4. A linear layer is an affine map

A fully connected layer with weights $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times d}$ and
bias $b \in \mathbb{R}^m$ implements the map

$$
f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^m, \qquad f(x) = Wx + b.
$$

This is the classical definition of an affine transformation: linear
($x \mapsto Wx$) plus translation ($+\,b$). The *bias trick* — appending
a $1$ to the end of $x$ and stacking $b$ as the last column of $W$ —
turns an affine map into a purely linear one in the augmented space
$\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$. This simplifies proofs, but in code we keep $b$
separate for efficiency (it broadcasts).

## 5. Where this is going

All the algebra to come — attention, softmax, layer normalisation — rests
on these three facts: there is a basis, there is an inner product, and
layers are affine. Internalise them; there's no shortcut.
