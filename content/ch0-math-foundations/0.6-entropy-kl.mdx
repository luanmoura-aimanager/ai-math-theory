## 1. Shannon information of a single outcome

If an event with probability $p$ occurs, the *information* it carries is

$$
I(p) = -\log p.
$$

The minus sign makes $I$ non-negative (since $p \le 1$, $\log p \le 0$).
Three sanity checks:

- $I(1) = 0$: a certain outcome carries zero information.
- $I(p) \to \infty$ as $p \to 0$: an impossible-looking outcome that
  nonetheless occurs is maximally informative.
- $I(p_1 p_2) = I(p_1) + I(p_2)$: information from two independent
  events adds. This additivity is what fixes the form of $I$ uniquely
  up to a log base.

Base of the log sets the units: base 2 gives *bits*, base $e$ gives
*nats*. In ML we use natural log throughout (gradient-friendly); the
formulas are identical up to a constant factor.<FN n={1} />

## 2. Entropy

Entropy is the expected information of a draw from $p$:

$$
\boxed{\;H[p] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{X \sim p}[-\log p(X)]
\;=\; -\sum_x p(x)\,\log p(x).\;}
$$

For continuous distributions, replace the sum with an integral; the
quantity is then called *differential entropy* and has subtler
properties (it can be negative, isn't invariant under change of
variables) — but the formula is the same shape.

Properties:

- $H[p] \ge 0$ for discrete $p$ (each term $-p \log p$ is non-negative
  on $[0, 1]$, with the convention $0 \log 0 = 0$).
- $H[p]$ is maximised by the uniform distribution. Intuition: the
  uniform distribution is "maximally unpredictable", carrying the most
  information per draw.
- $H$ is concave in $p$ — a fact we won't need today but that explains
  why "averaging two distributions doesn't reduce uncertainty".

## 3. Cross-entropy

If $p$ is the true distribution and $q$ is the one you code
with (a model), the *cross-entropy* is

$$
H[p, q] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{X \sim p}[-\log q(X)]
\;=\; -\sum_x p(x)\,\log q(x).
$$

Operationally: $H[p, q]$ is the expected number of bits (or nats) you
spend encoding draws from $p$ using an optimal code for $q$. When
$q = p$, you've made the right choice and $H[p, q] = H[p]$. When $q
\neq p$, you waste bits — and the wasted bits are what KL
divergence measures.

## 4. KL divergence

The Kullback–Leibler divergence of $p$ from $q$ is

$$
\boxed{\;\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) \;=\; \sum_x p(x)\,\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
\;=\; \mathbb{E}_{X \sim p}\!\left[\log\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\right].\;}
$$

It satisfies the identity

$$
\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = H[p, q] - H[p],
$$

which makes the "wasted bits" reading precise. Worked out:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H[p, q] - H[p]
&= -\sum_x p(x)\log q(x) \;-\; \big(-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)\big) \\
&= \sum_x p(x)\big[\log p(x) - \log q(x)\big] \\
&= \sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \\
&= \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q).
\end{aligned}
$$

KL is **not symmetric**: $\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) \neq \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$
in general. It is *not* a distance — it's a divergence. The first
argument is "the truth"; the second is "your model".

## 5. KL is non-negative (Jensen's inequality)

We want to show $\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) \ge 0$, with equality iff $p = q$.

The key tool is **Jensen's inequality** for a concave function $\varphi$
and a random variable $Z$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Z)] \le \varphi(\mathbb{E}[Z]).
$$

The function $\log$ is concave on $(0, \infty)$. So:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)
&= -\sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
\;=\; \sum_x p(x)\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}\!\left[\log\frac{q(X)}{p(X)}\right] \\
&\le \log\,\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p}\!\left[\frac{q(X)}{p(X)}\right] \quad \text{(Jensen, $\log$ concave)} \\
&= \log\sum_x p(x)\,\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} \\
&= \log\sum_x q(x) \\
&= \log 1 = 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore $\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) \ge 0$. Equality holds iff
$q(x)/p(x)$ is constant under $p$ — which forces $q = p$ since both are
probability distributions.

This is *the* fundamental inequality of probabilistic ML. Almost every
"loss is bounded below by zero" or "evidence lower bound" argument in
the field passes through it.

<Aside type="note">
The asymmetry of KL has a concrete consequence in practice. Minimising
$\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)$ over $q$ — *forward KL* — favours $q$ that
covers all the modes of $p$ (mass where $p$ has mass). Minimising
$\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$ — *reverse KL* — favours $q$ that concentrates
on one mode of $p$ (avoid mass where $p$ has none). Variational
inference uses reverse KL; that's why fitted approximate posteriors
often look "mode-seeking". Worth remembering when you next see a
fitted Gaussian missing half a bimodal target.
</Aside>

## 6. Information-theoretic identities you will use

A handful of identities that follow by direct expansion and that we'll
invoke without re-deriving from here on:

- **Mutual information.** $I(X; Y) = \mathrm{KL}(p_{XY}\,\|\,p_X p_Y) =
  H[X] - H[X \mid Y]$. Measures how much knowing $Y$ reduces uncertainty
  about $X$. Non-negative; zero iff $X \perp Y$.
- **Conditional entropy.** $H[X \mid Y] = -\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)}[\log
  p(X \mid Y)]$. Average uncertainty about $X$ remaining after $Y$ is
  observed.
- **Chain rule of entropy.** $H[X, Y] = H[X] + H[Y \mid X] = H[Y] +
  H[X \mid Y]$. Mirror of the product rule of probability.

## 7. Why this matters for what's next

Cross-entropy as a training loss (§0.7) is exactly $H[p_{\text{data}},
q_{\theta}]$, and its gradient with respect to $\theta$ doesn't see the
data-only term $H[p_{\text{data}}]$ — making it operationally
identical to negative log-likelihood. KL appears explicitly in
variational autoencoders, in RLHF's KL penalty against a reference
policy, and as the constraint in trust-region methods (TRPO, PPO).
Mutual information is the lens used to discuss compression, the
information-bottleneck theory of neural networks, and self-supervised
contrastive losses. One vocabulary, used everywhere.
