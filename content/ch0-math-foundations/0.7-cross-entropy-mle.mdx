## 1. The starting point: i.i.d. data and a parametric model

We have a training set $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{m}$ assumed
to be i.i.d. draws from some unknown joint $p_{\text{data}}(x, y)$. We
specify a parametric *conditional* model $q_\theta(y \mid x)$ and want to
fit $\theta$ to the data.

(I write $q$ instead of $p$ for the model to keep the truth/model
distinction explicit — same convention as §0.6.)<FN n={1} />

## 2. The likelihood

Under the i.i.d. assumption, the joint probability the model assigns to
the labels given the inputs factorises:

$$
q_\theta(y_1, \dots, y_m \mid x_1, \dots, x_m)
\;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{m} q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i).
$$

This is the **conditional likelihood** of the labels under the model. As
a function of $\theta$ with the data fixed, it is denoted
$\mathcal{L}(\theta)$.

## 3. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)

Maximum likelihood picks $\theta$ that maximises $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$.
Products are awful to differentiate, so take the log (which is monotone
and so doesn't change the argmax):

$$
\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}
\;=\; \arg\max_\theta\; \log\prod_{i=1}^{m} q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i)
\;=\; \arg\max_\theta\; \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i).
$$

Flip the sign to turn maximisation into minimisation (the convention in
optimisation):

$$
\boxed{\;\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}
= \arg\min_\theta\; -\sum_{i=1}^{m} \log q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i).\;}
$$

This is the **negative log-likelihood** (NLL). Divide by $m$ to get the
per-sample version (gradients are then unitless in batch size):

$$
\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}
= \arg\min_\theta\; -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \log q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i).
$$

## 4. The empirical distribution

Define the empirical distribution as the discrete measure that puts mass
$1/m$ on each training point:

$$
\hat p(x, y) \;=\; \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \mathbb{1}\{x = x_i,\, y = y_i\}.
$$

Expectation under $\hat p$ is "average over the training set",
which we already noted in §0.4:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{(X, Y)\sim \hat p}[f(X, Y)] \;=\; \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} f(x_i, y_i).
$$

## 5. NLL is cross-entropy

Apply this to $f(x, y) = -\log q_\theta(y \mid x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \log q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i)
&= \mathbb{E}_{(X, Y)\sim \hat p}\!\left[-\log q_\theta(Y \mid X)\right] \\
&= -\sum_{(x, y)} \hat p(x, y)\, \log q_\theta(y \mid x).
\end{aligned}
$$

That last line is the cross-entropy between $\hat p$ and the
conditional model $q_\theta$, written with the joint factored as
$\hat p(x, y) = \hat p(x)\,\hat p(y \mid x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\sum_{x, y} \hat p(x, y) \log q_\theta(y \mid x)
&= -\sum_x \hat p(x) \sum_y \hat p(y \mid x) \log q_\theta(y \mid x) \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{X \sim \hat p}\!\left[ H[\hat p(\cdot \mid X),\,
   q_\theta(\cdot \mid X)] \right],
\end{aligned}
$$

i.e. the input-averaged cross-entropy between the empirical conditional
$\hat p(y \mid x)$ and the model conditional $q_\theta(y \mid x)$.

Putting it together:

$$
\boxed{\;\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}
\;=\; \arg\min_\theta\;
\mathbb{E}_{X \sim \hat p}\!\left[ H[\hat p(\cdot \mid X),\,
q_\theta(\cdot \mid X)] \right].\;}
$$

**MLE on a discriminative model is exactly cross-entropy minimisation
on the empirical distribution.** The two names refer to the same
gradient.

## 6. Why the model's "missing" entropy term doesn't matter for gradients

Recall from §0.6 that $H[\hat p, q_\theta] = H[\hat p] +
\mathrm{KL}(\hat p\,\|\,q_\theta)$. So minimising cross-entropy over
$\theta$ is identical to minimising $\mathrm{KL}(\hat p\,\|\,q_\theta)$,
since $H[\hat p]$ doesn't depend on $\theta$ and drops out of the
gradient. Three names for the same optimisation:

- MLE (statistics).
- Cross-entropy minimisation (information theory).
- Forward KL minimisation against the empirical distribution (Bayesian
  ML).

## 7. The two cases you'll see daily

**Binary classification** with $y \in \{0, 1\}$ and
$q_\theta(y = 1 \mid x) = \hat y$ for some predicted probability:

$$
-\log q_\theta(y \mid x) =
\begin{cases}
-\log \hat y & \text{if } y = 1, \\
-\log (1 - \hat y) & \text{if } y = 0.
\end{cases}
$$

These two cases unify into one expression — the **binary cross-entropy**
loss:

$$
\boxed{\;\ell(\hat y, y) = -\,y \log \hat y \;-\; (1 - y)\log(1 - \hat y).\;}
$$

**Multi-class classification** with $y \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ and
$q_\theta(y = k \mid x) = \hat y_k$ for predicted class probabilities
summing to 1 (e.g. coming from a softmax — see Ch. 1):

$$
\boxed{\;\ell(\hat y, y) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbb{1}\{y = k\}\, \log \hat y_k
\;=\; -\log \hat y_y.\;}
$$

The last form ($-\log \hat y_y$) is what you read in code: take the
predicted probability of the true class, take its log, negate. That's
PyTorch's `F.cross_entropy` (modulo the fact that PyTorch's
`cross_entropy` expects pre-softmax logits and applies the softmax
internally for numerical stability — more on that in §0.10).

## 8. What we've earned

The same loss object reads three ways depending on the lens. Picking
the right lens unstrucks problems:

- **Statistical lens (MLE):** what data-generating assumption does my
  loss implicitly make? E.g. mean-squared error ↔ Gaussian noise (Ch. 1).
- **Information-theoretic lens (cross-entropy / KL):** how far is my
  model from the empirical distribution? Variational and distillation
  methods sit here.
- **Operational lens (NLL):** what does the gradient look like and how do
  I implement it? See §0.10 for the stability tricks that make this
  practical.

For every loss in the rest of the course, ask which of the three views
makes the calculation easy. Use that one.

<SelfTest>
A neural network outputs a single logit $z$ (a real number, no
activation). The label is binary, $y \in \{0, 1\}$.

1. Apply the sigmoid: $\hat y = \sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$. Write the
   binary cross-entropy $\ell(z, y)$ purely in terms of $z$ and $y$,
   eliminating $\hat y$.
2. Compute $\partial \ell / \partial z$. You should get a famously
   clean expression involving $\sigma(z) - y$. This is the identity
   that makes logistic regression numerically and didactically pleasant.
3. Sanity check: at $y = 1$, the gradient is $-(1 - \sigma(z))$; at
   $y = 0$, it is $\sigma(z)$. Why does this match the intuition that
   "the loss pushes the logit up when $y = 1$ and down when $y = 0$,
   proportionally to how wrong we are"?
</SelfTest>
