## 1. Convention: numerator vs. denominator

Before any derivative, fix the convention. For $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to
\mathbb{R}^m$ given by $y = f(x)$, *numerator layout* declares

$$
\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}, \qquad
\Big(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Big)_{ij} = \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial x_j}.
$$

*Denominator layout* flips it: $\partial y / \partial x \in
\mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$. I pick numerator because it makes the chain
rule stack matrices in the natural multiplication order. **Every
derivation in this course uses numerator layout.** Mix them and you
suffer.<FN n={1} />

## 2. Derivative of $Wx + b$ with respect to $x$

Let $f(x) = Wx + b$ with $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $b \in
\mathbb{R}^m$. Component-wise,

$$
y_i = \sum_{k=1}^{n} W_{ik}\, x_k + b_i.
$$

Differentiating $y_i$ with respect to $x_j$, every term with $k \neq j$
vanishes:

$$
\frac{\partial y_i}{\partial x_j}
= \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \Big( \sum_{k} W_{ik}\, x_k + b_i \Big)
= \sum_{k} W_{ik}\, \delta_{kj}
= W_{ij}.
$$

Stacking over every $(i, j)$ pair,

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\partial (Wx + b)}{\partial x} = W \;\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}.\;}
$$

The intuition is direct: differentiating an affine map with respect to
its input recovers its linear part.

## 3. Derivative with respect to $W$

Here $W$ is a matrix, so $\partial y / \partial W$ is in principle a
*rank-3 tensor*: it has indices $(i, j, k)$ with $i$ ranging over $y$ and
$(j, k)$ ranging over $W$. The cleanest route is to differentiate index by
index; the storage shape will fall out at the end.

$$
\frac{\partial y_i}{\partial W_{jk}}
= \frac{\partial}{\partial W_{jk}} \Big( \sum_{l} W_{il}\, x_l + b_i \Big)
= \sum_{l} \delta_{ij}\, \delta_{lk}\, x_l
= \delta_{ij}\, x_k.
$$

Note the $\delta_{ij}$: the derivative of the $i$-th output with respect
to the element $W_{jk}$ is non-zero only when $j = i$. In other words,
the weight $W_{jk}$ affects only the output $y_j$.

## 4. Why this shows up as an outer product in backprop

At training time, what we need is not the full tensor but the gradient
of a scalar loss $L$ with respect to $W$. By the chain rule:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{jk}}
= \sum_i \frac{\partial L}{\partial y_i}\, \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial W_{jk}}
= \sum_i \frac{\partial L}{\partial y_i}\, \delta_{ij}\, x_k
= \frac{\partial L}{\partial y_j}\, x_k.
$$

Define $\delta := \partial L / \partial y \in \mathbb{R}^{m}$. In matrix
form, $\partial L / \partial W$ is the $m \times n$ matrix whose $(j,k)$
entry is $\delta_j\, x_k$ — the outer product:

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = \delta\, x^\top \;\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}.\;}
$$

And for the bias, $\partial L/\partial b = \delta$ (each $b_i$ contributes
$1$ to $y_i$). This completes the *local update rule* of a linear layer;
chained across all layers, it is PyTorch's `backward()`.

## 5. Quick check (do it with a pen)

For $W = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $x = (5,
6)^\top$, compute $y = Wx$, then $\partial y / \partial x$, and finally
— assuming $L = \tfrac{1}{2}\|y\|^2$ — obtain $\delta$ and the outer
product $\delta\, x^\top$. If $W^\top y\, x^\top$ shows up in the slot where $\delta\, x^\top$
should be, the numerator convention from §1 was crossed.
