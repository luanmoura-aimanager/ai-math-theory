## 1. Why this session exists

So far Chapter 9 has been about *training-time alignment* — SFT, RLHF, DPO, GRPO, CAI. These methods produce an aligned policy by *modifying the weights* of a base model. Once trained, the model is "the policy" and you deploy it.

The complementary approach is **inference-time alignment**: keep the base model (or an SFT model) and align *at deployment time* by sampling multiple completions and selecting the best one according to some scoring function. The model weights don't move; the alignment happens in the sampling-and-selection loop.

Three canonical techniques:

- **Best-of-N (BoN).** Sample $N$ completions, score each with a reward model or judge, pick the highest-scoring.
- **Rejection sampling.** Sample completions; discard any that fail a quality threshold; return the first acceptable one.
- **LLM-as-judge selection.** Use a stronger LLM to compare candidates and pick the best.

These methods are *complementary* to training-time alignment, not substitutes. Production systems use both: a DPO-trained policy with inference-time Best-of-N selection for high-quality outputs on hard prompts.

This session closes Chapter 9: defines each technique, characterises the cost-quality trade-offs, and identifies where each is appropriate. With it, you have the full toolkit for taking a pretrained base LM to an aligned, deployable product.

## 2. Best-of-N

The simplest and most-used inference-time technique. Algorithm:

```
Given prompt x and reward function r:
    Sample N completions y_1, ..., y_N ~ π(·|x)
    Compute r_i = r(x, y_i) for each
    Return y* = y_{argmax_i r_i}
```

Three knobs: $N$ (number of samples), $\pi$ (the policy doing the sampling — usually SFT or RLHF-trained), $r$ (the scoring function — reward model, judge LLM, or task-specific verifier).

### 2.1 What it buys

BoN trades inference compute for quality. Sampling $N$ times costs $N \times$ the per-completion compute; the resulting quality improves with $N$.

Empirically (across many tasks, many models), BoN scales as

$$
\text{quality}(\pi, N) \approx \text{quality}(\pi, 1) + \alpha \log N,
$$

with $\alpha$ depending on the policy's existing quality and the reward function. Doubling $N$ adds a *constant* improvement, so quality grows logarithmically in compute. Realistic ranges: $N \in [4, 64]$ for production deployments; $N \in [100, 1000]$ for benchmark optimisation; $N \in [10^4, 10^5]$ for research demonstrations.

### 2.2 BoN as implicit optimisation

BoN is *implicitly optimising* the reward $r$ via sampling. Each sample is a step on the policy's distribution; selecting the best is like taking a single optimisation step in the direction of $r$.

Gao et al. (2022, [§5 of 9.14](/session/ch9-alignment/9.14-reward-hacking)) showed that BoN exhibits the *same* reward hacking as RL training does — past a critical $N$, the held-out human preference rate declines even as the reward model's score keeps climbing. The $N$ at which this happens is reward-model-dependent; for typical production setups, BoN starts hacking around $N \in [64, 256]$.

Practical implication: **don't crank $N$ unbounded**. Past the reward-model-specific critical $N$, you're spending more inference compute to get *worse* outputs.

### 2.3 BoN with verifiers vs reward models

A clean alternative when the task allows it: replace the reward model with a **verifier** — a function that can determine *correctness* of an answer, not just preference.

- Math problems → numerical answer checker.
- Code → unit test runner.
- Reasoning → multi-step proof checker.

Verifiers don't reward-hack (they evaluate exact correctness, not learned preference), and BoN with a verifier is the dominant test-time strategy for reasoning models. DeepSeek-R1 ([2025, arXiv:2501.12948](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948)) and OpenAI o1 both heavily rely on BoN-with-verifier for math and code reasoning evaluation.

The downside: verifiers exist only for tasks with checkable answers. For open-ended chat or writing, you're stuck with reward-model-based BoN and its over-optimisation risk.

## 3. Rejection sampling

A variant of BoN with a *threshold* instead of an argmax:

```
Given prompt x, reward r, threshold τ, max attempts N:
    Sample y_1, ..., y_n ~ π(·|x) one at a time
    Return the first y_i with r(x, y_i) > τ
    If no y_i satisfies the threshold in N attempts, return best-so-far
```

The difference from BoN: rejection sampling *stops early* if a candidate exceeds the threshold $\tau$, saving compute on easy prompts.

For hard prompts (where no candidate exceeds $\tau$), it falls back to BoN with $N$ samples.

### 3.1 When rejection sampling beats BoN

- **Easy prompts.** If $\pi$ produces high-quality completions most of the time, the first sample often passes the threshold. Average compute: ~$1$–$2$ samples per prompt rather than always $N$.
- **Variable-difficulty workloads.** Production traffic has a mix of easy and hard prompts. Rejection sampling adapts the compute spend to the difficulty.

### 3.2 When BoN beats rejection sampling

- **Hard prompts.** If most candidates fail $\tau$, rejection sampling does $N$ work anyway, but uses an arbitrary stopping rule (first acceptable rather than best). BoN's argmax is then strictly better.
- **Quality maximisation, not threshold.** If you want the *best* candidate, not the *first acceptable*, BoN is the right algorithm.

In practice: rejection sampling is the right default for **production traffic** (mix of difficulties, latency-sensitive); BoN is the right default for **batch quality work** (offline evaluation, content generation where wall-clock isn't the bottleneck).

### 3.3 Rejection sampling for SFT data generation

A related technique: use rejection sampling to *generate fine-tuning data*. Sample many candidates from a model; keep those that score above a threshold; SFT a new model on the kept candidates.

This is **rejection-sampling fine-tuning (RSFT)** — first appeared in LLaMA-3 ([9.11 §7.1](/session/ch9-alignment/9.11-iterative-dpo)) and is now a standard step in open-source instruction tuning. The trick: a model can be improved by SFTing on its *own* best outputs, provided the scoring function is informative.

This blurs the boundary between inference-time alignment and training-time alignment: the inference-time selection step *generates* training-time data, which trains a better model, which makes inference-time selection better, etc.

## 4. LLM-as-judge selection

A specialisation of BoN where the scoring function is **a stronger LLM** rather than a reward model:

```
Sample N candidates y_1, ..., y_N from π
For each pair (y_i, y_j):
    Ask judge LLM: "Which is better, y_i or y_j?"
Tournament or pairwise comparison → pick the winner.
```

The judge can be GPT-4, Claude, a held-out larger model, or even the same model with a fresh context. The advantage over RM-based BoN: no reward model needed; the judge directly applies an evaluation rubric.

### 4.1 Tournament vs pairwise

Two implementations:

- **Pairwise**: compare each candidate against every other; the one that wins the most is the answer. Cost: $O(N^2)$ judge calls.
- **Single-elimination tournament**: bracket-style comparisons. Cost: $O(N)$ judge calls.

Tournament is cheaper but loses some information (a candidate eliminated early might have been the best). Pairwise is more thorough but quadratic.

For small $N$ ($\leq 8$), pairwise is fine. For larger $N$, use tournament with a few re-runs to amortise randomness.

### 4.2 Judge quality matters

LLM judges have biases ([9.13 §2.3](/session/ch9-alignment/9.13-rlaif-constitutional)): verbosity, position, self-preference. Judge-based BoN inherits these.

The mitigation: use a *stronger* judge than the policy being judged. If the policy is a 7B model, judge with GPT-4 or Claude. If the policy is GPT-4, judge with a stronger model or use multiple judges and average.

For frontier models with no clearly-stronger judge available, judge-based BoN faces an upper limit. Constitutional principles ([9.13](/session/ch9-alignment/9.13-rlaif-constitutional)) and human evaluation samples are the typical fallbacks.

### 4.3 Cost

A judge LLM call costs ~$0.005 per pairwise comparison on GPT-4. For $N = 8$ with a tournament: 7 comparisons, $0.035. For $N = 32$ pairwise: 496 comparisons, $2.50. Production deployments avoid the quadratic regime.

## 5. Training-time vs inference-time alignment: comparison

| | Training-time (RLHF, DPO) | Inference-time (BoN, RS, judge) |
|---|---|---|
| Compute amortisation | One-time (training) | Per-query (inference) |
| Quality ceiling | Limited by base + RM | Limited by base + sampler |
| Latency at deploy | Same as base | $N\times$ base (BoN) or variable (RS) |
| Per-query cost | Same as base | $N\times$ base |
| Adaptability | Fixed after training | Can change scoring function anytime |
| Catastrophic failure mode | Reward hacking baked into weights | Bounded by current sampling |

The two are *complementary*, not substitutes. The right architecture is:

1. **Train** a well-aligned base policy via SFT + RLHF or SFT + iterative DPO. Use this as the default.
2. **At inference**, apply BoN or rejection sampling for high-stakes queries (correctness-critical, multi-step reasoning, user-facing). For ordinary queries, single-sample inference is fine.

The fraction of queries that get inference-time alignment depends on the cost-quality budget. Frontier reasoning models (o1, R1) do BoN-style selection on a large fraction of queries; cheap chat models do single-sample inference for most queries with optional BoN on flagged-hard prompts.

## 6. The 2026 inference-time alignment landscape

Three trends.

### 6.1 BoN with verifiers

For tasks with checkable correctness — math, code, structured outputs — BoN with a verifier is now the *test-time-scaling* paradigm. OpenAI's o1 documentation shows "thinking tokens" combined with selection over $N$ candidates; DeepSeek-R1's paper describes the same pattern.

The "verifier" varies: for math, an exact-answer checker plus PRM ([9.12](/session/ch9-alignment/9.12-process-reward-models)) for step-level scoring. For code, unit tests. For reasoning, ground-truth comparison or a held-out verifier model.

This is the dominant test-time strategy for hard-problem benchmarks (AIME, HumanEval, GPQA) as of 2026.

### 6.2 Inference-time scaling laws

Just as training-time has Kaplan/Chinchilla scaling laws ([7.11](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.11-scaling-laws-kaplan)–[7.12](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.12-chinchilla)), inference-time has its own emerging laws. **Snell, Lee, Xu, Choquette-Choo, 2024, *Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally***  ([arXiv:2408.03314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314)) and related work characterise the quality return per inference FLOP.

The headline: for hard problems, inference-time compute can match or exceed training-time compute in marginal capability gain. A smaller model with more inference budget often beats a larger model with less. This is the analytic basis for "reasoning-model" deployments where each query may use $\sim 10$K–$100$K extra tokens of "thinking."

### 6.3 Agentic loops as iterated inference-time alignment

A 2024–2026 trend: rather than BoN over fixed completions, treat inference as a **multi-step agent loop**:

1. Sample a candidate plan.
2. Critique it (via the model itself or a held-out judge).
3. Revise.
4. Loop.

This is "thinking + verification" iterated, often with explicit tool use (calculators, code execution, web search). The boundary between "inference-time alignment" and "agentic reasoning" blurs.

Cap 10.9 covers agentic decoding in detail; here, the connection is that *agentic reasoning* is a generalisation of BoN where the search is over partial plans rather than complete outputs.

## 7. Putting Chapter 9 together

Chapter 9 covered the alignment toolkit. To recap the full pipeline:

1. **Pretraining** (Chapters 6–7) gives a fluent next-token predictor.
2. **SFT** ([9.2](/session/ch9-alignment/9.2-sft)) teaches it to follow instructions.
3. **Reward modelling** ([9.3](/session/ch9-alignment/9.3-reward-modeling-preference-data)–[9.4](/session/ch9-alignment/9.4-bradley-terry-reward-objective)) learns a proxy for human preferences.
4. **RLHF (PPO)** ([9.5](/session/ch9-alignment/9.5-policy-gradient-reinforce)–[9.6](/session/ch9-alignment/9.6-ppo), [9.8](/session/ch9-alignment/9.8-rlhf-end-to-end), [9.9](/session/ch9-alignment/9.9-kl-penalty)) or **GRPO** ([9.7](/session/ch9-alignment/9.7-grpo)) optimises the policy against it.
5. **DPO** ([9.10](/session/ch9-alignment/9.10-dpo)) and **Iterative DPO** ([9.11](/session/ch9-alignment/9.11-iterative-dpo)) provide a simpler alternative.
6. **PRMs** ([9.12](/session/ch9-alignment/9.12-process-reward-models)) give dense per-step rewards for reasoning training.
7. **RLAIF and CAI** ([9.13](/session/ch9-alignment/9.13-rlaif-constitutional)) replace human labels with AI labels at scale.
8. **Reward hacking** ([9.14](/session/ch9-alignment/9.14-reward-hacking)) is the persistent failure mode; KL penalty, early stopping, RM ensembles mitigate it.
9. **Inference-time alignment** (this session) complements the training-time pipeline with sampling-and-selection at deployment.

A frontier deployment in 2026 uses essentially all of these. A small open-source model might use only steps 2, 5, 7, 9 — but the same conceptual toolkit applies.

The **architecture** has been stable since GPT-2 (Chapter 6); the **pretraining recipe** has been stable since Chinchilla (Chapter 7); the **alignment recipe** is where most recent progress has happened, and where most rapid evolution still occurs. As of 2026, alignment is no longer "RLHF with PPO" — it is a multi-stage pipeline with substantial AI-in-the-loop automation and inference-time complement.

<Aside type="note">
**The alignment "stack" in 2026 is roughly:** Pretrain → SFT → Iterative DPO (with constitutional AI labels) → optional inference-time BoN-with-verifier for hard queries. RLHF-via-PPO is still used by Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepMind for frontier models, but open-source has standardised on the simpler DPO-based variant. The constitutional aspect — explicit principles, AI judges — is universal.
</Aside>

## 8. What we keep going forward

Three things:

1. **Inference-time alignment is sampling + selection.** BoN, rejection sampling, judge-based comparison. Quality scales as $\log N$ until reward hacking sets in.
2. **Verifiers beat reward models when available.** For math/code/checkable tasks, BoN with verifiers is the dominant test-time strategy. For open-ended, fall back to RM or judges.
3. **Training-time and inference-time alignment compose.** Train a well-aligned base; add inference-time selection on hard queries. The right deployment uses both.
