## 1. Why this session exists

**Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, et al., 2023, *Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model*** ([arXiv:2305.18290](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)). The paper title is the entire idea: instead of training a reward model and then PPO-ing against it, *use the policy itself as the reward model*. The derivation does this in three steps:

1. Solve the KL-penalised RLHF objective in closed form. Result: the optimal policy is a Boltzmann distribution over the (unknown) reward.
2. Invert: express the reward as a function of the optimal policy. Result: $r(x, y) = \beta \log(\pi^*(y \mid x) / \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)) + \text{(constant in } y)$.
3. Substitute the inverted expression for $r$ into the Bradley-Terry preference likelihood ([9.4](/session/ch9-alignment/9.4-bradley-terry-reward-objective)). The reward cancels; the loss is now a function of the policy alone.

The result is one of the cleanest derivations in modern deep learning. It eliminates two of the three RLHF stages (reward modelling, PPO), replacing them with a single supervised loss directly on $(x, y_c, y_r)$ preference triples.

DPO has become the default open-source alignment recipe since 2024 (LLaMA-3-Instruct, Mistral-Instruct, Tülu 3, Hermes-3). Frontier closed models still use RLHF, but for almost everyone else, DPO is "RLHF without the pain."

This session derives DPO from scratch and ends with the loss function you can paste into your training loop.

## 2. Step 1: closed-form optimal policy

Start with the KL-penalised RLHF objective ([9.8](/session/ch9-alignment/9.8-rlhf-end-to-end), [9.9](/session/ch9-alignment/9.9-kl-penalty)):

$$
\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}\big[r(x, y)\big] - \beta \cdot \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi(\cdot \mid x) \,\Vert\, \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)\big)\right].
$$

(We dropped the subscript $\theta$ to emphasise we're optimising over *all* distributions, not just parameterised ones.) Drop the prompt $x$ for clarity — everything below is "for each fixed $x$".

Expand the KL:

$$
\max_\pi \sum_y \pi(y) \cdot r(y) - \beta \sum_y \pi(y) \cdot \log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y)},
$$

subject to $\pi$ being a valid distribution ($\sum_y \pi(y) = 1$, $\pi(y) \geq 0$).

### 2.1 Lagrangian

Introduce a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ for the normalisation constraint:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\pi, \lambda) = \sum_y \pi(y) \cdot r(y) - \beta \sum_y \pi(y) \cdot \log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y)} - \lambda \!\left(\sum_y \pi(y) - 1\right).
$$

Take $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \pi(y) = 0$:

$$
r(y) - \beta \cdot \left(\log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y)} + 1\right) - \lambda = 0.
$$

(The "$+1$" comes from $\partial/\partial \pi$ of $\pi \log \pi$.) Solve for $\log \pi(y)$:

$$
\log \pi(y) = \log \pi_\text{ref}(y) + \frac{r(y)}{\beta} - \frac{\lambda}{\beta} - 1.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\pi(y) = \pi_\text{ref}(y) \cdot \exp\!\left(\frac{r(y)}{\beta}\right) \cdot C,
$$

with $C = \exp\!\left(-\lambda/\beta - 1\right)$ — a normalising constant independent of $y$. Pinning $C$ from $\sum_y \pi(y) = 1$:

$$
C = \frac{1}{Z(x)}, \quad Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_\text{ref}(y) \cdot \exp\!\left(\frac{r(y)}{\beta}\right).
$$

(Restoring the $x$ dependence — $Z(x)$ is the prompt-specific partition function.) The optimal policy is

$$
\boxed{\ \pi^*(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \cdot \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x) \cdot \exp\!\left(\frac{r(x, y)}{\beta}\right). \ }
$$

### 2.2 Interpretation

This is a **Boltzmann distribution** over completions, with $r/\beta$ as the negative-energy and $\pi_\text{ref}$ as the prior. Three observations:

1. **The reference is the prior.** $\pi^*$ inherits all of $\pi_\text{ref}$'s structure (vocabulary, syntax, basic content) and only modulates it by the reward.
2. **$\beta$ controls how "sharp" the modulation is.** Large $\beta$: nearly flat exponent, $\pi^* \approx \pi_\text{ref}$. Small $\beta$: very sharp exponent, $\pi^*$ concentrates on high-reward completions.
3. **The exponential structure is fundamental.** This is the same shape as the softmax distribution in [1.7](/session/ch1-linear-models-perceptron/1.7-softmax) and the energy-based-model literature. RLHF's exponential family arises *from the KL constraint*, not from any modelling choice.

The partition function $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_\text{ref}(y) \cdot \exp(r(x, y)/\beta)$ is intractable for LLMs (the sum is over all possible completions). This is what stops us from just *computing* $\pi^*$ directly. The DPO trick will be to make $Z(x)$ cancel.

---

## 3. Step 2: invert to express $r$ in terms of $\pi^*$

Take logs of the boxed equation:

$$
\log \pi^*(y \mid x) = \log \pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x) + \frac{r(x, y)}{\beta} - \log Z(x).
$$

Solve for $r(x, y)$:

$$
\boxed{\ r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x). \ }
$$

This is the **DPO inversion**. The reward — the thing we previously had to train a separate model for — is *exactly* the log-ratio of the optimal policy to the reference, scaled by $\beta$, plus a term that depends only on $x$.

The $\beta \log Z(x)$ term is *independent of $y$*. So **for any two completions $y_1, y_2$ of the same prompt**:

$$
r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1 \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1 \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2 \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2 \mid x)}.
$$

The intractable $Z(x)$ cancels.

---

## 4. Step 3: substitute into Bradley-Terry

Recall ([9.4 §2](/session/ch9-alignment/9.4-bradley-terry-reward-objective)) the Bradley-Terry preference likelihood:

$$
p(y_c \succ y_r \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_c) - r(x, y_r)\big),
$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. The reward-model training loss was

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{RM}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_c, y_r)}\!\big[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_c) - r_\phi(x, y_r)\big)\big].
$$

Now substitute the DPO inversion for $r$, with $\pi^*$ replaced by the policy $\pi_\theta$ being trained (since DPO claims $\pi_\theta$ *is* the reward model in disguise):

$$
r(x, y) - r(x, y') = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y' \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y' \mid x)}.
$$

Plug into Bradley-Terry:

$$
\boxed{\ \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_c, y_r)}\!\left[\log \sigma\!\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_c \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_c \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_r \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_r \mid x)} \right)\right]. \ }
$$

This is the DPO loss. It is

- a **supervised loss** on preference triples,
- evaluable from $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$ alone (no reward model, no PPO),
- requires only the **log-probability of each completion** under both models — a single forward pass each.

You substitute it into your training loop and run. Standard cross-entropy-style optimisation. The total ML-engineering effort to switch from "train a model with SFT" to "train a model with SFT + DPO" is roughly 50 lines of code.

## 5. Why DPO works

Three perspectives on the same fact.

### 5.1 The reward and policy share parameters

The DPO claim is "your language model is secretly a reward model." More precisely: the log-ratio $\log(\pi_\theta / \pi_\text{ref})$ has the *same gradient structure* as a reward model. Optimising this log-ratio with the Bradley-Terry loss is equivalent to optimising a reward model and then deriving the optimal policy.

This is not a coincidence; it's the closed-form solution of the constrained optimisation problem. The reward model and the optimal policy are two sides of the same exponential-family pair.

### 5.2 No exploration needed

PPO is an RL algorithm — it samples from $\pi_\theta$, gets rewards, and updates. The sampling is what makes PPO expensive (long completions, four-model memory). DPO is *not* RL: there is no sampling step. You feed it pre-collected preference pairs, compute log-probabilities under $\pi_\theta$, take a gradient step. Off-policy, fully offline.

This is why DPO is so much cheaper than PPO-RLHF: it eliminates the entire rollout phase.

### 5.3 The implicit reward is well-defined

The DPO trained policy $\pi_\theta^\text{DPO}$ corresponds, via the inversion, to an implicit reward

$$
\hat r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta^\text{DPO}(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)},
$$

defined up to a $y$-independent constant. This reward can be *recovered* after training — useful for evaluation, Best-of-N selection ([9.15](/session/ch9-alignment/9.15-inference-time-alignment)), or any downstream task that wants a scalar score.

DPO doesn't kill the reward model; it just makes it *implicit*. If you need an explicit reward, you can derive it from $\pi_\theta^\text{DPO}$.

## 6. The implementation

Pseudocode:

```python
def dpo_loss(pi_theta, pi_ref, x, y_c, y_r, beta=0.1):
    # log p(y | x) under each model — sum over tokens
    log_p_chosen_theta = log_prob(pi_theta, x, y_c)
    log_p_chosen_ref   = log_prob(pi_ref,   x, y_c)
    log_p_reject_theta = log_prob(pi_theta, x, y_r)
    log_p_reject_ref   = log_prob(pi_ref,   x, y_r)

    log_ratio_chosen = log_p_chosen_theta - log_p_chosen_ref
    log_ratio_reject = log_p_reject_theta - log_p_reject_ref

    # DPO objective
    logits = beta * (log_ratio_chosen - log_ratio_reject)
    return -F.logsigmoid(logits).mean()
```

That's the whole thing. Substitute `log_prob` with your model's per-sequence log-likelihood (sum of per-token log-softmax values for the target tokens).

### 6.1 Memory budget

Two models in memory: $\pi_\theta$ (training) and $\pi_\text{ref}$ (frozen). Per training step, four forward passes (two completions × two models), one backward (only through $\pi_\theta$). No critic, no reward model, no sampling.

For a 7B model in bf16 with Adam, total memory: $\sim 14$ GB (working) + $\sim 14$ GB (frozen ref) + $\sim 56$ GB (Adam, etc.) $\approx 84$ GB. Compare with PPO-RLHF's $\sim 300$ GB — roughly 4× less. This is why DPO has become the default for $\leq 70$B model alignment.

### 6.2 Data requirements

DPO needs the same preference data as a reward model training run: $(x, y_c, y_r)$ triples. Quality matters as much as quantity. Tülu 3 ($\sim 380$K preferences), LLaMA-3-Instruct ($\sim 10$M preferences) — large preference corpora are the new "scaling axis" for alignment.

The data does *not* need to be on-policy: $y_c$ and $y_r$ can come from any model, even a different one. (Though sampling from $\pi_\theta$ itself, then preference-labelling, leads to **iterative DPO** — [9.11](/session/ch9-alignment/9.11-iterative-dpo).)

## 7. What DPO doesn't capture

DPO is a beautiful derivation but has limits. Three worth knowing:

### 7.1 No off-distribution improvement

PPO can find completions *better than any in the preference data* by sampling from the policy and scoring with the reward model. DPO is bounded by the preference data's quality — it can only encourage what's in $y_c$ and discourage what's in $y_r$. If the best $y_c$ in your data is mediocre, DPO can't exceed it.

This is the main empirical reason frontier labs still use PPO: at the limit of data quality, PPO can push further. (Iterative DPO partially closes this gap; see [9.11](/session/ch9-alignment/9.11-iterative-dpo).)

### 7.2 No reward model for downstream use

PPO-RLHF leaves you with a reward model $r_\phi$ that can be used for many things: Best-of-N inference, evaluation, data filtering. DPO leaves you with only $\pi_\theta^\text{DPO}$; you can recover the implicit reward but it's tightly tied to $\pi_\theta^\text{DPO}$, not an independent score.

### 7.3 Length bias

A subtle empirical issue: DPO tends to *increase response length* relative to the SFT baseline, even when length isn't preferred. The reason is that DPO's loss is per-sequence, not per-token, and longer completions have more total log-probability mass to shift. Length-normalised DPO variants (e.g., R-DPO, IPO) address this with a length-aware penalty.

## 8. The historical significance

DPO arrived in May 2023. By late 2023 it was the default for academic alignment work. By mid-2024 it had displaced PPO for most open-source instruction tuning. The trajectory is unusually fast for an ML methodology — the derivation is just *clean enough* that practitioners trusted it immediately, and the implementation is just *small enough* that you can switch in an afternoon.

DPO also opened the door to a family of related "supervised alignment" methods:

- **IPO (Identity Preference Optimisation)** — addresses DPO's tendency to over-fit.
- **KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimisation)** — uses *individual* ratings rather than pairwise.
- **SimPO** — drops the reference model entirely.
- **ORPO** — combines SFT and preference learning in one step.

The derivational shape — "solve the KL-constrained policy, invert for the reward, substitute into a preference likelihood" — generalises. Each method varies the assumptions (Bradley-Terry vs Plackett-Luce vs etc.) or the data model (pairwise vs pointwise) but shares the core trick.

<Aside type="history">
**The DPO derivation is one of the cleanest results in modern deep learning.** Three pages of algebra take you from "we need RL with a learned reward and KL penalty" to "minimise this supervised loss on preference data." The clarity is partly because the underlying optimisation problem — KL-penalised reward maximisation — has a closed-form solution that no one had thought to *invert*. The trick to look for the closed form, invert it, substitute it into the preference likelihood, and watch the partition function cancel — that combination took six years (InstructGPT-era 2017 RLHF papers to DPO 2023) to find. A reminder that "the obvious thing" is sometimes obvious only in retrospect.
</Aside>

## 9. What we keep going forward

Three things:

1. **The closed-form KL-optimal policy is a Boltzmann distribution over rewards** with the reference as prior. This is true for any KL-penalised reward-maximisation problem.
2. **Invert it.** $r(x, y) = \beta \log(\pi^*/\pi_\text{ref}) + \beta \log Z(x)$. The reward is the log-ratio. The partition function cancels in pairwise comparisons.
3. **DPO loss:** $-\log \sigma(\beta(\log(\pi_\theta(y_c)/\pi_\text{ref}(y_c)) - \log(\pi_\theta(y_r)/\pi_\text{ref}(y_r))))$. Supervised, off-policy, two models in memory.

[Next session](/session/ch9-alignment/9.11-iterative-dpo) covers the modern recipe: **Iterative / Online DPO**, where you do DPO, generate new completions from the trained policy, preference-label them, and DPO again. This closes the gap with PPO's "go beyond the data" property and is the recipe behind LLaMA-3-Instruct, Tülu 3, and most 2024-onwards open instruction-tuned models.
