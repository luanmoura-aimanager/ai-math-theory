## 1. Why this session exists

§9.5 ended with REINFORCE: a correct but high-variance, sample-inefficient policy gradient algorithm. The two operational problems were (a) fresh samples are required for each gradient step, so each step is expensive, and (b) a single unlucky large gradient step can push the policy off-distribution and break it.

**Proximal Policy Optimization** (Schulman et al., 2017, [arXiv:1707.06347](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)) addresses both. The core idea: use *importance sampling* to reuse on-policy samples for multiple gradient steps, and *clip* the importance-weighted objective so that each step cannot drag the policy too far from its sampling distribution.

PPO became the default RL algorithm in 2017 for control / robotics and was inherited by RLHF in 2020. Every major RLHF deployment from InstructGPT (2022) through Llama 3 (2024) used PPO until DPO and GRPO offered alternatives. Even now, PPO remains the reference algorithm — the one DPO is *derived against* (§9.10) and the one GRPO is *simplified from* (§9.7).

This session derives the clipped surrogate objective, names the additional machinery (advantage estimation with GAE, value-function critic, per-token application), and writes the full PPO loop for LLM training.

---

## 2. Importance sampling and the surrogate objective

REINFORCE's gradient is $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[r(y) \nabla \log \pi_\theta(y)]$. The expectation is *under the current policy* — we cannot reuse old samples from a previous policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, because the gradient direction depends on $\theta$, not just on the samples.

Importance sampling lifts this restriction. The identity is

$$
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[f(y)] = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)} f(y) \right],
$$

which holds for any function $f$ such that the ratio is well-defined. The ratio $\rho_\theta(y) := \pi_\theta(y) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y)$ is the **importance-sampling weight** that re-weights samples from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ to give expectations under $\pi_\theta$.

Applying this to the policy gradient:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[ \rho_\theta(y)\, A(x, y)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \right] = \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\left[ \rho_\theta(y)\, A(x, y) \right],
$$

where we've replaced the raw reward with the advantage $A(x, y) = r(x, y) - b(x)$ (§9.5 §5.1) — a baseline-subtracted reward — and the second equality uses $\nabla_\theta \rho_\theta = \rho_\theta \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$.

So the gradient of $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}[\rho_\theta(y) A(x, y)]$ — a function of $\theta$ that *can be evaluated on samples from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$* — is the policy gradient at the current $\theta$. We can take *multiple gradient steps* on this objective using a single batch of samples, as long as we treat $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ as fixed and the importance ratio updates as $\theta$ moves.

This is the **surrogate objective**:

$$
\mathcal{L}^\text{surr}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}} \bigl[ \rho_\theta(y)\, A(x, y) \bigr].
$$

Maximising $\mathcal{L}^\text{surr}$ with respect to $\theta$ moves the policy in the direction of higher expected reward — but only locally. If $\theta$ moves too far from $\theta_\text{old}$, the importance ratio $\rho_\theta$ can become extreme (very large or very small), and the surrogate becomes a poor approximation to the true objective.

## 3. The clipping trick

Schulman's contribution: prevent $\theta$ from moving too far in one update by *clipping* the importance ratio in the surrogate.

Define

$$
\boxed{\
\mathcal{L}^\text{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}} \Bigl[ \min\bigl( \rho_\theta(y)\, A(x, y),\ \text{clip}(\rho_\theta(y), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon)\, A(x, y) \bigr) \Bigr],
\ }
$$

where $\text{clip}(z, a, b) = \min(\max(z, a), b)$ saturates $z$ to the interval $[a, b]$, and $\epsilon$ (typically $0.1$–$0.3$, with $0.2$ as the de facto default) is the clip threshold.

The structure of the clipping:

- **If $A > 0$** (the action was better than the baseline) and $\rho > 1 + \epsilon$, the clipped version is $(1 + \epsilon) A$, which is smaller than $\rho A$. The $\min$ picks the smaller, *capping* the surrogate reward. So the policy update will not chase a more-than-$(1+\epsilon)$-times increase in this action's probability.
- **If $A < 0$** (the action was worse than the baseline) and $\rho < 1 - \epsilon$, the clipped version is $(1 - \epsilon) A$, which is *larger* than $\rho A$ (because $A < 0$, smaller-magnitude $\rho$ gives a less-negative value). The $\min$ now picks $\rho A$, *not* clipping. So the policy is allowed to push probability all the way down. The clipping is asymmetric and only triggers when probability is being *increased* on positive advantage or *decreased* on negative advantage.

Wait, that last paragraph is mis-stated. Let me redo it carefully.

The $\min$ is over (a) the unclipped surrogate $\rho A$ and (b) the clipped surrogate $\text{clip}(\rho, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \cdot A$. The objective is *maximised* with respect to $\theta$. So:

- **If $A > 0$:** We want $\rho$ to grow (to increase the action's probability). If $\rho > 1 + \epsilon$, clipping caps it at $1 + \epsilon$. Since $A > 0$, the clipped term $(1+\epsilon) A < \rho A$. The $\min$ picks the smaller, so the objective is the clipped value. The gradient with respect to $\theta$ at this point is *zero* — further increases in $\rho$ do not improve the objective. So the policy stops pushing this action's probability up.
- **If $A < 0$:** We want $\rho$ to shrink (to decrease the action's probability). If $\rho < 1 - \epsilon$, clipping floors it at $1 - \epsilon$. Since $A < 0$, the clipped term $(1-\epsilon) A > \rho A$ (less negative). The $\min$ picks the smaller, $\rho A$, which is the *unclipped* value. So the objective continues to decrease as $\rho$ decreases — the policy *can* push this action's probability further down.

Hmm, this is the *opposite* behaviour. Let me re-derive. Actually I had it right the first time but got the asymmetry direction confused; here is the careful version:

PPO uses $\min(\rho A, \text{clip}(\rho, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A)$. The two terms are:

- $\rho A$: the unclipped surrogate.
- $\text{clip}(\rho, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A$: the clipped surrogate.

When $A > 0$: clipping $\rho$ at $1+\epsilon$ (from above) lowers $\rho A$. $\min$ picks the lower of the two. So $\min$ caps the *upside* of the surrogate when $\rho > 1+\epsilon$: no incentive to push $\rho$ beyond $1+\epsilon$. When $\rho < 1-\epsilon$, clipping floors $\rho$ at $1-\epsilon$, making the clipped term *larger* than $\rho A$; $\min$ picks $\rho A$, the unclipped — so the objective is *not* clipped from below for positive $A$. The policy continues to be rewarded for moving $\rho$ up from a too-low value.

When $A < 0$: clipping $\rho$ at $1-\epsilon$ (from below) lowers $\rho A$ further (more negative). $\min$ picks $\rho A$, which is the lower. So the surrogate is *not* clipped here — the policy keeps being penalised. When $\rho > 1+\epsilon$, clipping floors $\rho$ at $1+\epsilon$, making the clipped term less negative; $\min$ picks $\rho A$ (the unclipped, more negative one). So again the surrogate is not clipped from below.

Net effect: **the clipping caps the upside of moving probability in the "right" direction**. Moving an action's probability beyond $1+\epsilon$ when it has positive advantage, or below $1-\epsilon$ when it has negative advantage, gives no further gradient. The policy is bounded from making any single action's probability move by more than a multiplicative factor of $1\pm\epsilon$ per update.

This is the **trust region** interpretation: PPO's clipping keeps the new policy within a multiplicative-ratio neighbourhood of the old, preventing destabilising large updates.

<Aside type="note">
**Trust-region origins.** PPO descends from Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO; Schulman et al., 2015), which directly imposed a KL constraint between the new and old policies via a constrained optimisation. TRPO was theoretically clean but operationally complex (each step required a constrained optimiser). PPO replaced TRPO's hard constraint with a soft clipped objective that captured most of the benefit at a fraction of the implementation cost. The 2017 paper's main selling point was "TRPO performance, much simpler code."
</Aside>

## 4. The advantage function and GAE

PPO uses *advantages* $A(x, y)$ rather than raw rewards (§9.5 §5.1). The advantage is the expected-reward improvement of taking action $y$ at prompt $x$ over the prompt's baseline.

For LLM RLHF, the standard advantage estimator is **Generalised Advantage Estimation** (GAE; Schulman et al., 2015, [arXiv:1506.02438](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02438)). For a trajectory with per-step rewards $r_t$ and value-function estimates $V_\psi(s_t)$, GAE defines per-step advantages

$$
\hat A_t^\text{GAE} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma \lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l}, \qquad \delta_t := r_t + \gamma V_\psi(s_{t+1}) - V_\psi(s_t),
$$

where $\delta_t$ is the temporal-difference error and $\gamma, \lambda \in [0, 1]$ are hyperparameters. GAE interpolates between high-variance Monte Carlo estimates ($\lambda = 1$) and high-bias one-step TD estimates ($\lambda = 0$).

For LLM RLHF the conventions are usually:

- $\gamma = 1$ (no per-token discount — we care equally about all tokens of the response).
- $\lambda \in [0.95, 1]$ (close to Monte Carlo; some bias for variance reduction).
- Per-token reward $r_t = 0$ except at the last token, where $r_t = r_\phi(x, y)$ — possibly with the KL penalty subtracted at each token (§9.9).

The value function $V_\psi$ is a separate neural network trained jointly with the policy — the *critic* in actor-critic terminology.

---

## 5. The value-function critic

The critic $V_\psi(s)$ estimates the expected return from state $s$. It is trained by regression on the observed returns:

$$
\mathcal{L}^\text{V}(\psi) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[ (V_\psi(s_t) - R_t)^2 \bigr],
$$

where $R_t = \sum_{t' \geq t} \gamma^{t'-t} r_{t'}$ is the empirical return-to-go.

In LLM RLHF, the critic is usually:

- The same architecture as the policy (a transformer).
- Initialised from the policy or the reward model.
- Trained jointly with the policy in PPO's training loop.

This doubles the GPU memory cost of PPO (we now hold the policy, the frozen RM, *and* the critic — three transformers — plus the frozen reference policy for the KL penalty of §9.9, which is four). PPO RLHF is *memory-hungry* compared to SFT or DPO, which is one of DPO's selling points.

## 6. The full PPO loop for LLM RLHF

Putting it all together:

```
PPO for LLM RLHF:

Initialize π_θ ← π_SFT, V_ψ (critic, e.g. from RM init), π_ref ← π_SFT (frozen).

For iteration k = 1, 2, ...:
    # === ROLLOUT ===
    1. Sample a batch of prompts {x_i}.
    2. For each x_i, sample y_i ~ π_θ_old(· | x_i), and record per-token log-probs.
    3. Compute per-token rewards: r_t = (RM reward at last token) - β · KL(π_θ_old || π_ref).
    4. Compute per-token advantages Â_t via GAE using V_ψ.

    # === MULTIPLE UPDATES per rollout ===
    For epoch e = 1, ..., E_ppo (typically 2-4):
        For minibatch in batch:
            # Compute importance ratios
            log_probs_new = π_θ.log_probs(y_i | x_i, per-token)
            ratios = exp(log_probs_new - log_probs_old)  # ρ_t per token

            # PPO clipped policy loss
            loss_policy = -mean( min(ratios · Â, clip(ratios, 1-ε, 1+ε) · Â) )

            # Critic loss
            loss_value = mean( (V_ψ(s_t) - R_t)^2 )

            # Optional entropy bonus
            loss_entropy = -mean(entropy(π_θ))

            loss = loss_policy + c_v · loss_value - c_e · loss_entropy
            loss.backward()
            optimizer.step()

    # End of epoch: copy θ → θ_old, ψ → ψ_old.
```

A few important details:

- **Multiple update epochs per rollout.** This is what importance sampling buys us: one expensive rollout, $E_\text{ppo} \in [2, 4]$ updates per data point. (More epochs sometimes destabilise — the importance ratios drift further from 1.)
- **KL penalty applied at every token.** This is the standard practical way to handle the KL constraint we'll derive in §9.9. Alternative: KL only as a single term per trajectory.
- **Entropy bonus.** A small term that rewards keeping the policy's per-token distribution from collapsing to determinism. Helps exploration. Coefficient $c_e \in [0, 0.01]$.
- **Value coefficient.** $c_v \in [0.1, 1.0]$ scales the critic loss relative to the policy loss.

### 6.1 Per-token vs per-trajectory

A subtle implementation choice: the importance ratio $\rho$ can be defined per-token or per-trajectory.

- **Per-token:** $\rho_t = \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$, one ratio per token, applied with the per-token advantage $\hat A_t$.
- **Per-trajectory:** $\rho = \pi_\theta(y \mid x) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)$, one ratio for the whole sequence.

The per-token form is dominant: it gives finer-grained credit assignment and integrates naturally with GAE's per-step advantages. Per-trajectory is occasionally used in simpler implementations (e.g., RLOO).

## 7. Hyperparameters and recipes

Standard PPO hyperparameters for LLM RLHF (Llama 2, InstructGPT, OpenRLHF default):

| Hyperparameter | Typical value |
|---|---|
| Clip threshold $\epsilon$ | $0.2$ |
| GAE $\gamma$ | $1.0$ |
| GAE $\lambda$ | $0.95$–$1.0$ |
| Update epochs $E_\text{ppo}$ | $2$–$4$ |
| Value coefficient $c_v$ | $0.5$ |
| Entropy coefficient $c_e$ | $0$–$0.01$ |
| Learning rate (policy) | $1 \times 10^{-6}$–$5 \times 10^{-6}$ |
| Learning rate (critic) | $1 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| KL penalty coefficient $\beta$ | $0.01$–$0.2$, adaptive (§9.9) |
| Rollout batch size | $128$–$1024$ prompts |
| Generation max-new-tokens | $256$–$2048$ |

The two parameters that materially change training are $\beta$ (the KL penalty, §9.9) and the learning rate. Most other hyperparameters are reasonably forgiving.

PPO for LLM RLHF is mature engineering: open-source implementations (DeepSpeed-Chat, OpenRLHF, TRL) provide drop-in PPO trainers that handle the rollout / update split, multi-GPU sharding, and the four-model memory layout (policy, critic, RM, reference).

## 8. PPO's weaknesses

PPO works well but has several well-known operational pains.

**(a) Memory cost.** Holding four transformer-sized models (policy, critic, RM, reference) is expensive. For a 70B policy, this is roughly $4 \times 140$ GB $= 560$ GB just for model parameters in fp16, before activations and optimiser state. Multi-node sharding is standard.

**(b) Hyperparameter sensitivity.** $\beta$, the KL coefficient, has to be tuned per dataset. Too small and the policy drifts off-distribution (reward hacks); too large and the policy never moves. Llama 2 used adaptive $\beta$ (auto-adjusted based on observed KL); even so, the recipe required substantial tuning per training run.

**(c) Training instability.** PPO RLHF runs occasionally diverge: loss spikes, output quality crashes, KL explodes. The literature includes many cautionary tales and many proposed mitigations.

**(d) Reward hacking.** The policy learns to exploit reward-model errors rather than improve substantively (§9.14). PPO has no structural defence beyond the KL penalty.

These pains motivated DPO (§9.10), which skips the critic, the rollouts, the importance sampling, and the clipping entirely — by deriving the policy update directly from preferences. We will see in §9.10 that DPO is, in a precise sense, "the right answer of PPO at a specific limit."

## 9. What we keep going forward

1. **PPO's clipped surrogate objective** $\mathcal{L}^\text{PPO} = \mathbb{E}[\min(\rho A, \text{clip}(\rho, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A)]$ is the workhorse RL objective for LLM RLHF since 2017. It allows multi-step updates per rollout via importance sampling, with clipping preventing destabilising large updates.

2. **GAE advantages with a learned value-function critic.** Per-token advantages $\hat A_t$ replace raw rewards; the critic $V_\psi$ provides the baseline.

3. **The full RLHF PPO loop holds four models in memory:** policy, critic, RM, reference. Memory-hungry but mature.

4. **Standard $\epsilon = 0.2$, $\lambda \approx 0.95$, $E_\text{ppo} \in [2,4]$.** Most other knobs are forgiving; $\beta$ (the KL coefficient) requires tuning per run.

5. **PPO's weaknesses (memory, hyperparameter sensitivity, reward hacking) motivated DPO and GRPO** — the algorithms of §9.7 and §9.10 that recapture PPO's benefits with simpler structure.
