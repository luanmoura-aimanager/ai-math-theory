## 1. Why this session exists

The RLHF and GRPO formulae of [9.7](/session/ch9-alignment/9.7-grpo)–[9.8](/session/ch9-alignment/9.8-rlhf-end-to-end) both contain an extra term beyond the reward:

$$
\tilde r(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta \cdot \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x) \,\Vert\, \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)\big).
$$

The $-\beta \cdot \mathrm{KL}$ term looks like an arbitrary regulariser. It is not arbitrary; it is the *single most important safety mechanism* in RLHF, preventing two structural failure modes that would otherwise destroy the trained model.

This session derives the penalty from first principles, identifies what goes wrong without it, and shows how to tune $\beta$ in practice. The same penalty reappears in DPO's derivation ([9.10](/session/ch9-alignment/9.10-dpo)) — DPO's closed-form solution is exactly the KL-penalised RLHF objective minimised analytically.

---

## 2. What goes wrong without the KL

Imagine running PPO with just the raw reward $r_\phi(x, y)$, no KL penalty. The objective is

$$
\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[r_\phi(x, y)\big].
$$

The policy $\pi_\theta$ can drift arbitrarily far from $\pi_\text{SFT}$ in pursuit of higher reward. Two specific failure modes follow.

### 2.1 Reward hacking

The reward model $r_\phi$ is an *approximation* of human preferences, trained on a finite set of comparisons. It has bugs, biases, and out-of-distribution failures. If the policy drifts far enough, it can find completions that score very high under $r_\phi$ but are *bad* under true human preferences.

A canonical example: $r_\phi$ might have learned that longer answers are preferred (training data was correlated with length). PPO without KL discovers this and produces increasingly verbose completions — eventually arriving at multi-page rambles that score well under $r_\phi$ but are unhelpful to users.

This is **reward hacking** (or Goodhart's law in disguise): when a proxy metric becomes the optimisation target, the proxy stops being a good measure. The KL penalty bounds how far the policy can drift, keeping it in the region where $r_\phi$ is approximately a valid proxy.

### 2.2 Mode collapse

A second failure: the policy starts to **collapse** to a single deterministic output per prompt. If one completion scores well, the policy concentrates probability mass on it; the gradient amplifies that concentration; eventually the policy is near-deterministic, producing the same completion for any related prompt.

Mode collapse destroys the model's *generative diversity*. The pretrained base could write a story in 10 different styles; after PPO without KL, it writes the same story 10 times.

The KL penalty resists collapse because $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_\text{ref})$ explodes if $\pi_\theta$ becomes degenerate (a delta) when $\pi_\text{ref}$ has spread mass. The penalty fights collapse and preserves the diversity of $\pi_\text{ref}$.

Both failure modes are *practical observations*, not theoretical guarantees. InstructGPT (Ouyang et al. 2022) §3.5 documents both; every subsequent RLHF paper that ablates the KL term reproduces them.

## 3. The penalty term, mathematically

Recall ([0.6](/session/ch0-math-foundations/0.6-entropy-kl)) the KL divergence between two distributions $p, q$ on $\mathcal{Y}$:

$$
\mathrm{KL}(p \,\Vert\, q) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p}\big[\log p(y) - \log q(y)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p}\!\left[\log \frac{p(y)}{q(y)}\right] \geq 0,
$$

with equality iff $p = q$. Two key properties:

- **Non-negative.** $\mathrm{KL} \geq 0$.
- **Asymmetric.** $\mathrm{KL}(p \,\Vert\, q) \neq \mathrm{KL}(q \,\Vert\, p)$ in general.
- **Mode-seeking.** The forward direction $\mathrm{KL}(p \,\Vert\, q)$ is *infinite* if $p$ puts mass anywhere $q$ doesn't. So if $\pi_\text{ref}$ has full support, $\pi_\theta$ cannot collapse to a delta on regions outside $\pi_\text{ref}$'s support without paying infinite KL.

In RLHF, $p = \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$ (the policy being trained) and $q = \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)$ (the SFT reference). The penalty term added to the reward:

$$
- \beta \cdot \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_\text{ref}\big) = -\beta \cdot \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\!\left[\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}\right].
$$

### 3.1 Per-token implementation

For autoregressive LLMs, $\pi(y \mid x) = \prod_t \pi(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})$, so

$$
\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)} = \sum_{t = 1}^{T} \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}.
$$

The KL penalty distributes across tokens. In PPO, each token's reward becomes

$$
\boxed{\ r_t = -\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})} + \mathbb{1}[t = T] \cdot r_\phi(x, y). \ }
$$

The reward model fires at the end of the sequence; the KL penalty fires every token as a per-step log-ratio correction. GAE then accumulates these into advantages for PPO.

This per-token implementation has two consequences:

- **The penalty is computed online** — using $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$ at sampling time. Both must be in memory.
- **The KL is "as $\pi_\theta$ moves"** — strictly $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_\text{ref})$, not the symmetric or reversed direction. The asymmetry matters in §3.

### 3.2 Why forward-KL, not reverse?

The choice $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_\text{ref})$ (forward, sampling from $\pi_\theta$) vs $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\text{ref} \,\Vert\, \pi_\theta)$ (reverse, sampling from $\pi_\text{ref}$) is not arbitrary.

**Forward KL is *mode-seeking* in the wrong direction for our purposes** — strictly speaking, it forces $\pi_\theta$ to cover all of $\pi_\text{ref}$'s support, which is what we want for diversity preservation. But sampling from $\pi_\theta$ during training is *practical* — those are the samples PPO already needs for the policy gradient. So forward KL is computed for free from the same samples.

**Reverse KL** would require sampling from $\pi_\text{ref}$, which is extra forward passes through the frozen reference. Possible but inefficient.

Empirically, forward KL works well enough that the inefficiency-driven choice is also a good choice in practice. DPO's derivation ([9.10](/session/ch9-alignment/9.10-dpo)) uses forward KL implicitly through the same reasoning.

## 4. Choosing $\beta$

$\beta$ is the single most important hyperparameter of RLHF after the SFT and reward-model choices.

### 4.1 Effects of $\beta$

- **$\beta \to 0$**: KL term vanishes. Policy can drift arbitrarily; reward hacking and mode collapse dominate.
- **$\beta \to \infty$**: KL term dominates. Policy cannot move from $\pi_\text{ref}$; RL contributes nothing.
- **$\beta$ "just right"**: policy improves reward while staying close to $\pi_\text{ref}$. Balance of exploration and constraint.

Typical $\beta \in [0.01, 0.3]$ in published recipes. InstructGPT used $\beta = 0.02$. LLaMA-2 used $\beta = 0.01$. The trend over time has been *lower* $\beta$ — meaning more freedom for the policy to move — as practitioners have learned where the sweet spot is for different model scales.

### 4.2 Adaptive $\beta$

Some implementations adapt $\beta$ during training. The most common rule: monitor the average KL during sampling; if it exceeds a target value $\mathrm{KL}^*$, increase $\beta$; if it falls below, decrease $\beta$. This keeps the *effective* drift bounded.

Adaptive $\beta$ is helpful when the reward model's gradient pushes the policy unevenly across prompts: easy prompts have small gradients (less KL), hard prompts have large gradients (more KL). A fixed $\beta$ either under-constrains hard prompts or over-constrains easy ones. Adaptive control balances them.

### 4.3 Connection to TRPO / natural gradient

Mathematically, the KL-penalised reward is the Lagrangian of the constrained optimisation

$$
\max_\theta \mathbb{E}\big[r(x, y)\big] \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_\text{ref}\big) \leq \delta,
$$

with $\beta$ the Lagrange multiplier. TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) implements this constraint directly via a trust-region search; PPO with KL penalty approximates it with the dual form. The "KL penalty" is the *soft* version of the "trust region" idea — penalise instead of constrain.

This makes the KL penalty mathematically natural: it is the Lagrangian formulation of "don't move the policy too far from the reference in one step."

## 5. The "KL budget" view

A useful mental model: think of the cumulative KL between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$ as a **budget** that PPO spends to improve reward.

Empirically, the policy improves reward roughly linearly with KL distance from the reference, up to some point. Past that, reward hacking sets in and the *true* reward (human preference) degrades even as $r_\phi$ keeps improving. The "sweet spot" KL budget is implementation-specific but typically $\mathrm{KL} \in [10, 30]$ nats per completion at convergence.

This is why early stopping on a held-out preference set is standard practice in production RLHF: stop training when the held-out preference rate stops improving, even if $r_\phi$ score on the training distribution keeps climbing. The KL budget gives a tight handle on this; you can monitor cumulative KL and decide when "enough is enough".

<Aside type="warning">
**The KL penalty does not fix a bad reward model.** It only prevents the policy from running away to the extreme tail of the reward model's predictions. If the reward model is systematically biased — say, prefers verbose answers — the KL-bounded policy still becomes verbose, just less so than the unbounded policy. Fixing the bias requires better preference data or better reward modelling, not bigger $\beta$.

A common debugging anti-pattern: "increase $\beta$ to fix reward hacking" — this often hides the symptom rather than the cause. If you see degraded quality on a held-out set, investigate the reward model first; tune $\beta$ second.
</Aside>

## 6. Why the reference matters

The KL is computed against $\pi_\text{ref}$, which in the canonical RLHF recipe is $\pi_\text{SFT}$ — the supervised-fine-tuned model. Three reasons this is the right choice:

**(a) SFT is already in the "instruction-following" mode.** Using $\pi_\text{base}$ (the pretrained model) as reference would anchor the policy to "next-token predictor" behaviour, which is incompatible with the RLHF goal of "instruction follower." The KL would explode trying to keep an instruction-following policy close to a next-token predictor.

**(b) SFT defines the "valid distribution" of completions.** Anything outside $\pi_\text{SFT}$'s support is suspicious — it's not the kind of response humans tend to write or that the reward model has been trained on. The KL penalty enforces "stay inside what we've validated."

**(c) SFT gives us natural diversity.** $\pi_\text{SFT}$ still has high entropy from its pretraining background; this is the diversity we want to preserve. Anchoring to $\pi_\text{base}$ would over-constrain; anchoring to a more focused intermediate model would under-constrain.

A few RLHF variants use $\pi_\text{base}$ or a separate "diversity model" as reference. These are exotic; the standard is $\pi_\text{SFT}$.

## 7. Connection to information theory

The KL penalty has a natural information-theoretic interpretation. $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_\text{ref})$ measures the *additional information* required to encode samples from $\pi_\theta$ given a code optimised for $\pi_\text{ref}$. In nats:

$$
\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_\text{ref}) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[\log \pi_\theta(y) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y)].
$$

If $\pi_\theta = \pi_\text{ref}$, no extra information is required. As $\pi_\theta$ diverges, the per-sample "encoding penalty" grows. The penalty $-\beta \cdot \mathrm{KL}$ is paying for the bits of information by which the new policy departs from the old.

This connects to **rate-distortion theory**: the policy may distort outputs (increase $r$) but pays an information-theoretic cost (KL). The Pareto frontier of $(r, \mathrm{KL})$ traces what's achievable; PPO with a particular $\beta$ picks one point on it.

## 8. What we keep going forward

Three things:

1. **The KL penalty prevents reward hacking and mode collapse.** Both are practical, observed failure modes; the penalty is the structural safeguard.
2. **Per-token implementation** as $r_t = r_\text{model}(\mathbb{1}[t = T]) - \beta \cdot \log(\pi_\theta(y_t)/\pi_\text{ref}(y_t))$. PPO's GAE then accumulates these.
3. **$\beta$ is the most important hyperparameter** of RLHF after the reward model. Typical $\beta \in [0.01, 0.3]$. Adaptive $\beta$ that targets a fixed KL is a common production refinement.
