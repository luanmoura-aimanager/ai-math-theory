## 1. Why this session exists

Before we deploy neural language models, we should know what the simplest baseline gives. An **$n$-gram model** parameterises $p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ by truncating the conditioning context to the last $n - 1$ tokens and using an empirical estimate from a corpus. It has no parameters to *train* in the gradient-descent sense: the "training" is just counting.

Computing one on a toy corpus reveals the three reasons $n$-grams can't be the answer:

1. **Sparsity.** Most $n$-grams never appear, even in massive corpora.
2. **Parameter explosion.** Storage is $\Theta(V^n)$, useless for $n \ge 3$ at LLM-scale vocabularies.
3. **Fixed range.** Any dependency longer than $n - 1$ tokens is mathematically inaccessible to the model.

These limits — and how badly they bite — are *exactly* what a neural language model has to overcome.<FN ns={[1, 3]} />

---

## 2. The Markov assumption

Section 5.3 gave the universal autoregressive factorisation:

$$
p(x_1, \dots, x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid x_{<t}).
$$

The conditional $p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ in principle depends on the *entire* prefix $x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}$. The **$n$-gram approximation** truncates this conditioning to the last $n - 1$ tokens:

$$
\boxed{\ p(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \approx p(x_t \mid x_{t - n + 1}, x_{t - n + 2}, \dots, x_{t - 1}). \ }
$$

This is the **Markov assumption** of order $n - 1$. Names:

- $n = 1$: **unigram.** $p(x_t)$ — no conditioning at all. Bag of words.
- $n = 2$: **bigram.** $p(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ — depends on the immediately preceding token.
- $n = 3$: **trigram.** $p(x_t \mid x_{t-2}, x_{t-1})$.

The full conditioning is recovered only in the limit $n \to T$, which is not a useful model.

### 2.1 Parameter count

Under the bigram assumption, $p(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ is a $V \times V$ matrix: one row per possible $x_{t-1}$, each row a distribution over $V$ next-token possibilities. Number of free parameters (after the $\sum_k p(\cdot \mid x_{t-1}) = 1$ constraint per row): $V(V - 1)$.

For an $n$-gram, the conditioning context $(x_{t-n+1}, \dots, x_{t-1})$ takes $V^{n-1}$ values, and each value has a $V$-way distribution. Free parameters: $V^{n-1}(V - 1) \approx V^n$ for $V$ large.

For $V = 50{,}000$, even a trigram has $\sim 10^{14}$ parameters. Practical $n$-grams use much smaller vocabularies (characters, or word-level with $V \approx 10{,}000$).

## 3. Maximum-likelihood estimation: counting

For a fixed corpus $\mathcal{C}$, define the **count** of any contiguous $k$-token sequence $(w_1, \dots, w_k)$ as

$$
c(w_1, \dots, w_k) = \text{number of times } (w_1, \dots, w_k) \text{ appears in } \mathcal{C}.
$$

The **MLE** for the bigram probability is the ratio:

$$
\boxed{\ \hat p(x_t = b \mid x_{t-1} = a) = \frac{c(a, b)}{c(a)}. \ }
$$

(Compare with the multinomial MLE of [0.7 §4](/session/ch0-math-foundations/0.7-cross-entropy-mle): given counts, the maximum-likelihood probability is the count proportion.) For a general $n$-gram:

$$
\hat p(x_t \mid x_{t-n+1}, \dots, x_{t-1}) = \frac{c(x_{t-n+1}, \dots, x_t)}{c(x_{t-n+1}, \dots, x_{t-1})}.
$$

This is the entire "training" procedure. Count the $n$-grams in the corpus, divide by the $(n-1)$-gram counts in the conditioning context, store the table.

### 3.1 Special sentence-boundary tokens

To make the model handle the *start* and *end* of a sentence, prepend $n - 1$ copies of a special `<bos>` token and append one `<eos>`. Bigram example: the sentence "*the cat sat*" becomes the token sequence

$$
\langle\text{bos}\rangle,\ \text{the},\ \text{cat},\ \text{sat},\ \langle\text{eos}\rangle,
$$

generating four bigrams: $(\text{bos}, \text{the})$, $(\text{the}, \text{cat})$, $(\text{cat}, \text{sat})$, $(\text{sat}, \text{eos})$. The $\langle\text{eos}\rangle$ token makes the model assign a probability to "the sentence ends here" — necessary so that the joint $p(x_1, \dots, x_T)$ is a normalised distribution over sequences of *any* length.

## 4. A worked bigram

Take the toy corpus (sentence boundaries already inserted):

$$
\mathcal{C} = \{\ \langle\text{bos}\rangle,\ \text{I},\ \text{am},\ \text{Sam},\ \langle\text{eos}\rangle,\ \langle\text{bos}\rangle,\ \text{Sam},\ \text{I},\ \text{am},\ \langle\text{eos}\rangle,\ \langle\text{bos}\rangle,\ \text{I},\ \text{do},\ \text{not},\ \text{like},\ \text{green},\ \text{eggs},\ \text{and},\ \text{ham},\ \langle\text{eos}\rangle\ \}.
$$

(Three sentences, 18 tokens excluding boundaries.) Count unigrams and bigrams:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c(\langle\text{bos}\rangle) &= 3, & c(\text{I}) &= 3, & c(\text{am}) &= 2, & c(\text{Sam}) &= 2, \\
c(\text{do}) &= 1, & c(\text{not}) &= 1, & c(\text{like}) &= 1, & c(\text{green}) &= 1, \\
c(\text{eggs}) &= 1, & c(\text{and}) &= 1, & c(\text{ham}) &= 1, & c(\langle\text{eos}\rangle) &= 3.
\end{aligned}
$$

Some bigrams of interest:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c(\langle\text{bos}\rangle, \text{I}) &= 2, & c(\langle\text{bos}\rangle, \text{Sam}) &= 1, \\
c(\text{I}, \text{am}) &= 2, & c(\text{I}, \text{do}) &= 1, \\
c(\text{am}, \text{Sam}) &= 1, & c(\text{am}, \langle\text{eos}\rangle) &= 1, \\
c(\text{Sam}, \text{I}) &= 1, & c(\text{Sam}, \langle\text{eos}\rangle) &= 1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Bigram MLEs:

$$
\hat p(\text{I} \mid \langle\text{bos}\rangle) = \frac{2}{3}, \qquad \hat p(\text{Sam} \mid \langle\text{bos}\rangle) = \frac{1}{3}.
$$

$$
\hat p(\text{am} \mid \text{I}) = \frac{2}{3}, \qquad \hat p(\text{do} \mid \text{I}) = \frac{1}{3}.
$$

$$
\hat p(\text{Sam} \mid \text{am}) = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \hat p(\langle\text{eos}\rangle \mid \text{am}) = \frac{1}{2}.
$$

### 4.1 Probability of a new sentence

Use the model to score the candidate sentence "*Sam I am*":

$$
\hat p(\langle\text{bos}\rangle,\ \text{Sam},\ \text{I},\ \text{am},\ \langle\text{eos}\rangle) = \hat p(\text{Sam} \mid \langle\text{bos}\rangle) \cdot \hat p(\text{I} \mid \text{Sam}) \cdot \hat p(\text{am} \mid \text{I}) \cdot \hat p(\langle\text{eos}\rangle \mid \text{am}).
$$

All four bigrams appear in the corpus, with counts $1, 1, 2, 1$ respectively and denominator counts $3, 2, 3, 2$:

$$
= \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{18}.
$$

### 4.2 Probability of an unseen bigram

Now try "*I like ham*":

$$
\hat p(\text{like} \mid \text{I}) = \frac{c(\text{I}, \text{like})}{c(\text{I})} = \frac{0}{3} = 0.
$$

The model assigns probability **zero** to any sentence containing the bigram $(\text{I}, \text{like})$ — even though both "I" and "like" appear in the corpus, just never adjacent. The joint probability of the sentence is exactly zero, and the cross-entropy loss is $+\infty$. This is the **sparsity problem**.

## 5. Why MLE breaks: sparsity and smoothing

Most $n$-grams of length $n \ge 2$ never appear in any finite corpus. **Heaps' law** estimates the number of distinct $n$-grams in a corpus of size $N$ at $\propto N^\beta$ with $\beta \in (0, 1)$: vocabulary growth is sub-linear, but $V^n$ grows exponentially in $n$. By $n = 3$ the fraction of valid trigrams in even a billion-token corpus is tiny.

The classical fix is **smoothing** — redistribute a small amount of probability mass to unseen events. The simplest is **Laplace add-one**:

$$
\hat p_\text{add-1}(b \mid a) = \frac{c(a, b) + 1}{c(a) + V}.
$$

This guarantees every bigram gets positive probability, at the cost of a heavy bias toward unseen events. More sophisticated methods — **Kneser–Ney smoothing**, **interpolation**, **backoff** — improved the state of the art through the 1990s and 2000s. None of them solve the underlying structural problem: probability mass for unseen $n$-grams is allocated by *prior* (uniform, or based on lower-order counts), not by *generalisation from similar contexts*.

A neural language model, by contrast, generalises through the embedding geometry of [5.2](/session/ch5-sequence-modeling/5.2-embeddings-word2vec): tokens that play similar grammatical or semantic roles get similar embeddings, and the network *interpolates* its predictions across the embedding space. An unseen bigram still receives a meaningful probability because the embeddings of its constituents place them near seen patterns. That single capability — generalisation via continuous representations rather than discrete counting — is the qualitative jump from $n$-grams to neural LMs.

<Aside type="history">
Bengio et al. (2003)<FN n={2} />, *A Neural Probabilistic Language Model*, was the first paper to articulate this argument and to demonstrate that a feed-forward network with learned word embeddings could outperform a smoothed trigram on perplexity. The architecture (a fixed-window feedforward LM with 30M parameters by 2000s standards) is now a historical footnote, but its central observation — that distributed representations dissolve the curse of dimensionality of $n$-gram models — is the foundation of everything that came after.
</Aside><FN n={4} />

## 6. Structural limits of $n$-grams

Beyond sparsity, three structural facts box $n$-grams in:

**(a) Fixed range.** The Markov assumption *guarantees* that any dependency longer than $n - 1$ tokens is invisible. The model cannot resolve

> *The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because $\rule{1cm}{0.4pt}$ was too big.*

The disambiguator (whether $\rule{1cm}{0.4pt}$ is *"it"* referring to the trophy or the suitcase) depends on the verb *"big"* nine tokens later and the noun *"trophy"* eight tokens earlier. No $n$-gram with $n \le 8$ can see the relevant context. Increasing $n$ to fix this triggers parameter explosion.

**(b) No compositionality.** $(\text{the},\ \text{red},\ \text{car})$ and $(\text{a},\ \text{red},\ \text{car})$ are *unrelated* trigrams to the model. There is no notion that the determiner and the noun phrase have separate roles. Neural models share representations across context via the embedding matrix; $n$-grams cannot.

**(c) No syntactic generalisation.** If the corpus contains $(\text{the},\ \text{cat},\ \text{sat})$ but not $(\text{the},\ \text{dog},\ \text{sat})$, the model has no reason to assign similar probabilities. Embeddings give the second a "similar" distribution because $\text{dog}$ and $\text{cat}$ are nearby in embedding space.

These three deficits motivate the move to neural language models, which Bengio et al. (2003) pioneered and which became the universal architecture by the late 2010s.

## 7. What we keep going forward

Two durable lessons from the $n$-gram era:

1. The **left-to-right autoregressive factorisation** is correct and reusable — neural LMs inherit it directly.
2. **Perplexity as a comparison metric** comes from this era. Smoothed-trigram perplexities on news text were $\sim 200$; modern LLMs achieve $\sim 10$.

The $n$-gram baseline gives us a number to beat. Every architecture from Chapter 5.5 onward is, mathematically, a parameterisation of $p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ designed to fix the limitations above — *parameter sharing* across contexts (embeddings), *unbounded conditioning* (RNNs in §5.5, attention in §6), and *generalisation* via continuous representations.
