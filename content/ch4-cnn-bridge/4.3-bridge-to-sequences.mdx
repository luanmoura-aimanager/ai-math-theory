## 1. Why this session exists

Chapter 4 has been a quick consolidation. We have one operation (convolution), one symmetry argument (translation equivariance), and one cost (depth tax for large receptive fields). That kit is *brilliant* for natural images and waveforms. It is *not* what language needs. Before Chapter 5 introduces tokenisation and embeddings — and Chapter 6 builds attention — we will spell out exactly why.

There are three structural mismatches, in increasing order of seriousness. Once they're on the table, the design of attention will look like the obvious response.

---

## 2. Limitation 1: long-range mixing requires deep stacks

Section 4.2 showed: with stride-1 convs of kernel $K = 3$, the receptive field grows as $r_L = 1 + 2L$. A context window of $T = 2048$ tokens needs $L \ge 1024$ such layers for the *last* token to be able to influence the *first*. With dilation $d_\ell = 2^{\ell - 1}$ (WaveNet-style) you can do it in $\log_2 T$ layers, but you pay for it in two ways:

- The receptive field is now structured on a *dilation grid*: positions $t$ and $t + d$ interact only if their relative offset is reachable through the dilation pattern. Arbitrary pairs $(t_1, t_2)$ are not freely available.
- Information that has to travel from position $1$ to position $T$ goes through $\log_2 T$ non-linearities, each of which *loses* information. By the time it arrives, the receptive field is "wide" in name only — see the effective-receptive-field result of Luo et al. (2016), recalled at the end of 4.2.

In a Transformer, by contrast, every layer is a single matrix multiply that mixes all $T$ positions in *one* shot. Depth is then used for *abstraction*, not for *reach*.<FN ns={[1, 2]} />

---

## 3. Limitation 2: equivariance is the wrong symmetry for language

Translation equivariance is a feature in vision: a cat at $(50, 50)$ should fire the same detector as a cat at $(150, 150)$. The semantics of an image are largely translation-stable.

Language is the opposite. The same word in different sentence positions plays *different roles*:

> *"The dog bit the man"* vs *"The man bit the dog"*

These two sentences have identical token multisets. A purely translation-equivariant model — one whose response to a shifted input is just a shifted response — cannot, by construction, prefer one over the other. To distinguish them, the network must *break* translation symmetry: it must care about *which* position a word is in, not just *whether* a word is in some neighbourhood.

In CNNs the symmetry can only be broken after global pooling (which is invariant, not symmetry-breaking) or by ad-hoc positional features added to the input. Attention takes the opposite stance: it bakes position-awareness into the input via *positional encodings* (Chapter 6.5) and lets the attention weights mix tokens *however they want*, with no built-in translation symmetry to fight against.

<Aside type="note">
**1D convs are still translation-equivariant on sequences.** Sliding a kernel along a token sequence treats position 5 and position 500 symmetrically: the same kernel, the same parameters, the same response. That is the structure we *don't* want for language, where the verb usually lives mid-sentence and the subject before it.
</Aside>

---

## 4. Limitation 3: convolution mixes by position, attention mixes by content

This is the deepest of the three. A conv layer combines a token with *its neighbours by index*. Whether the neighbours are semantically relevant or not, they get the same weight. In:

> *"The bank by the river was muddy."*

the meaning of *bank* depends on *river*, two tokens away. In:

> *"The bank on Main Street was closed."*

the meaning of *bank* depends on *Street*, three tokens away. The disambiguating word lives at a *different* index in each sentence. A conv kernel of fixed width and fixed weights cannot route information conditionally on what's there; it always combines positions $[i, i + 1, \dots, i + K - 1]$ with the same coefficients.

What language needs is a *content-addressed* mixing rule: at every position, choose which other positions to attend to *based on what's at them*. That is the one-line definition of attention — a learned, query-dependent weighted sum.

A way to see the contrast quantitatively: a conv layer's output is

$$
z[i] = \sum_{k} w[k]\, x[i + k] \qquad \text{(weights $w[k]$ fixed at training time)},
$$

while an attention layer's output will turn out to be

$$
z[i] = \sum_{j} \alpha_{ij}(x)\, x[j] \qquad \text{(weights $\alpha_{ij}$ computed from $x$ itself)}.
$$

The conv kernel is a *parameter*. The attention weights are an *activation*. That single change — making the mixing coefficients depend on the input — is the algorithmic shift behind every modern LLM.<FN ns={[3, 4, 5, 6]} />

---

## 5. What language needs, in three lines

The diagnosis above implies three desiderata for the operator that replaces convolution:

1. **Global receptive field in one layer.** No depth tax, so depth can buy abstraction rather than reach.
2. **No built-in translation symmetry.** Position is allowed to matter, and must be encoded explicitly when it does.
3. **Content-addressed mixing.** The weights with which tokens combine should be functions of the tokens themselves.

Attention satisfies all three. Chapter 6 derives it from scratch and shows that the entire Transformer architecture is essentially attention plus an MLP, repeated.

---

## 6. Where CNNs still earn their keep

It would be misleading to leave the impression that CNNs are obsolete. They are alive and well in three places that matter for modern LLM systems:

- **Vision encoders for multimodal models.** Many vision-language models still use convolutional backbones — or hybrid conv-attention encoders — as the image-side tower. Convolutions are extremely strong on natural images, where translation equivariance *is* the right prior.
- **Audio and biosignals.** Spectrograms, raw audio, ECG, Raman spectra: 1D and 2D convolutions remain the workhorse for any signal whose local statistics are stationary and whose patterns are translation-stable.
- **Efficient sequence-model variants.** Several "linear-time" alternatives to attention — gated convolutions, state-space models with convolutional kernels (Mamba's discretised form), Hyena — are essentially clever 1D convolutions with learned, content-dependent kernels. They live downstream in Chapter 8.

The result of Chapter 4 isn't *"CNNs are bad."* It is the more precise statement: **for language modelling, the convolutional inductive bias is the wrong shape, and attention is the right shape.** What we keep from Chapter 4 are (a) the abstract notion of equivariance and weight sharing, (b) the fact that compute and parameter counts can be controlled by clever structure, and (c) the explicit cost of a "local-only" operator. Those threads recur in every architecture we meet from now on.

<SelfTest>

**(a)** A CNN classifier with a final global max-pool is translation-*invariant* (its output is unchanged by any input shift). Argue from the definitions in 4.2 that the *intermediate* feature maps are translation-equivariant, and that only the final pool breaks the symmetry. Where exactly does invariance enter?

**(b)** Give two distinct concrete English sentences whose token *multisets* are identical but whose *meanings* differ, and explain in one sentence why a purely translation-equivariant model cannot distinguish them.

**(c)** Sketch why the "content-addressed" formulation $z[i] = \sum_j \alpha_{ij}(x)\, x[j]$ cannot be written as a convolution $z[i] = \sum_k w[k]\, x[i + k]$ for any fixed $w$. (Hint: what is $\alpha_{ij}$ allowed to depend on?)

**(d)** WaveNet stacks $L$ dilated 1D convs with dilations $1, 2, 4, \dots, 2^{L - 1}$. Compute the resulting receptive field. For $T = 1024$, what is the smallest $L$ that lets the model mix every position with every other? Compare with the smallest $L$ for a single attention layer.

</SelfTest>
