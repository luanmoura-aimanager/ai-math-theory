## 1. Why this session exists

A Transformer generates text one token at a time. To produce token $T + 1$ it must run the model on the sequence $(t_1, \dots, t_T)$ and project the *last* output position $z_T$ onto vocabulary logits. To then produce token $T + 2$ it must... run the model again on $(t_1, \dots, t_T, t_{T+1})$? Re-doing $T+1$ tokens of forward computation for each *single* new token would be quadratic-cumulative — generating $N$ tokens after a $T$-token prompt would cost $\sum_{i = 1}^{N} (T + i) \cdot 24 d^2$ in FFN alone, which is roughly the same as one giant forward pass over the full $(T + N)$ context.

The fix is a structural observation that follows from the causal mask of [6.5](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.5-causal-masking): **for any past position $j < T + 1$, the keys $k_j$ and values $v_j$ do not change** when a new token $t_{T+1}$ is appended. They depend only on the embedding of $t_j$ and the model weights — neither of which moves. Cache them once; reuse them for every subsequent generation step.

The result is the **KV cache**: a per-layer per-head store of all previously-seen $K, V$ matrices, accumulated as generation proceeds. Compute per new token drops from $O((T + i) d^2)$ to $O(d^2)$ for the FFN and from $O((T + i)^2 d)$ to $O((T + i) d)$ for attention — both *linear* in the cache size. The KV cache is *the* enabling optimisation of fast autoregressive inference.

This session derives the cache, counts its size, and previews the architectural variants (GQA/MQA — full treatment in Cap 8.3 — and MLA — full treatment in Cap 8.5) that shrink it for very long contexts.

## 2. Why $K$ and $V$ don't change

Recall the forward pass of one Transformer block on input $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}$. Inside multi-head attention (6.4) we compute, per head:

$$
Q = X W_Q, \qquad K = X W_K, \qquad V = X W_V,
$$

each in $\mathbb{R}^{T \times d_h}$ (with separate $W_Q, W_K, W_V$ per head). Then $S = Q K^\top / \sqrt{d_h}$, apply the causal mask, softmax row-wise, multiply by $V$.

When we append a new token at position $T + 1$:

- The new embedding $x_{T+1} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is computed (token + RoPE applied per-head).
- New $Q, K, V$ entries are derived from $x_{T+1}$ alone: $q_{T+1} = x_{T+1} W_Q$, similarly $k_{T+1}, v_{T+1}$.
- **The old rows $k_1, \dots, k_T$ and $v_1, \dots, v_T$ are unchanged.** They depend on $x_1, \dots, x_T$ and on $W_K, W_V$ — neither has moved.

The causal mask of [6.5](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.5-causal-masking) further guarantees that position $T + 1$'s output depends only on $q_{T+1}$ and on $k_1, \dots, k_{T+1}, v_1, \dots, v_{T+1}$ — never on any *future* $K, V$.

So the right computation for one new token is:

1. Read cached $K_\text{past} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_h}, V_\text{past} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_h}$.
2. Compute fresh $q_{T+1}, k_{T+1}, v_{T+1}$ (each in $\mathbb{R}^{d_h}$) from the new token's embedding.
3. Append: $K_\text{new} = [K_\text{past}; k_{T+1}]$, $V_\text{new} = [V_\text{past}; v_{T+1}]$.
4. Compute the *single-row* attention $q_{T+1}^\top K_\text{new}^\top / \sqrt{d_h}$, softmax-mix with $V_\text{new}$ to get the output.
5. Store $K_\text{new}, V_\text{new}$ as the cache for the next step.

No quadratic work over the past tokens. The "expensive" $Q K^\top$ matmul collapses to one query vector against the full $K$ matrix, $O(T d_h)$ per head.

## 3. Compute count per generated token

Per block, per generated token, the cost decomposes as:

| Op | FLOPs (1 batch, 1 step) |
|---|---|
| Project new token to $Q, K, V, $ output | $4 \cdot 2 d^2 = 8 d^2$ |
| Attention scores $q_{T+1}^\top K^\top$ (per head) | $h \cdot 2 d_h T = 2 d T$ |
| Mix $\text{softmax}(s) V$ (per head) | $h \cdot 2 T d_h = 2 d T$ |
| FFN | $16 d^2$ |
| **Total/block** | $\boxed{\ 24 d^2 + 4 d T \ }$ |

Multiply by $L$ blocks. Compare to **prefill** (one forward pass over a length-$T$ prompt, [6.12 §2.3](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.12-compute-and-memory)):

$$
\#\text{FLOPs}_\text{prefill/block} = 24 T d^2 + 4 T^2 d.
$$

Setting $T = 1$ in the prefill formula recovers the per-step count, confirming the cache is mathematically equivalent to "one forward pass over a 1-token sequence with a longer K, V context."

Generating $N$ tokens after a $T_p$-token prompt: prefill cost $24 T_p d^2 + 4 T_p^2 d$ once, then $N$ steps of $24 d^2 + 4 d (T_p + i)$ each. For $T_p = 2048, d = 4096, N = 200$:

- Prefill: $\approx 8.93 \times 10^{11}$ FLOPs per block.
- Per generation step: $\approx 4.0 \times 10^8 + 1.3 \times 10^7$ per block — three orders of magnitude smaller.

This is *why* "first token is slow, subsequent tokens are fast" is the dominant latency profile of LLM inference.

## 4. Memory: the KV cache size

The cache stores all $K$ and $V$ entries for *every layer*, *every head*, for the entire decoded sequence. Total size, in elements:

$$
\#\text{elements}_\text{KV} = 2 \cdot L \cdot h \cdot d_h \cdot T \cdot \text{batch}.
$$

(The 2 is for $K$ and $V$ together; $h \cdot d_h = d$.) In bytes, multiply by the bytes-per-element of the storage dtype (fp16: 2 bytes; fp8: 1 byte; int8: 1 byte).

$$
\boxed{\ \#\text{bytes}_\text{KV} = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot L \cdot d \cdot T \cdot \text{batch} = 4 \cdot L \cdot d \cdot T \cdot \text{batch}. \ }
$$

Numerical examples (single batch, fp16):

| Model | $L$ | $d$ | $T = 4{,}096$ | $T = 32{,}768$ | $T = 128{,}000$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| GPT-2 small | 12 | 768 | 0.15 GB | 1.2 GB | 4.7 GB |
| LLaMA-7B | 32 | 4096 | 2.1 GB | 17 GB | 67 GB |
| LLaMA-70B | 80 | 8192 | 11 GB | 86 GB | 336 GB |
| GPT-3 (175B) | 96 | 12288 | 19 GB | 154 GB | 605 GB |

At $T = 128{,}000$, the KV cache for LLaMA-70B is *larger than 4× H100 80GB GPUs combined*. This is the structural reason long-context inference is hard, and the motivation for everything in §5–§6.

## 5. Architectural variants that shrink the cache

The bytes-per-token of the KV cache is $4 L d$ in fp16 — the cache size of *one* token across all layers. Cutting this number is the central design lever of modern LLMs.

### 5.1 GQA: Grouped-Query Attention

**Ainslie, Shazeer, et al., 2023<FN n={2} />, *GQA: Training Generalized Multi-Query Transformer Models from Multi-Head Checkpoints*** ([arXiv:2305.13245](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13245)). Idea: keep $h$ *query* heads, but share $K, V$ across groups. With $g$ groups, you have $h/g$ K-heads and V-heads:

$$
\text{cache shrink factor} = \frac{g}{h}.
$$

For LLaMA-70B with $h = 64, g = 8$: cache is reduced by $8 / 64 = 1/8$. The 86 GB cache at 32k context drops to $\approx 11$ GB.

GQA is the dominant variant in modern open LLMs (LLaMA-2-70B, LLaMA-3, Mistral, Qwen). The extreme case $g = 1$ — every head shares one set of K, V — is **MQA** (Multi-Query Attention, Shazeer 2019)<FN n={1} />. MQA cuts the cache to $1 / h$ but loses some quality; GQA's "small $g$" recovers most of MHA's quality at a small fraction of the cache.

Full treatment in Cap 8.3; the right place to learn it is *after* this session, because the motivation is purely the cache size.

### 5.2 MLA: Multi-head Latent Attention

**DeepSeek-V2** (DeepSeek-AI, 2024, [arXiv:2405.04434](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04434))<FN n={3} />. A further compression: instead of storing $K, V$ directly, store a *latent* $c_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c}$ with $d_c \ll d$ per token; reconstruct $K, V$ via learned projections at attention time. The cache reduces from $2 L d$ per token to $L d_c$ per token, a $\sim 10\times$ shrink at iso-quality.

MLA powers DeepSeek-V2, V3, and R1. We will derive it in Cap 8.5; place it mentally as "the next step beyond GQA when the cache is still the bottleneck."

### 5.3 Quantising the cache

A blunter but effective tool: store the cache in **fp8 or int8** instead of fp16 — half the memory, with measurable but tolerable quality loss. Modern inference servers (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM) implement this. We discuss it in Cap 10.6 (Quantization).

### 5.4 Sliding-window inference

If the model uses sliding-window attention ([6.13 §2](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.13-long-context)), the KV cache only needs to hold the *last $w$ tokens*. The cache becomes a rolling buffer of constant size — **constant memory streaming generation**. Mistral 7B's 4k window cache, used at 32k context, occupies the same memory as the prompt-prefix cache: $\approx 0.5 \cdot 4096 / 32768 = 1/16$ of the full cache.

## 6. PagedAttention and continuous batching

Two further optimisations specific to *serving* LLMs to many users:

**PagedAttention** (Kwon et al., 2023<FN n={4} />, *Efficient Memory Management for Large Language Model Serving with PagedAttention*, [arXiv:2309.06180](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180)). Standard inference allocates the KV cache as one contiguous chunk per request — wasteful when requests have different lengths (you pre-allocate for the max). PagedAttention divides the cache into fixed-size "pages" (e.g., 16 tokens each) and a per-request page table, like an OS virtual-memory subsystem. Memory fragmentation drops by ~10×, allowing 2–4× more concurrent requests on the same hardware. This is the foundation of **vLLM** and most modern LLM-serving stacks. Full treatment in Cap 10.8.

**Continuous batching** (Yu et al., 2022, *Orca*)<FN n={5} />. Group requests with different ages into the same batch dynamically — when one request finishes, immediately add a new one without waiting for the whole batch to complete. Doubles throughput on real workloads. Also Cap 10.8.

Both rely on the KV cache existing as a separable per-request memory unit. The KV cache *is* the per-request state; everything else (model weights, activations during prefill) is shared.

## 7. Practical takeaways

Three things to remember:

1. **The KV cache is the dominant memory cost of long-context inference.** $4 L d T$ bytes in fp16. For $T \sim 100$k on a 70B-scale model, this hits hundreds of GB.
2. **The cache compute saving is large.** Per-token attention cost drops from $O((T+i)^2 d)$ to $O((T+i) d)$; per-token FFN cost drops from $O((T+i) d^2)$ to $O(d^2)$. Generation is ~$1000\times$ cheaper per token than prefill.
3. **Modern architectures (GQA, MLA) target the cache directly.** The $4 L d T$ formula has only $T$ as a fixed user-controlled term; cutting $L d$ at iso-quality is the central inference-side engineering challenge of 2024–2026.
