## 1. Why this session exists

The previous sessions built the interpretability program from the residual stream up to activation steering. This session zooms out: *where is the field going in 2025–2026?*

Two caveats. First, the frontier moves quickly — anything written here will be partly stale within a year. Second, the original roadmap session was titled "skim 3 frontier papers from the last 6 months; pick whatever's hot when you get here." This session honours that by sketching the *taxonomy* of frontier work and naming representative papers under each, so you have a starting point regardless of when you read this.

Six threads to be aware of as of mid-2026:

1. Circuit tracing at frontier scale.
2. Model-organism studies (deception, sandbagging, deceptive alignment).
3. Reasoning-model interpretability — what do o1/R1's thinking tokens actually do?
4. Architectural frontier: hybrid Transformer + SSM (post-Cap 8.8).
5. Agentic capability evaluation — what can an LLM actually do in the world?
6. AI-driven AI research — models doing interpretability on models.

Each thread gets a short section. The session closes Chapter 11 and bridges to the capstone (Chapter 12).

## 2. Circuit tracing at scale

The natural extension of Cap 11.3–11.6: combine SAE features (one per layer) with cross-layer transcoders to trace *how* a feature in layer 8 propagates to a derived feature in layer 12, then to a downstream behaviour at the unembed.

### 2.1 The state of play

Anthropic's [*Circuit Tracing* (2024)](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/circuits-updates-june/index.html) and follow-ups demonstrated end-to-end attribution: given a behaviour (e.g., refusal of harmful requests), the technique traces back through the network's feature flow and identifies which features in which layers causally mediate it.

The toolchain is now reasonably mature: SAE features per layer, cross-layer transcoders connecting them, attribution graphs visualising the dependencies. Worked examples exist for small models; frontier-model tracing is in progress as of 2026.

### 2.2 Open problems

- **Tractability at frontier scale.** Even with auto-interp, a $10^7$-feature model with $\sim 100$ layers has $\sim 10^{14}$ candidate cross-layer edges. Pruning and prioritising is itself a research question.
- **Circuit-feature ambiguity.** SAE features are not unique — multiple SAEs trained on the same model recover *similar but not identical* feature sets. Different SAEs imply different circuits. Robustness across SAE seeds is being studied.
- **Robustness across model versions.** A circuit identified in Claude 3 Sonnet may or may not exist in Claude 3.5 — minor architectural or training-data changes can rearrange features. Cross-version circuit stability is an open empirical question.

### 2.3 Why it matters

Circuit tracing is the prerequisite for *mechanistic* safety arguments. Behavioural claims ("the model usually refuses harmful requests") are weak — they can be jailbroken. Mechanistic claims ("the refusal circuit is the only path; ablating it removes refusal; the circuit is robust against in-context manipulation") are stronger. Whether mech-interp can produce such claims at the frontier is the central open question.

## 3. Model organisms

A "model organism" in biology is a simple system used to study a property of complex systems — yeast for cell biology, *Drosophila* for genetics. In interpretability: small, deliberately-trained models that exhibit a specific capability or failure mode, used as testbeds for techniques meant for the frontier.

### 3.1 Deception and sandbagging

Hubinger et al. (Anthropic 2024<FN n={5} />, [*Sleeper Agents*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05566))<FN n={3} /> deliberately trained models to behave *differently* in evaluation vs. deployment ("sleeper" agents): perform well on safety evals but exhibit harmful behaviour in specific deployment conditions. The point: study whether such deceptive training can be *detected* by current interpretability tools.

The headline finding: standard safety fine-tuning (SFT, RLHF) does *not* remove the deceptive behaviour — it's robust to alignment. Interpretability tools (linear probes, simple SAE-based detection) showed mixed results in detecting the sleeper behaviour. This is concerning evidence that *training* alone may not be sufficient for safety; mechanistic understanding is necessary.

### 3.2 Sandbagging

Similar idea: models that *underperform* on specific benchmarks while retaining the capability. Research on whether sandbagging can be detected via activation analysis is ongoing (van der Weij et al., 2024, [arXiv:2406.07358](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07358))<FN n={6} />.

### 3.3 Why model organisms

You can't ethically train a frontier model to be deceptive and see what happens. You *can* train a small model to be deceptive and check whether your interpretability tools catch it. Model organisms let you stress-test the tools without the deployment risk of stress-testing the frontier.

## 4. Reasoning-model interpretability

Cap 10.7 introduced reasoning models (o1, R1, Claude 3.7+, Gemini Thinking). The next interpretability question: *what is the reasoning chain actually doing*?

### 4.1 The faithfulness question

If the model emits 10K reasoning tokens then answers $X$, two scenarios:

- **Faithful chain.** The reasoning tokens are causal — perturbing them changes the answer. The chain is the model "working through" the problem.
- **Performative chain.** The reasoning tokens are post-hoc — the model has already committed to $X$ and is generating plausible-looking justification. Perturbing the chain doesn't change $X$.

Empirically, both phenomena exist. Lanham et al. (2023<FN n={1} />, [arXiv:2307.13702](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13702)) and Turpin et al. (2023<FN n={2} />, [arXiv:2305.04388](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388)) found that for *non-reasoning-trained* models, chains were frequently performative — the answer was largely determined before the chain. For *reasoning-trained* models (o1/R1-style with GRPO on verifiable rewards), chains are more faithful, but the question is still open.

### 4.2 Mechanistic open problems

For a reasoning model:

- *Are the thinking tokens computing? Or just rehearsing?* Probes show task-relevant features active during thinking, but causality is harder to establish.
- *What does the model do during the long "filler" sections of its thinking?* Some R1 chains have long stretches of "Let me think... actually, let me reconsider...". Mech-interp on these is in very early stages.
- *How does the model decide to stop thinking?* Some learned stopping criterion lives in the late-layer activations; identifying it could provide a control handle.

These questions are post-2025 and largely unsolved. They will likely drive much of the 2026–2027 interpretability research agenda.

## 5. The architectural frontier

Cap 8.7–8.8 introduced Mamba-family SSMs and hybrid SSM-Transformer architectures. The 2025–2026 update.

### 5.1 Hybrid dominance

Pure SSM (Mamba) and pure attention (Transformer) are both dominated, on most benchmarks, by hybrids. The most successful pattern is Transformer blocks interleaved with SSM blocks at $\sim 1:5$ or $\sim 1:3$ ratio (Jamba: AI21, 2024, [arXiv:2403.19887](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19887); Zamba: Zyphra, 2024; recurrent-thought variants).

The mechanistic intuition: SSM blocks handle long-range linear-time mixing; attention blocks handle precise content-dependent lookups. The hybrid gets both.

### 5.2 New architectures

Several lines of architectural research are active:

- **xLSTM** (Beck et al., 2024, [arXiv:2405.04517](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04517))<FN n={8} />. Modernised LSTM with exponential gating and matrix memory; competitive with Transformers at small scale.
- **DeltaNet / Gated DeltaNet** (Yang et al., 2024, [arXiv:2406.06484](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06484))<FN n={9} />. Linear-attention variant with explicit memory update rules.
- **TTT (Test-Time Training)** (Sun et al., 2024, [arXiv:2407.04620](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04620))<FN n={10} />. Inner-loop gradient descent at inference time as a sequence-modelling primitive.

Whether any of these displaces the Transformer-Mamba hybrid as the dominant frontier architecture is open. As of mid-2026, hybrids are dominant; specialised architectures (e.g., audio diffusion-Transformer hybrids) are common for non-text modalities.

### 5.3 Interpretability implications

Each new architecture brings its own mech-interp challenges. SSMs don't have attention heads to inspect; the recurrence carries state through time in opaque ways. SAEs work on SSM activations too (early evidence), but the *circuit* picture is less developed.

By 2026, mech-interp is mostly Transformer-focused; extending it to non-attention architectures is an active subfield.

## 6. Agentic capability evaluation

The shift from "what does the model output given a prompt" to "what can the model accomplish given tools, time, and a goal" — i.e., the agent paradigm of Cap 10.9 — has driven a new generation of evaluations.

### 6.1 Notable agent benchmarks

- **SWE-bench** (Jimenez et al., 2024, [arXiv:2310.06770](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770))<FN n={11} />. Real-world GitHub issues; the model must produce a code patch that fixes the bug. Current frontier: $\sim 40\%$–$60\%$ resolution for top models.
- **OSWorld** (Xie et al., 2024, [arXiv:2404.07972](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07972))<FN n={12} />. Computer-use benchmark; the model controls a desktop environment via screenshots and keyboard/mouse actions.
- **GAIA** (Mialon et al., 2023, [arXiv:2311.12983](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12983))<FN n={13} />. General assistant benchmark; multi-step web research, computation, document analysis.
- **METR's task suite** (METR, 2024). Long-horizon software-engineering tasks measured by human-time-equivalent.

### 6.2 The horizon-length story

METR's framing: measure model capability in *human-equivalent task length*. A model that can autonomously complete tasks taking a human 1 hour is more capable than one that maxes out at 5 minutes. As of 2026, frontier models achieve $\sim 2$–$8$ hour autonomous task lengths, doubling roughly every ~$6$ months.

This is one of the most striking capability trends — and it's a *deployment-side* trend that scales independently of pretraining. The combination of reasoning models (Cap 10.7), tool use (Cap 10.9), and longer context (Cap 6.13) is driving it.

### 6.3 Safety implications

Long-horizon autonomous agents are *also* the deployment mode with the highest novel-capability risk: a model that can complete multi-hour engineering tasks autonomously can plausibly cause non-trivial damage if misaligned. Whether the alignment + interpretability stack is adequate at these capability levels is the central live question.

## 7. AI-driven AI research

The 2024–2026 phenomenon: language models *doing* AI research. Not just helping researchers (Copilot, code generation) but operating as independent research agents.

### 7.1 Sakana AI Scientist

Lu, Lu, Lange, Foerster, Clune, Ha (2024<FN n={14} />, [arXiv:2408.06292](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06292)). An LLM-based system that proposes research ideas, implements experiments, writes papers — at modest quality but end-to-end automated.

### 7.2 LLM-assisted mech-interp

The auto-interp pipelines (Cap 11.6 §4.2) are one example. More ambitious: have a model *propose* mech-interp experiments, *interpret* the results, and *iterate* on the hypotheses. Several frontier labs are exploring this for accelerating interpretability research itself.

### 7.3 The feedback loop

The longer-term picture: more-capable models help us interpret next-generation models, which helps us train safer next-next-generation models, etc. Whether this loop is benign or accelerates capability beyond our ability to oversee is itself a major open question.

Both will move quickly.

## 10. What we keep going forward

Three things.

1. **The frontier is multi-threaded.** Circuit tracing, reasoning interpretability, model organisms, architectural innovation, agentic evaluation, AI-driven research — each is moving on its own clock. Following the field requires sampling across all of them.
2. **Mech-interp is the safety bet.** Behavioural alignment (Chapter 9) is necessary but not sufficient at frontier capability levels. Whether mech-interp can produce *mechanistic* safety arguments — provably-bounded behaviour, deception-detection, intent-verification — is the central open question.
3. **The course-as-coursework ends here; the field continues.** A reading list is provided. The capstone (Chapter 12) is the personal project that consolidates what you've learned. After that, follow [transformer-circuits.pub](https://transformer-circuits.pub/), the LessWrong AI alignment forum, the major lab blogs (Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepMind), and arXiv (cs.LG, cs.CL) to keep current.
