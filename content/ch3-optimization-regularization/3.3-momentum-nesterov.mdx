## 1. Why this session exists

Plain SGD treats every gradient as a fresh truth: $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \alpha g_t$. If the loss surface is an elongated valley — large curvature in some directions, small in others — SGD oscillates across the steep walls and crawls down the gentle floor. The fix is one of the oldest ideas in numerical optimisation: don't move along the instantaneous gradient, move along an *average* of recent gradients.

This is **momentum**. It uses the EMA of §3.2 directly. Nesterov's variant adds one cosmetic-looking but provably better twist: evaluate the gradient at the *look-ahead* point.

---

## 2. The classical momentum update

Maintain a velocity $v_t \in \mathbb{R}^p$, initialised to $v_0 = 0$. At each step:

$$
\boxed{\
\begin{aligned}
v_t &= \beta\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta)\, g_t, \\
\theta_{t+1} &= \theta_t - \alpha\, v_t.
\end{aligned}
\ }
$$

This is exactly the EMA of §3.2 applied to the gradient, followed by a step in $-v_t$. Typical $\beta = 0.9$, so $v_t$ averages the last $\sim 10$ gradients.

<Aside type="note">
You will also see the formulation $v_t = \beta v_{t-1} + g_t$ (without the $1 - \beta$ factor) and $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \alpha v_t$. The two forms are equivalent up to a rescaling of the learning rate: with the un-normalised form, the effective LR is $\alpha / (1 - \beta)$. PyTorch's `SGD(momentum=β)` actually uses the un-normalised form. Adam will use the normalised one. Read the docstring of any optimiser you depend on.
</Aside>

## 3. Why this helps: the elongated-valley intuition

Consider the quadratic $L(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}(\lambda_1 \theta_1^2 + \lambda_2 \theta_2^2)$ with $\lambda_1 \gg \lambda_2$ — a long, narrow valley along the $\theta_2$ axis. The gradient is $g = (\lambda_1 \theta_1, \lambda_2 \theta_2)$.

**Plain SGD.** Step is $-\alpha g$. To make progress along the slow direction ($\theta_2$), $\alpha$ has to be large. But large $\alpha$ in the fast direction ($\theta_1$) overshoots: $\theta_1^{(t+1)} = (1 - \alpha \lambda_1) \theta_1^{(t)}$ flips sign if $\alpha \lambda_1 > 1$, oscillating. For stable progress in $\theta_1$, $\alpha < 2/\lambda_1$; in $\theta_2$, the effective rate is then $\alpha \lambda_2 = \lambda_2 / \lambda_1$ — tiny. The condition number $\kappa = \lambda_1 / \lambda_2$ caps how fast SGD can move.

**With momentum.** Along $\theta_1$ the gradient alternates sign on consecutive steps (because of the oscillation). The EMA averages those out — successive contributions cancel. Along $\theta_2$ the gradient has consistent sign, so the EMA *accumulates*. Net effect: the optimiser damps motion across the steep walls and accelerates along the slow floor.

Quantitatively (Polyak, 1964; proof on a quadratic)<FN n={1} />, the optimal momentum coefficient is

$$
\beta^\star = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa} - 1}{\sqrt{\kappa} + 1}\right)^2,
$$

and the convergence rate improves from $1 - 1/\kappa$ (plain GD) to $1 - 1/\sqrt{\kappa}$ (momentum). A **square-root speedup** in condition number. For $\kappa = 10^4$ (entirely realistic for deep nets), that is a $100\times$ acceleration on the worst directions.<FN ns={[3, 4]} />

## 4. The damped-oscillator analogy

Polyak called this method **heavy-ball**. The update

$$
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \alpha\, g_t + \beta\,(\theta_t - \theta_{t-1})
$$

(the un-normalised form, after substituting $v_t$) reads like the discretisation of the second-order ODE

$$
m \ddot\theta + \gamma \dot\theta + \nabla L(\theta) = 0,
$$

i.e. a ball with mass $m$ rolling on the loss surface, subject to friction $\gamma$. The mass smooths short-term gradient fluctuations (high curvature directions); friction prevents endless oscillation. The discrete coefficients $(\alpha, \beta)$ correspond to a time-step and mass/friction ratio.

For Luan: this is the same kind of damped-oscillator analysis you do in classical mechanics. The "tuning" of $(\alpha, \beta)$ is the same as picking a critically-damped friction in a mass-spring system — you want enough damping to avoid ringing but not so much that the ball gets stuck.

## 5. Nesterov accelerated gradient

Nesterov (1983)<FN n={2} /> noticed: if we are about to move to $\theta_t - \alpha \beta v_{t-1}$ anyway (because of momentum), then the *next* gradient we care about is the one *there*, not the one at $\theta_t$. Evaluate the gradient at the look-ahead point.

$$
\boxed{\
\begin{aligned}
\tilde\theta_t &= \theta_t - \alpha\, \beta\, v_{t-1}, \\
v_t &= \beta\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta)\, \nabla L(\tilde\theta_t), \\
\theta_{t+1} &= \theta_t - \alpha\, v_t.
\end{aligned}
\ }
$$

The gradient is evaluated after taking a "momentum-only" partial step. Intuition: it's the difference between *driving toward a wall and braking after you hit it* versus *seeing the wall coming and starting to brake earlier*. The correction is small per step but compounds.

For convex smooth problems, Nesterov's method achieves the *optimal* rate $O(1/T^2)$ for first-order methods — provably faster than plain GD's $O(1/T)$ on the same class. Deep nets aren't convex, but Nesterov still tends to outperform classical momentum on many problems and is the default in some frameworks (`torch.optim.SGD(nesterov=True)`).

---

## 6. Implementation note: the "look-ahead trick"

The form in §5 is awkward to implement because $\tilde \theta_t$ is not stored. A standard reparametrisation (Sutskever et al., 2013) keeps the iterate equal to $\tilde\theta_t$ at all times, so the gradient is always evaluated at the iterate. Define $\theta'_t := \theta_t - \alpha \beta v_{t-1}$. Then the update can be rewritten as

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_t &= \beta\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \beta)\, \nabla L(\theta'_t), \\
\theta'_{t+1} &= \theta'_t - \alpha\bigl(\beta v_t + (1 - \beta) \nabla L(\theta'_t)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$

This is what most deep-learning frameworks actually compute. No need to remember the previous $\theta$ — the bookkeeping lives entirely in $v$.

---

## 7. What momentum buys in deep learning

Three things, in practice.

**(a) Better conditioning for free.** Deep-net Hessians are notoriously ill-conditioned. Momentum's $\sqrt{\kappa}$ rate is a real win.

**(b) Variance reduction in mini-batch noise.** The EMA averages out short-scale mini-batch noise (§3.1), so the optimiser sees a smoother trajectory than the raw gradient stream. This is one of the reasons momentum + SGD is competitive with adaptive methods on vision tasks.

**(c) Cheap.** One extra vector of memory (the same shape as $\theta$). One extra FMA per parameter per step. Negligible compared to the forward/backward cost.

Modern LLMs use Adam (§3.5), which is momentum + an adaptive per-parameter scale. Vision models often still use plain SGD with momentum because it generalises slightly better empirically. Both stories are real.

---

## 8. Takeaways

- Momentum is just an EMA of gradients used as the descent direction.
- On an elongated valley, it damps oscillations in steep directions and accumulates motion in shallow ones.
- Optimal convergence rate on quadratics: $1 - 1/\sqrt{\kappa}$ vs $1 - 1/\kappa$ for GD — a square-root improvement in condition number.
- Nesterov's look-ahead variant evaluates the gradient at $\theta_t - \alpha \beta v_{t-1}$; the same EMA but on the look-ahead point.
- Implementation: PyTorch's `SGD(momentum=β, nesterov=True)` ships both.
