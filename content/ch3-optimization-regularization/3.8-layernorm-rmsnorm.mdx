## 1. Why this session exists

Every modern Transformer uses **LayerNorm**, not BatchNorm. RMSNorm — a stripped-down LayerNorm — is the version in LLaMA, Mistral, and most LLMs from 2023 onward. The reason is structural: BN's dependence on the batch breaks down for variable-length sequences and small per-GPU batches, both of which are the norm in Transformer training. LayerNorm sidesteps the issue by normalising **across the feature dimension** for each individual token, with no batch coupling at all.

Past this point, every architecture choice we make for LLMs builds on LayerNorm / RMSNorm. (If you want a rigorous treatment of BatchNorm first — useful context but not required here — see [Appendix 3.C](/session/ch3-optimization-regularization/3.C-batchnorm).)<FN n={3} />

---

## 2. The LayerNorm operation

Take a single hidden vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (typically: the activation at one token position, in a single example, at one layer). Compute the **per-vector** mean and variance:

$$
\mu = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{k=1}^d x_k, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{k=1}^d (x_k - \mu)^2.
$$

Normalise:

$$
\hat x_k = \frac{x_k - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \varepsilon}},
$$

then apply a learnable per-coordinate affine:

$$
\boxed{\ y_k = \gamma_k\, \hat x_k + \beta_k.\ }
$$

The trainable parameters are $\gamma, \beta \in \mathbb{R}^d$, one $(\gamma, \beta)$ pair per *feature*. The statistics $\mu, \sigma^2$ are computed *per token* by averaging over the $d$ features of that token's hidden vector.

That's it. There's no batch in the formula. Same operation at train and inference time. No running EMAs needed.

---

## 3. The single picture: BN vs LN

Take a 3-D activation tensor $A \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T \times d}$ — batch $\times$ sequence-length $\times$ hidden-dim. The two normalisations operate along *different* axes:

- **BatchNorm:** compute $\mu, \sigma^2$ for each *feature coordinate*, averaging over $B \times T$ (batch and time together) → one $(\mu, \sigma^2)$ per of the $d$ features. The normalised value at position $(b, t, k)$ uses statistics that depend on every $(b', t')$ for that $k$.
- **LayerNorm:** compute $\mu, \sigma^2$ for each *position*, averaging over the $d$ features → one $(\mu, \sigma^2)$ per of the $B \times T$ tokens. The normalised value at $(b, t, k)$ uses statistics that depend only on $(b, t)$.

Same trainable $\gamma, \beta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ in both cases. Different axis being averaged.

---

## 4. Why LN beats BN for Transformers

Three reasons, in order of importance:

**(a) Variable sequence length.** A Transformer batch contains sequences of different lengths, padded. BN would compute statistics over the *padded* tokens, which are garbage. LN computes statistics *per token*, so each token's normalisation is unaffected by what's in the rest of the batch.

**(b) Small effective batches.** Cutting-edge LLM training distributes a global batch of, say, 4M tokens across thousands of GPUs. Per-GPU batch size in tokens-per-update can be tiny (1–4 sequences). BN's $\mu_\mathcal{B}, \sigma_\mathcal{B}^2$ are very noisy at small $B$, and the EMA estimates at inference get correspondingly noisy. LN doesn't see the batch dimension at all — it works identically at $B = 1$ as at $B = 1000$.

**(c) Causality.** In autoregressive language modelling, the prediction at token $t$ may only depend on tokens $1, \dots, t$. BN's per-feature mean involves *all* time steps, so token $t$'s normalisation depends on tokens $> t$ — a *leak* from the future. LN's per-token statistic is purely local in time and respects causality automatically.

For these reasons, every Transformer (encoder, decoder, encoder–decoder) since *Attention Is All You Need* uses LayerNorm.

<Aside type="history">
Ba, Kiros & Hinton (2016<FN n={1} />, *Layer Normalization*) proposed LN as a fix for *recurrent* networks, where BN's batch dependence was a problem because RNN gradients couple time steps non-trivially. The Transformer paper picked it up the next year and froze it as the canonical normalisation for sequence models.
</Aside>

## 5. Backward pass

Like BN, LN's backward couples the normalised entries — but it couples them along the *feature* axis (one token's $d$ features) rather than the batch axis. The structure of the formula is identical with $B$ replaced by $d$ and the sum over the batch replaced by a sum over features:

$$
\boxed{\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial x_k} = \frac{1}{d \cdot \sqrt{\sigma^2 + \varepsilon}}\left[d \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat x_k} - \sum_{j=1}^d \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat x_j} - \hat x_k \sum_{j=1}^d \hat x_j \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat x_j}\right]. \ }
$$

The sum is over features of a single token. This means LN's backward parallelises beautifully across tokens (no cross-batch sum), which is why it scales cleanly on multi-GPU systems.

---

## 6. RMSNorm: the modern stripped-down version

Zhang & Sennrich (2019)<FN n={2} /> noticed that the *mean-centring* of LN can be removed without hurting performance, leaving just the rescaling by RMS:

$$
\boxed{\ \operatorname{RMSNorm}(x)_k = \gamma_k \cdot \frac{x_k}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^d x_j^2 + \varepsilon}}. \ }
$$

No $\mu$, no $\beta$. Just a per-vector rescaling by the root-mean-square of the features. Saves one mean computation, one subtraction, and one bias parameter per coordinate — about 7% of the parameters compared to LN.

LLaMA 1/2/3, Mistral, Qwen, Gemini, Claude all use RMSNorm. The empirical case for it is that it trains slightly faster than LN with no quality regression. The theoretical case (Zhang & Sennrich): re-centring is doing little useful work in a deep network where the activations are already roughly centred by the previous layer's normalisation; the rescaling is what matters.

For the rest of this course, when we say "LayerNorm" we mean the LN of Ba et al.; when we discuss modern LLM implementations specifically we will use RMSNorm.

---

## 7. Pre-norm vs post-norm

Where do you put the LN inside a Transformer block? Two conventions:

- **Post-norm** (original Transformer, *Attention Is All You Need*): $y = \operatorname{LN}(x + f(x))$. Apply the residual, then normalise.
- **Pre-norm** (GPT-2 and everything since): $y = x + f(\operatorname{LN}(x))$. Normalise the input to the sublayer, then add the residual.

Pre-norm is now standard. The reason is that pre-norm leaves a clean *additive residual stream* with no normalisation between layers — gradients flow through the residual with no bottleneck. Post-norm sandwiches each residual addition in an LN, which empirically makes very deep training less stable.

We'll do this in detail in Chapter 6 (Transformers). For now: modern Transformers are **pre-norm + RMSNorm**.

## 8. The whole course's normalisation menu

For reference, the four normalisations we will eventually see in code:

| Name | Average over | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| BatchNorm | batch $\times$ spatial | ConvNets (ResNet, EfficientNet) |
| LayerNorm | features (per token) | Transformers (BERT, GPT-2/3, T5) |
| RMSNorm | features (per token), no mean | Modern LLMs (LLaMA, Mistral, Gemini) |
| GroupNorm | groups of features | Vision, when batch is too small for BN |

Pick the one whose averaging axis is independent of the dimension you don't have stable statistics for. Transformers don't have stable batch statistics → average over features. ConvNets do have stable batch statistics → average over batch. The right normalisation falls out of the data structure.

---

## 9. Takeaways

- LayerNorm normalises *per token, across the feature dimension*. No batch coupling.
- Same operation at train and test time; no EMAs needed.
- Why Transformers use it: variable sequence length, small effective batches, causal correctness.
- RMSNorm = LN without the mean-centring; what every recent LLM uses.
- Pre-norm placement ($\operatorname{LN}(x)$ inside the sublayer, residual outside) is the modern convention.
- Backward: same structure as BN's but summed over features instead of batch.

## 10. End-of-chapter recap

This chapter built the optimiser and regulariser stack used by every modern LLM training run. Main path:

- **3.1** SGD with mini-batches gives an unbiased gradient estimator; variance scales as $\Sigma/B$.
- **3.2** EMAs give low-variance estimates with bias correction $1/(1-\beta^t)$.
- **3.3** Momentum is just an EMA of gradients — Polyak's $\sqrt{\kappa}$ speedup.
- **3.4** AdaGrad / RMSProp = (cumulative or EMA) of $g^2$, used as a per-parameter rescaling.
- **3.5** Adam = momentum + RMSProp + bias correction.
- **3.6** AdamW = Adam with decoupled weight decay (every LLM uses this).
- **3.7** Schedule = linear warmup + cosine decay (or WSD for extendability) + global-norm gradient clipping at $c = 1.0$.
- **3.8** LayerNorm and RMSNorm = per-token mean/var (or RMS) normalisation over features; the Transformer choice.

Optional appendices, for cultural completeness — none block Chapters 4–12:

- [**3.A** L2 weight decay = Gaussian prior MAP](/session/ch3-optimization-regularization/3.A-l2-map). $\lambda = 1/\tau^2$. The Bayesian reading of the regulariser already used in 3.6.
- [**3.B** Dropout](/session/ch3-optimization-regularization/3.B-dropout) = approximate ensemble of $2^N$ sub-networks; $1/(1-p)$ scaling. Rare in modern LLM *pre-training* (dropout = 0); still used in fine-tuning small models.
- [**3.C** BatchNorm](/session/ch3-optimization-regularization/3.C-batchnorm) = per-feature mean/var normalisation over the batch. Standard in CNNs; *not* used in Transformers (LN/RMSNorm wins for the reasons in §4 above).

Chapter 4 will use the main-path stack to revisit CNNs and then bridge to sequences (Chapter 5) and attention (Chapter 6).
