## 1. Why this session exists

Chapter 7 derived modern pretraining from scratch — data, tokenization, precision, parallelism, scaling laws. To anchor it historically, we read the paper that introduced the era: **Brown, Mann, Ryder, et al., 2020, *Language Models are Few-Shot Learners*** ([arXiv:2005.14165](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)). The GPT-3 paper.

GPT-3 was the watershed. Before: language models were research artefacts. After: they were $100M-training-budget products that did few-shot in-context learning, code generation, translation, math (poorly), reasoning (poorly), and many other things that previously required task-specific fine-tuned models. The paper documents an architecture that is, by 2026 standards, *the same as today's*; the difference is scale, data, and training discipline.

This session: read §2 (Model and Training) section-by-section, identify each design choice in our framework, and find three statements that now feel obvious. Pair this with the [AIAYN read of 6.11](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.11-transformer-block-aiayn): together they bracket the period from "the architecture works" (2017) to "the architecture is enough at scale" (2020).

---

## 2. The paper in context

GPT-3 (June 2020) sits between:

- **GPT-2** (Feb 2019): same architecture, smaller scale (1.5B params), trained on WebText (~10B tokens). Showed "unsupervised multi-task learning" — a language model can do summarisation, translation, QA *implicitly*, with no task-specific head.
- **Chinchilla** (March 2022): the scaling-law correction. Showed GPT-3 was under-trained.

GPT-3's experimental setup:

- **175B parameters**: $L = 96$ blocks, $h = 96$ heads, $d = 12{,}288$.
- **300B tokens** of training data: a mixture of CommonCrawl-filtered (60%), WebText2 (22%), Books1+Books2 (16%), Wikipedia (3%).
- **Compute**: ~$3 \cdot 10^{23}$ FLOPs total. Roughly $3000$ V100-GPU-years.
- **No fine-tuning.** The paper's central claim is *few-shot prompting*: at this scale, the base LM does in-context learning *without* explicit instruction tuning.

The combination — biggest-ever model, biggest-ever data run, no task-specific finetuning, capable of broad task performance from prompts — was the moment LLMs went from "research toy" to "general-purpose substrate".

## 3. The architecture (§2.1)

Open the paper to §2.1, "Model and Architectures." The text is short — three paragraphs. Match each sentence to what we have built.

### 3.1 What §2.1 says, decoded

> "We use the same model and architecture as GPT-2, including the modified initialization, pre-normalization, and reversible tokenization described therein..."

Decoded: pre-LN block ([6.10](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.10-pre-vs-post-norm)). Byte-level BPE tokenizer ([7.2 §5](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.2-bpe-tokenization)). Standard initialization scaled by $1/\sqrt{N}$ (a per-residual-branch trick from GPT-2's *Improving Language Understanding* paper).

> "...with the exception that we use alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention patterns in the layers of the transformer, similar to the Sparse Transformer."

This is the *one* architectural diff from GPT-2: alternating layers use *sparse* attention patterns (Child et al., 2019, Sparse Transformer) to reduce compute. In retrospect, this didn't catch on — later models (LLaMA, etc.) reverted to dense attention everywhere. Sparse Transformer's "fixed pattern" was supplanted by sliding-window and the FlashAttention efficiency story.

> "To study the dependence of ML performance on model size, we train 8 different sizes of model..."

This is *the* methodological move: train at 8 sizes from 125M to 175B, plot loss against compute, fit power laws. Kaplan-style scaling-law study ([7.11](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.11-scaling-laws-kaplan)). Cf. Chinchilla's later three-method study with corrections.

### 3.2 Table 2.1: the model sizes

The famous table:

| Model | $N$ | $L$ | $d$ | $h$ | $d_h$ | $d_\text{ff}/d$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| GPT-3-125M | 125M | 12 | 768 | 12 | 64 | 4 |
| GPT-3-350M | 350M | 24 | 1024 | 16 | 64 | 4 |
| GPT-3-760M | 760M | 24 | 1536 | 16 | 96 | 4 |
| GPT-3-1.3B | 1.3B | 24 | 2048 | 24 | 128 | 4 |
| GPT-3-2.7B | 2.7B | 32 | 2560 | 32 | 80 | 4 |
| GPT-3-6.7B (curie) | 6.7B | 32 | 4096 | 32 | 128 | 4 |
| GPT-3-13B | 13B | 40 | 5140 | 40 | 128 | 4 |
| GPT-3-175B (davinci) | 175B | 96 | 12288 | 96 | 128 | 4 |

This table is a *parameter sweep* — same architectural ratios ($d_\text{ff}/d = 4$, $d_h = 64$ or $128$), only $L$ and $d$ varied. Notice:

- **Both $L$ and $d$ scale up** as $N$ grows, in roughly fixed proportion.
- **$d_h$ is held at 128** for the largest models — a hint at the empirical rule "head dim should be $\sim 128$, scale $h$ to match $d$" that LLaMA and others adopted.
- **$d_\text{ff} = 4d$** universally, as predicted by [6.8 §3](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.8-ffn).

This table is *the* GPT-3 family chart; every later "family" (LLaMA, Mistral, Qwen) follows the same template.

## 4. The training data (§2.2)

> "Datasets for language models have rapidly expanded, culminating in the Common Crawl dataset constituting nearly a trillion words. ..."

GPT-3's training data, by token weight:

| Source | Quantity | Weight in training |
|---|---:|---:|
| CommonCrawl (filtered) | 410B | 60% |
| WebText2 | 19B | 22% |
| Books1 | 12B | 8% |
| Books2 | 55B | 8% |
| Wikipedia | 3B | 3% |

Notice the disparity: CommonCrawl is the largest source but Books1 (smaller) is sampled with comparable weight per token — i.e., Books1 is *repeated more*. Modern recipes ([7.5 §2.4](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.5-data-mixing-curriculum)) systematise this.

§2.2 describes the filtering pipeline:

> "(1) we downloaded and filtered a version of CommonCrawl based on similarity to a range of high-quality reference corpora,
> (2) we performed fuzzy deduplication at the document level..."

This is exactly the CCNet + MinHash pipeline of [7.1 §3](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.1-pretraining-data). GPT-3 was an early adopter; later corpora (DCLM, FineWeb-Edu) extended the methodology with better filters and more aggressive deduplication.

### 4.1 The tokens-per-parameter ratio

GPT-3-175B was trained on 300B tokens. Ratio: $D/N \approx 1.7$. **Far below Chinchilla's prescription of $\sim 20$**, and even below Kaplan's prescription of $\sim 7$.

Why? Kaplan's analysis ([7.11 §4.1](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.11-scaling-laws-kaplan)) gave $N^* \propto C^{0.73}$, $D^* \propto C^{0.27}$ — heavily favouring $N$ at large $C$. GPT-3 implemented this. We now know ([7.12](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.12-chinchilla)) that this is the wrong direction.

A redo at the same compute: $C = 3 \cdot 10^{23}$ FLOPs. Chinchilla optimum: $N^* \approx \sqrt{C/120} \approx 5 \cdot 10^{10}$, $D^* \approx 10^{12}$. So **a 50B-parameter model trained on 1T tokens** would have *out-performed* GPT-3-175B at the same compute. The 70B Chinchilla model and the LLaMA-1-65B (both at ~$D/N = 20$) — both at less than half GPT-3's parameter count — beat GPT-3 on most benchmarks.

GPT-3 is the historical *demonstration* of the scaling thesis; it is also the historical *example* of getting the allocation wrong.

## 5. The training process (§2.3)

GPT-3's training mechanics. Match each to our toolkit.

> "We use the same model and architecture as GPT-2 [...], with the same modified initialization, pre-normalization, and reversible tokenization, with the exception that..."

(See §3.1.)

> "All models use a context window of $n_\text{ctx} = 2048$ tokens."

$T_\text{train} = 2048$. Context-extension via RoPE-scaling ([7.13](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.13-long-context-training)) didn't exist yet — GPT-3 has *learned absolute positional embeddings* ([Appendix 6.B](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.B-learned-pe)), and is hard-capped at $T_\text{ctx} = 2048$. (Years later, RoPE-based descendants of this lineage push to $128k$+.)

> "Adam with $\beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.95, \epsilon = 10^{-8}$."

Standard Adam ([3.5](/session/ch3-optimization-regularization/3.5-adam)) with the canonical hyperparameters that have remained essentially unchanged in 5 years.

> "We clip the global norm of the gradient at 1.0."

Gradient clipping at $1.0$ ([3.7 §3](/session/ch3-optimization-regularization/3.7-lr-schedules-and-clipping)).

> "Cosine decay for learning rate down to 10% of its value, over 260 billion tokens..."

Cosine LR schedule, decaying to $10\%$ of peak ([3.7 §3](/session/ch3-optimization-regularization/3.7-lr-schedules-and-clipping)). Note: GPT-3 *did not* use the WSD schedule of MiniCPM ([7.5 §4](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.5-data-mixing-curriculum)) — that came later. Cosine to $10\%$ was the 2020 standard.

> "We use a batch size of $3.2 \cdot 10^6$ tokens for the 175B model."

3.2M tokens per step (cf. modern $4$–$16$M-token steps). This is the *peak* of GPT-3's batch ramp:

> "The batch size is increased linearly from a small value (32k tokens) to the full value over the first 4-12 billion tokens of training..."

Batch ramp from $32$k tokens to $3.2$M, over the first $4$–$12$B tokens. Match this to the critical-batch-size argument ([7.11 §5](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.11-scaling-laws-kaplan)): $B^*$ grows as training proceeds, so small early batches are sufficient and save compute.

> "We always train on sequences of the full $n_\text{ctx} = 2048$ token context window, packing multiple documents into a single sequence when documents are shorter than 2048 tokens, in order to increase computational efficiency."

Document packing (later codified as "packed dataset" by various frameworks). This is how modern training avoids wasting compute on padded short documents.

> "We use a mixture of model parallelism within each matrix multiply and model parallelism across the layers of the network."

**Tensor parallelism + pipeline parallelism** ([7.9](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.9-tensor-pipeline-parallelism)). GPT-3 was a pre-FSDP training; it used Megatron-LM-style TP within each matrix multiply and PP across layers. ZeRO existed at the time (DeepSpeed 0.3 had ZeRO-1/2) but FSDP/ZeRO-3 was a few months out.

§2.3 ends with:

> "All models were trained on V100 GPUs on part of a high-bandwidth cluster provided by Microsoft."

The V100 era; A100 launched in May 2020 (the same month GPT-3's paper came out). Modern equivalents use H100/H200/B200, $\sim 3$–$10\times$ faster per device than V100.

## 6. The training-compute table (§D.1, appendix)

Brown et al. report total compute in PF-days:

| Model | Parameters | PF-days | FLOPs |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| GPT-3-125M | 125M | 2.6 | $2.25 \cdot 10^{20}$ |
| GPT-3-1.3B | 1.3B | 27.5 | $2.38 \cdot 10^{21}$ |
| GPT-3-13B | 13B | 270 | $2.34 \cdot 10^{22}$ |
| GPT-3-175B (davinci) | 175B | 3640 | $3.14 \cdot 10^{23}$ |

Cross-check the $6ND$ rule:

$$
6 \cdot 175 \cdot 10^9 \cdot 300 \cdot 10^9 = 3.15 \cdot 10^{23}.
$$

Match: $3.14 \cdot 10^{23}$ vs computed $3.15 \cdot 10^{23}$. **The $6ND$ rule predicts GPT-3's reported compute to within 1%.** This is a satisfying confirmation that what we've derived in [6.12](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.12-compute-and-memory) and [7.11](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.11-scaling-laws-kaplan) is the right formula.

## 7. The few-shot story (§3 of the paper)

§3 isn't the focus of our chapter (it's about *evaluation*, not training), but the architectural payoff lives there.

The paper's most influential claim: at $175$B parameters, **the model performs many tasks competitively *without any fine-tuning*** — just from a few examples in the prompt ("few-shot prompting"). E.g., translation, QA, arithmetic, simple reasoning. This was the first systematic demonstration of *in-context learning* at scale.

Why does this matter for *training*? Because it implies that the *base* pretraining (next-token cross-entropy on a generic corpus) is already enough to produce a system with broadly useful capabilities. You don't need a separate "instruction tuning" or "RL alignment" step to get from "trained LM" to "useful product"; the LM itself, prompted appropriately, was already useful.

This was *not* obvious before GPT-3. The 2018-era view was: LMs are pretraining for downstream task-specific fine-tuning. GPT-3 showed the LM-as-product story.

The follow-up — that *instruction tuning + RLHF on top* makes it dramatically better — is the topic of Cap 9. But GPT-3 established the baseline: even just a pretrained LM at $175$B can be a useful AI system.

## 8. What survives, what doesn't (2020 → 2026)

**Survives:**

- Decoder-only Transformer architecture.
- Pre-LN block, byte-level BPE tokenizer.
- Adam + cosine LR schedule (now often WSD) + gradient clipping at 1.0.
- Document packing.
- Batch size growing during training.
- $6 N D$ as the compute estimate.
- Scaling-law-driven planning.
- In-context learning via prompting (the few-shot story).

**Did not survive:**

- **Token-per-parameter ratio of 1.7.** Chinchilla corrected to $\sim 20$; modern over-training pushes to $50$–$300$.
- **Sparse attention in alternating layers.** Replaced by dense attention everywhere + FlashAttention.
- **Learned absolute positional embeddings.** Replaced by RoPE.
- **2048-token context window.** Modern models go to $128k$+ via RoPE scaling.
- **TP + PP without FSDP.** FSDP/ZeRO-3 now handles the optimiser/gradient/parameter sharding more cleanly.
- **WebText2 + Books1/2 as data sources.** Replaced by DCLM, FineWeb-Edu, and other classifier-filtered web data.
- **GPT-3-175B itself as the SOTA reference.** Beaten by every 70B+ Chinchilla-trained model from 2022 onward.

GPT-3 was, in retrospect, a *correct architecture trained at the wrong scale allocation*. The 2022 Chinchilla correction shifted everyone to the right allocation; the 2023 RoPE adoption changed the positional encoding; the 2023 FSDP adoption changed the parallelism story; the 2024 inference-aware over-training changed the deployment economics. But the *skeleton* — embedding → $L$ pre-LN blocks → final LN → tied LM head, trained by causal cross-entropy on a curated web mixture — is exactly what GPT-3 said in 2020.

## 9. Three things that should now feel obvious

Per the AIAYN reading ritual ([6.11 §5.1](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.11-transformer-block-aiayn)): close the paper, write down three statements from §2 that now feel obvious. Sample three I'd write:

**(a) "We use the same architecture as GPT-2 [...] with pre-normalization."**

Pre-LN was the *single* enabling change for stable deep stack training ([6.10](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.10-pre-vs-post-norm)). Without it, 96-layer training wouldn't have converged. The phrase "pre-normalization" sits in one half-sentence; behind it is a whole session's worth of analysis.

**(b) "Adam with $\beta_1 = 0.9, \beta_2 = 0.95, \epsilon = 10^{-8}$. We clip the global norm of the gradient at 1.0."**

These hyperparameters are unchanged from 2020 to 2026, and they're chosen for principled reasons: $\beta_2 = 0.95$ (not the typical default $0.999$) keeps the second-moment estimator responsive to recent gradient statistics, which matters in LLM training where the loss landscape evolves smoothly across the long run ([3.5 §4](/session/ch3-optimization-regularization/3.5-adam)). Gradient clipping at 1.0 prevents the rare spike that would otherwise destabilise training. Two simple knobs, very well-chosen.

**(c) "$C \approx 3 \cdot 10^{23}$ FLOPs" matches "$6 \cdot N \cdot D$".**

The $6 N D$ rule, derived in [6.12](/session/ch6-attention-transformer/6.12-compute-and-memory), predicts GPT-3's reported compute to within 1%. This is the rule that makes scaling-law planning work — without it, you can't even estimate the cost of a training run.

## 10. What we keep going forward

Chapter 7 closes here. The chapter built modern pretraining from the architectural primitives of Chapter 6: the data pipeline, the tokenizer, the precision recipe, the data-sequencing recipe, the memory-management tricks, the distributed-training axes, the scaling-law planning machinery, and the long-context extension.

The result is a complete recipe: pick a compute budget, fit a Chinchilla-corrected loss curve to your data, choose $(N, D)$ to hit your target capability, allocate to a TP × PP × FSDP cluster, train with bf16 + cosine/WSD + gradient clipping + document packing + activation checkpointing, extend to 128k+ context, and ship.

[Chapter 8 — Modern LLM architectures] covers what's *inside* the resulting blocks at the frontier: SwiGLU, RMSNorm, GQA/MQA, MLA, MoE, hybrid SSM. These are the post-2020 architectural refinements that build on top of GPT-3's skeleton — each cutting some specific cost (KV-cache, FFN parameters, inference compute) without changing the fundamental recipe.
