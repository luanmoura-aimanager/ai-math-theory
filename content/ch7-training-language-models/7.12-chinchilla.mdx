## 1. Why this session exists

Kaplan et al. (2020, [7.11](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.11-scaling-laws-kaplan)) gave us power-law scaling and the conversion $C \approx 6 N D$. They also gave a prescription: at fixed compute, **scale model size aggressively, dataset size modestly**. GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B tokens, ~1.7 tokens per parameter) followed this recipe.

In 2022, Hoffmann et al. at DeepMind redid the analysis with more careful methodology and reached the opposite prescription:

> **At fixed compute, the loss-minimising allocation has roughly equal contributions from model size and dataset size. The optimal ratio is $D / N \approx 20$ tokens per parameter.**

The corollary: GPT-3 was *substantially over-parameterised and under-trained*. A model with $1/4$ the parameters trained on $4\times$ more data would have achieved lower loss at the same compute cost.

DeepMind demonstrated this by training **Chinchilla** (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) and showed it outperformed Gopher (280B parameters, 300B tokens — a Kaplan-style allocation at the same compute). Chinchilla was 4× smaller and beat Gopher across the board.

This result reshaped LLM training. Every model from 2022 onward — LLaMA, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek — uses Chinchilla-style allocations, with a 2024–2026 twist: models are often *over-trained* relative to Chinchilla (LLaMA-3 at $\sim 215$ tokens per parameter), because the field realised that **training cost is amortised across inference**, and a slightly-overtrained smaller model is cheaper to deploy than a Chinchilla-optimal larger one.

This session: the Chinchilla derivation, the corrected prescription, why production LLMs over-train, and the "data wall" worry (revisited from [7.1 §5](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.1-pretraining-data)).

## 2. The Chinchilla experiment

**Hoffmann, Borgeaud, Mensch, et al., 2022<FN n={1} />, *Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models*** ([arXiv:2203.15556](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556)). Three complementary methodologies, all converging on the same answer.

### 2.1 Approach 1: fix model size, vary compute

For each model size $N \in \{75\text{M}, 250\text{M}, \dots, 10\text{B}\}$, train for many different durations (different $D$). For each $N$, plot loss against compute $C = 6 N D$. The envelope of these curves traces $L^*(C)$: the *best* loss achievable per compute.

The minimum of each curve corresponds to the optimal $D^*(N)$. Read off the pair $(N, D^*(N))$ at each $N$ value, then fit a power law:

$$
N \propto C^{a}, \qquad D \propto C^{1 - a}.
$$

Chinchilla's result: $a \approx 0.49$, i.e. **$N$ and $D$ should scale in roughly equal proportion**. (Kaplan's value was $a \approx 0.73$ — far more weight on $N$.)

### 2.2 Approach 2: parametric loss function

Fit a joint loss surface

$$
L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}.
$$

(Three power-law pieces: irreducible $E$, $N$-bottlenecked, $D$-bottlenecked.) Minimise subject to $C = 6 N D$. The fit gives $\alpha \approx 0.34$, $\beta \approx 0.28$, and the optimum at $D^*/N^* \approx 20$.

### 2.3 Approach 3: IsoFLOP curves

Pick a fixed compute budget $C$ and train many models *at that compute*, varying $N$ (and adjusting $D = C/(6N)$ to keep $C$ constant). Plot loss as a function of $N$ at fixed $C$. The minimum identifies the optimal $N$ at that compute, and hence $D$.

Repeating for multiple $C$ values traces the optimal allocation curve. Again: $D / N \approx 20$.

### 2.4 The Chinchilla itself

DeepMind trained **Chinchilla** (70B, 1.4T tokens) using the new prescription. Comparison with Gopher (280B, 300B tokens — DeepMind's earlier Kaplan-style model) at *the same compute*:

| Model | $N$ | $D$ | $D/N$ | MMLU |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Gopher | 280B | 300B | 1.07 | 60.0 |
| Chinchilla | 70B | 1400B | 20.0 | **67.5** |

Chinchilla beat Gopher on almost every benchmark, despite being $4\times$ smaller. **The Kaplan-style allocation was leaving substantial performance on the table**.

## 3. The corrected prescription

Boxed:

$$
\boxed{\ N^* \propto C^{0.49}, \qquad D^* \propto C^{0.51}, \qquad \frac{D^*}{N^*} \approx 20 \text{ tokens per parameter}. \ }
$$

### 3.1 The rule of thumb

A simple two-step recipe:

1. **Pick compute budget $C$ in FLOPs.**
2. **Compute $N^*, D^*$ via** $N \cdot D \approx C / 6$ and $D / N \approx 20$. So $N^* \approx \sqrt{C / 120}, D^* \approx 20 N^*$.

For $C = 10^{23}$ FLOPs (a typical 2023-era frontier run):

$$
N^* \approx \sqrt{10^{23} / 120} \approx 3 \times 10^{10}, \qquad D^* \approx 6 \times 10^{11}.
$$

So 30B parameters trained on 600B tokens, at the same compute as a Kaplan-prescribed 150B model on 100B tokens.

### 3.2 Why Kaplan got it wrong

Two methodological problems with Kaplan, fixed by Chinchilla:

**(a) Learning-rate scaling.** Kaplan trained all model sizes with the same LR schedule. Hoffmann et al. used a cosine schedule with peak LR scaled to model size (smaller LR for larger models). This let small models *actually train to convergence*, exposing their true loss potential.

**(b) Cosine LR schedule cycle length.** Kaplan used a fixed cosine cycle independent of training duration. If your cycle ends at 100B tokens but you only train for 50B, you've evaluated at the wrong point of the schedule — small models look artificially worse. Hoffmann et al. matched cycle length to training duration.

Both corrections favour smaller models. Once applied, the optimal allocation shifted toward more data and away from more parameters.

### 3.3 What survives from Kaplan

The framework: power laws, $L_\infty$, joint formula, critical batch size. These are all still right. Only the *optimal allocation* and the exact exponents changed.

## 5. Inference-aware over-training

Chinchilla optimises **training-loss-per-training-FLOP**. But a deployed LLM is served *trillions* of times after training, so the relevant quantity is **total cost per user query**.

### 5.1 The total-cost argument

Let $C_\text{train}$ = total training FLOPs, $C_\text{inf-per-query}$ = inference FLOPs per query, $Q$ = expected number of queries over the model's lifetime. Then total cost (ignoring constants):

$$
C_\text{total} = C_\text{train} + Q \cdot C_\text{inf-per-query}.
$$

Inference FLOPs scale as $\sim 2 N$ per token generated (no backward, no Adam — just the forward). For $Q$ in the billions or trillions of queries, $Q \cdot C_\text{inf-per-query}$ dominates by orders of magnitude.

**You want a small $N$, even at the cost of more training**, because every query thereafter benefits from the smaller-$N$ model.

### 5.2 The new optimum

If you anticipate $Q$ queries, the inference-aware optimum (Sardana, Frankle, 2024<FN n={2} />, [arXiv:2401.00448](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00448), *Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal*) is:

$$
D^* / N^* \approx 20 \cdot (1 + \kappa \cdot Q / Q_\text{ref})^{1 / \beta},
$$

where $\kappa$ is a weighting factor and $Q_\text{ref}$ is the queries-equivalent of one training run. For frontier LLMs deployed broadly ($Q$ in the $10^{12}$+ range), the optimum is $D/N$ in the hundreds — exactly LLaMA-3's regime.

**Modern frontier LLMs train at $D/N$ between $50$ and $300$**, depending on the model's expected deployment scale. Small consumer-facing models over-train the hardest (their deployment volume is huge per parameter); proprietary or research models stay closer to $D/N \approx 20$.

### 5.3 The "Chinchilla ceiling"

A natural concern: doesn't over-training waste compute? After all, Chinchilla showed that past the optimum, additional training tokens give diminishing returns *per training FLOP*. Yes — but the diminishing returns are on the training-cost side, while the *inference saving* is on the inference-cost side. The break-even calculation is straightforward: for any deployed model, you can compute "how many queries does over-training pay off after?" Answer is usually in the hundreds of millions.

For Meta deploying LLaMA-3 to billions of monthly active users, the break-even is reached in days. For a research lab training a 70B model that 100 academics will play with, Chinchilla-optimal stays optimal.

<Aside type="note">
**The "all the data you have" recipe.** Increasingly, frontier labs in 2024–2026 give up trying to compute the optimal $D$ and just train on *all* the high-quality tokens they have. The data wall ([7.1 §5](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.1-pretraining-data)) is real: at $10^{13}$+ tokens of unique high-quality web data, plus code/math/books, you're at the boundary of what's possible to assemble. So the recipe becomes: pick $N$ to fit a target inference cost, train on every quality token available. This is empirically close to the inference-aware optimum for production deployments.
</Aside>

## 6. The fundamental trade-offs

A summary of how the field navigates the $(N, D, C)$ space in 2026:

**Compute is fixed.** Most labs operate under a hard compute budget — a cluster, a quarter, a budget. $C$ is given.

**Inference cost matters.** $N$ should be as small as you can get away with, *subject to acceptable loss*. Smaller is better for serving.

**Data quality matters.** $D$ should be as much *high-quality* token as you can assemble (with up to 4× repetition tolerable, [7.1 §5 Aside](/session/ch7-training-language-models/7.1-pretraining-data)).

**Over-training is the default.** $D/N \in [50, 300]$ is the modern range. Chinchilla's $D/N = 20$ is a *lower bound* on the optimal, not the optimal itself.

**Smaller is better when it fits.** A 7B model with $D/N = 200$ ($\sim 1.4$T tokens of training) often beats a 30B model with $D/N = 50$ ($\sim 1.5$T tokens, same compute) for *deployment*. The 7B is cheaper to serve.

## 7. The diminishing-returns curve

A practical question: as $D/N$ grows past Chinchilla, how much loss do you save?

The picture is monotone but slow. From Sardana & Frankle (2024) §3, fitted to the Chinchilla parametric form: increasing $D/N$ from 20 to 200 reduces loss by $\sim 3\%$ at fixed $N$. Small in absolute terms — but at the *capability* level, this maps to several MMLU/HumanEval points and substantial qualitative improvements.

So over-training is "expensive per loss point" but the loss points are *valuable*. As of 2026, every additional MMLU point on a frontier model is worth substantial training compute — the field has reached the regime where small loss improvements matter commercially.

## 8. Scaling laws as a planning tool

How a frontier run is planned, in practice:

1. **Pick deployment scale.** Estimate $Q$ (queries per year × deployment lifetime).
2. **Pick target capability.** What MMLU / HumanEval / domain-specific score do you need?
3. **Calibrate the scaling law.** Run a sweep at $C_\text{sweep} = 10^{20}\text{–}10^{22}$ FLOPs. Fit the Chinchilla loss formula to your specific architecture and data distribution. (Your exponents will differ from the published Chinchilla values by a few percent.)
4. **Extrapolate.** Use the fitted formula to predict loss at $(N, D)$ pairs in your compute budget. Pick the $(N, D)$ that hits your target capability *and* has the lowest inference cost.
5. **Train.** Reserve the GPUs. Run for the prescribed number of tokens. Monitor whether the actual loss matches the prediction (within a few percent); if not, debug.

The whole machinery turns LLM development from an art into something close to civil engineering. The scaling-law fit is the architectural drawing; the training run is the construction.

## 9. What we keep going forward

Three things:

1. **Chinchilla-optimal: $D/N \approx 20$.** At fixed training compute, this is the loss-minimising allocation. Kaplan's $D/N \approx 7$ was wrong by a factor of ~3.
2. **Production LLMs over-train.** $D/N \in [50, 300]$. The motive is inference cost: a smaller-but-overtrained model serves cheaper. LLaMA-3 trains at $\sim 215$ tokens per parameter.
3. **Scaling laws are a planning tool.** Fit at small scale; extrapolate; train. This is how 100M-GPU-hour training runs are made tractable.
