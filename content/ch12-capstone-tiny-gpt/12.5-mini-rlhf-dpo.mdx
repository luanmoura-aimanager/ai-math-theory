## 1. Why this session exists

[Chapter 9](/session/ch9-alignment/9.1-why-pretrained-llms-arent-useful) covered the full alignment toolkit: SFT, reward modelling, PPO, GRPO, DPO, RLAIF, inference-time alignment. This session does the *minimum viable instance* on your TinyGPT. The minimum: $\sim 50$ preference pairs, a reward model with $\sim 30$ minutes of training, a DPO step that modifies your TinyGPT v2's weights.

The point isn't to produce a usefully-aligned model — it's to *run the alignment loop* end-to-end on your own model. Once you have, the Chapter 9 derivations stop being abstract.

By the end you should have:

- $\sim 50$ pairwise preferences in a JSON file.
- A small reward model (a copy of TinyGPT v2 with a scalar head) trained on those preferences.
- A DPO-updated TinyGPT v2 that demonstrably prefers one stylistic register over another in its samples.

The whole thing fits in $\sim 300$ lines of Python.

---

## 2. The alignment target

Two prompts, two registers. Pick something where you can confidently label preferences yourself:

- **Prompt 1:** "ROMEO:"
- **Prompt 2:** "JULIET:"
- **Preferred register:** more dramatic, multi-sentence, period-correct Shakespearean.
- **Dispreferred register:** terse, repetitive, modern-sounding.

Why two prompts and not 200? Because the goal is to *feel* alignment, not to make a useful product. With $\sim 50$ pairs across 2 prompts, you can hand-label in $30$ minutes and the DPO step's effect on samples should be visible afterwards.

## 3. Build the preference dataset

### 3.1 Generate candidates

Use TinyGPT v2 (the model from 12.4) at temperature 0.9 with top-$p$ = 0.95 to generate $\sim 20$ continuations per prompt, of length $\sim 150$ tokens each.

```python
import torch
from tiny_gpt_v2 import TinyGPT_v2, GPTConfig
# load model and tokeniser...

samples = {"ROMEO:": [], "JULIET:": []}
for prompt in samples:
    for _ in range(20):
        idx = tokenise(prompt).unsqueeze(0).cuda()
        out = model.generate(idx, max_new_tokens=150, temperature=0.9, top_p=0.95)
        samples[prompt].append(decode(out[0].tolist()))

import json
with open("samples.json", "w") as f:
    json.dump(samples, f, indent=2)
```

### 3.2 Hand-label preferences

Open `samples.json`, read pairs of completions for the same prompt, and label which one is *more Shakespearean*. Repeat $\sim 25$ times per prompt for $\sim 50$ pairs total.

Format the dataset as a list of dicts:

```json
[
  {
    "prompt": "ROMEO:",
    "chosen": "ROMEO: O fair morning! how strange is this hour...",
    "rejected": "ROMEO: I am here. I am here. I am here..."
  },
  ...
]
```

A sensible rejection criterion: penalise repetition loops, single-sentence answers, modern-sounding diction; prefer multi-clause, period vocabulary, multi-character dialogue. Be consistent. (This is exactly the labelling work that Chapter 9.3 abstracts — labellers applying a rubric.)

Save to `preferences.json`.

### 3.3 The cost of this step

In real RLHF, this is the *expensive* step — Anthropic and OpenAI pay labellers tens of thousands of dollars per dataset (Cap 9.3 §4). Your $50$-pair version is the toy-scale demonstration.

The dataset is also the *limiting factor*. If your labels are inconsistent or your samples don't actually distinguish the registers, no amount of clever training will produce a meaningful alignment. Spend the $30$ minutes here carefully.

## 4. Train a reward model

The Bradley-Terry reward objective ([Cap 9.4 §2](/session/ch9-alignment/9.4-bradley-terry-reward-objective)) — recap:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{RM} \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_c, y_r) \sim D}\big[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_c) - r_\phi(x, y_r)\big)\big]
$$

where $r_\phi$ is the reward model's scalar score for a (prompt, completion) pair, $y_c$ is the chosen response, $y_r$ the rejected.

### 4.1 Reward-model architecture

Easy version: start from a copy of TinyGPT v2's weights, replace the LM head with a scalar head:

```python
class RewardModel(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, base_model):
        super().__init__()
        self.base = base_model
        # replace lm_head with scalar head
        self.scalar_head = nn.Linear(base_model.config.n_embd, 1, bias=False)
    def forward(self, idx):
        # forward through base model up to (but not including) the lm_head
        # one common trick: extract hidden states by inspecting the model's internal
        # for a tiny rewrite, replicate the base forward without the unembed
        x = self.base.wte(idx)
        for block in self.base.blocks:
            x = block(x)
        x = self.base.rms_f(x)
        # mean-pool over sequence (or take final token's hidden)
        h = x.mean(dim=1)  # (B, n_embd)
        return self.scalar_head(h).squeeze(-1)  # (B,)
```

The scalar head is initialised randomly; the rest of the model inherits from your trained TinyGPT v2. This is the standard RM-from-pretrained-model recipe (Cap 9.3 §3.2).

### 4.2 The training loop

```python
import torch.nn.functional as F

rm = RewardModel(base_model=tiny_gpt_v2_trained_copy).cuda()
optimiser = torch.optim.AdamW(rm.parameters(), lr=1e-5, weight_decay=0.0)

for epoch in range(5):
    for batch in dataloader_preferences:
        prompt_ids = tokenise(batch["prompt"]).cuda()
        chosen_ids = tokenise(batch["prompt"] + batch["chosen"]).cuda()
        rejected_ids = tokenise(batch["prompt"] + batch["rejected"]).cuda()
        r_c = rm(chosen_ids)
        r_r = rm(rejected_ids)
        loss = -F.logsigmoid(r_c - r_r).mean()
        loss.backward()
        optimiser.step()
        optimiser.zero_grad()
```

Expected: $\sim 20$ minutes of training on a single GPU. Loss should decrease from $\sim 0.7$ (random) to $\sim 0.3$–$0.5$. Don't expect $\sim 0$ — with only 50 pairs, the model can't perfectly fit; that's fine.

### 4.3 Sanity-check the RM

Before moving to DPO, verify the RM does what you intended. Generate $5$ new samples per prompt with temperature 0.9, score each with `rm()`, and check: do the high-RM-scored samples look "more Shakespearean" to you than the low-RM-scored?

If yes, the RM has learned a meaningful proxy for your preferences. If no — debug; common cause is too few pairs or labelling noise.

## 5. DPO step

DPO ([Cap 9.10 §3](/session/ch9-alignment/9.10-dpo)) reformulates RLHF as a closed-form preference loss directly on the policy, avoiding the explicit RL step:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} \;=\; -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_c, y_r) \sim D}\bigg[\log \sigma\bigg(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_c|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_c|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_r|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_r|x)}\bigg)\bigg].
$$

Two models: $\pi_\theta$ (the trainable policy, started from $\pi_\text{ref}$) and $\pi_\text{ref}$ (a frozen reference, the SFT model — for us, TinyGPT v2). Hyperparameter $\beta$ controls how much $\pi_\theta$ is allowed to diverge from $\pi_\text{ref}$; typical $\beta \in [0.1, 0.5]$.

### 5.1 Compute the log-probabilities

For a (prompt, completion) pair, the log-probability is the sum of per-token cross-entropies on the *completion* portion only (not the prompt):

```python
def compute_logprob(model, idx, prompt_len):
    """Return sum of log probabilities of completion tokens."""
    logits, _ = model(idx)  # (B, T, V)
    logits = logits[:, :-1, :]  # shift for next-token
    targets = idx[:, 1:]
    log_probs = F.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)
    gather_lp = log_probs.gather(-1, targets.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)  # (B, T-1)
    # mask out prompt tokens (set to 0 to ignore)
    completion_mask = (torch.arange(gather_lp.shape[1])[None, :] >= prompt_len - 1).float().cuda()
    return (gather_lp * completion_mask).sum(dim=1)
```

### 5.2 The DPO training loop

```python
ref_model = tiny_gpt_v2_trained_frozen.cuda()
policy = tiny_gpt_v2_trained_copy.cuda()    # start from ref weights, trainable

optimiser = torch.optim.AdamW(policy.parameters(), lr=1e-6, weight_decay=0.0)
beta = 0.1

for epoch in range(3):
    for batch in dataloader_preferences:
        chosen_ids = tokenise(batch["prompt"] + batch["chosen"]).cuda()
        rejected_ids = tokenise(batch["prompt"] + batch["rejected"]).cuda()
        prompt_len = len(tokenise(batch["prompt"]))

        # Policy log-probs
        lp_c_pi = compute_logprob(policy, chosen_ids, prompt_len)
        lp_r_pi = compute_logprob(policy, rejected_ids, prompt_len)

        # Reference log-probs (frozen, no grad)
        with torch.no_grad():
            lp_c_ref = compute_logprob(ref_model, chosen_ids, prompt_len)
            lp_r_ref = compute_logprob(ref_model, rejected_ids, prompt_len)

        # DPO loss
        h = beta * ((lp_c_pi - lp_c_ref) - (lp_r_pi - lp_r_ref))
        loss = -F.logsigmoid(h).mean()
        loss.backward()
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(policy.parameters(), 1.0)
        optimiser.step()
        optimiser.zero_grad()
```

Run for $\sim 3$ epochs. Total training time: $\sim 10$ minutes on a single GPU.

### 5.3 Note: no RM needed for DPO

DPO doesn't actually require the RM — its derivation (Cap 9.10) shows that the policy *is* implicitly the reward model. So you could skip the reward-model step entirely and just train DPO directly on the preferences.

We did the RM separately because it (a) lets you sanity-check the dataset (§4.3), (b) demonstrates the Bradley-Terry objective end-to-end, (c) shows the *equivalence* of RM-then-RL vs DPO-directly. In production, most teams use DPO without an explicit RM step.

## 6. Evaluate the aligned model

Generate samples from the DPO-aligned model with the same prompt and seed as the v2 baseline. Compare side-by-side.

### 6.1 Expected behaviour change

- **More Shakespearean diction.** Period vocabulary, longer multi-clause sentences.
- **Less repetition.** The "I am here. I am here." failure mode you penalised should be less frequent.
- **Multi-character dialogue.** If you preferred turns with multiple speakers, the model should produce more of them.

The effect is *modest* — with 50 pairs and a tiny model, you're not going to get dramatic transformation — but should be visible. If you see *no* difference, common causes:

- $\beta$ too small: model didn't update enough. Try $\beta = 0.3$.
- $\beta$ too large: model collapsed to extreme behaviour, output may now be repetitive in a *new* way. Try $\beta = 0.05$.
- Learning rate too low: gradients aren't moving weights. Try $5 \times 10^{-6}$.
- Labels too inconsistent: re-check your preference dataset.

### 6.2 The KL leash visualisation

Recall (Cap 9.9): the KL between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$ measures how much the policy has diverged. Compute it on a held-out set of $\sim 20$ prompts:

```python
def estimate_kl(policy, ref_model, prompts, n_samples=5):
    total_kl = 0
    for prompt in prompts:
        idx = tokenise(prompt).unsqueeze(0).cuda()
        out = policy.generate(idx, max_new_tokens=100, temperature=1.0)
        lp_pi = compute_logprob(policy, out, prompt_len=idx.shape[1])
        with torch.no_grad():
            lp_ref = compute_logprob(ref_model, out, prompt_len=idx.shape[1])
        kl_estimate = (lp_pi - lp_ref).mean().item()  # one-sample MC estimate
        total_kl += kl_estimate
    return total_kl / len(prompts)

print(estimate_kl(policy, ref_model, ["ROMEO:", "JULIET:"], n_samples=5))
```

You should see a non-trivial KL value (typically $\sim 1$–$10$ nats per completion). If KL is near zero, training did nothing; if it's huge ($\sim 100$+), the policy has collapsed.

A well-tuned DPO step in your setup: KL $\sim 2$–$5$ per completion. Visible behaviour change, but the model still resembles its reference.

## 7. The full pipeline you've now run

```
1. Tokeniser preparation     (Cap 5.1)
2. Pretrain TinyGPT v1       (Cap 12.2–12.3; the whole Chapters 0–7 stack at toy scale)
3. Architecture upgrade v2   (Cap 12.4; RoPE + RMSNorm from Chapter 8)
4. Preference data           (Cap 9.3; hand-labelled rubric, 50 pairs)
5. Reward model              (Cap 9.4 Bradley-Terry on your data)
6. DPO step                  (Cap 9.10 closed-form preference learning)
7. Evaluation                (sample comparison + KL diagnostic from Cap 9.9)
```

This is the entire shape of a modern frontier LLM pipeline, at $1/10000$ the scale. The fact that you can run it on a single GPU in an afternoon is itself a testament to how clean these techniques are.

### 7.1 What would change at frontier scale

- **Steps 2–3:** pretrained model, billions of parameters, trillions of tokens. Cap 7.11–7.12 scaling laws.
- **Step 4:** $10^4$–$10^6$ preference pairs across thousands of prompt categories.
- **Step 5:** RM training adds RM-ensemble, calibration, length-normalisation, etc.
- **Step 6:** DPO or PPO at billions-of-parameters scale; usually multiple rounds of *iterative* DPO (Cap 9.11).
- **Plus:** SFT step (Cap 9.2) before alignment; constitutional principles (Cap 9.13); RLHF on AI-generated preference data; inference-time complement (Cap 9.15, Cap 10.7).

You don't need to do those for the capstone. Knowing that the *shape* is the same gives you the conceptual scaffolding to read any 2026 alignment paper.
