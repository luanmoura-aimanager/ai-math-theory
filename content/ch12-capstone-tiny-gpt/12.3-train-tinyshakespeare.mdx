## 1. Why this session exists

You have working infrastructure (12.1) and your own model (12.2). Now train it, watch the loss go down, and look at what it produces along the way.

The training itself is mechanical. The point of this session is *what you observe* — the qualitative progression of model capability as loss decreases, the per-checkpoint sampling that lets you "feel" the architecture working. This is the closest you'll get in this course to *seeing* a language model learn.

By the end you should have a sense of: how long a tiny model takes to train to fluent character-level Shakespeare, what plateaus on the loss curve correspond to behaviourally, and what categories of error a $10$M-parameter character LM exhibits.

## 3. Watching the loss curve

A reference training trajectory (your exact numbers will vary):

| Step | Train loss | Val loss |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 4.27 | 4.27 |
| 100 | 2.71 | 2.74 |
| 500 | 1.74 | 1.83 |
| 1000 | 1.35 | 1.55 |
| 2000 | 1.03 | 1.49 |
| 3500 | 0.79 | 1.48 |
| 5000 | 0.62 | 1.47 |

A few things to notice.

### 3.1 The early steep drop

From step 0 to step ~$500$, val loss drops from $4.27$ to $\sim 1.83$. This is the model learning *unigram* and *bigram* statistics of the corpus — which characters are common, which character pairs are common. About $50\%$ of the total achievable loss reduction happens here.

The dominant feature is the per-character entropy of English text, $\sim 1$–$2$ bits per character ($\sim 0.7$–$1.4$ nats). A unigram model would hit $\sim 3.0$ val loss; a bigram model $\sim 2.3$; a 4-gram $\sim 1.8$. So the model has reached approximately *4-gram* statistics by step 500.

### 3.2 The slower middle

From step ~$500$ to step ~$2000$, val loss drifts down to $\sim 1.5$. This is the model learning *longer-range* statistics: word completion, simple syntactic patterns, character-name conventions ("DUKE OF YORK:" formatting).

### 3.3 The plateau

After step ~$2000$, val loss is essentially flat at $\sim 1.5$. Training loss continues to decrease (it reaches $\sim 0.6$); the gap is overfitting. The model is *memorising* the training corpus rather than learning new structure.

This is the limit of what a $10$M-parameter character model can learn from $1$ MB of Shakespeare. Bigger models or more data would extend the curve; we won't.

## 4. Sampling along the trajectory

Modify `sample.py` to load a checkpoint by step. The cleanest way: configure `train.py` to save checkpoints at $\{500, 1000, 2000, 5000\}$ steps (look for the `save_checkpoint` logic and add intermediate saves). Then:

```bash
python sample.py --out_dir=out-shakespeare-char --checkpoint=ckpt_step500.pt --start="ROMEO:" --max_new_tokens=200
```

### 4.1 Step 500 (val loss $\sim 1.83$, ~bigram level)

> *ROMEO: he the his and the of the for to the so and a thes of and the andd the the the and an*

Recognisable English-ish character distribution. No word boundaries enforced beyond what the model picked up. Lots of "the", spaces look syntactically random.

### 4.2 Step 1000 (val loss $\sim 1.55$)

> *ROMEO: I shall not be the king of the lord that is the day of the world, and the king of the king, and the lord of the king of the king...*

Words now well-formed. Articles, pronouns, common nouns. No coherent meaning, but the local statistics are clearly working. Repetition is a problem — model is greedy-trapped on high-frequency words.

### 4.3 Step 2000 (val loss $\sim 1.49$)

> *ROMEO: O, what a sight is this! my lord, I am not so unhappy as I am, and the king is not so much as a king.*

Multi-clause structure appearing. Some flavour of Shakespearean diction ("O, what a sight"). Still no coherent argument across sentences, but local coherence is high. Repetition still present but less severe.

### 4.4 Step 5000 (val loss $\sim 1.47$)

> *ROMEO: I am not the man that I was, for I have been a fool. The world is full of strange things, and I cannot find the way to my home. JULIET: Why, my lord, what is the matter? ROMEO: Nothing, my dear; only a thought that came to me as I was reading the play.*

Inter-character dialogue is now well-formed. Period-correct vocabulary, multiple speakers, plausible turn-taking. Still no coherent plot, but the *format* of Shakespeare is solidly internalised.

## 5. Failure modes to characterise

Spend $\sim 15$ minutes sampling from your trained model with various prompts. Catalogue the failure modes — useful both for understanding what character-level models can/can't do and for setting up the Chapter 12.4 / 12.5 upgrades.

### 5.1 Pathology checklist

- **Repetition loops.** The model emits the same phrase repeatedly. Especially at low temperature. (Cap 10.1 §5: the beam-search curse, also relevant to greedy decoding.)
- **Character drift.** The model starts as one character and gradually becomes another. No persistent identity tracking.
- **Inconsistent facts.** "King of France" → "queen of France" → "King of England" within a paragraph. The model has no consistent world model.
- **Verbatim corpus copying.** Especially with very low temperature. The model has memorised chunks of the training text. (Detect by grepping outputs against the training corpus.)
- **Off-corpus tokens.** Characters outside the training vocabulary appear in completions. Rare with character-level (~$65$-char vocab); more common with BPE.

### 5.2 What works well

- Local fluency. Word formation, common-collocation patterns, basic sentence structure.
- Genre/format mimicry. The "Shakespeare-flavour" is convincing in surface texture.
- Common-word distribution. The output's word frequencies roughly match Shakespeare's.

### 5.3 What doesn't

- Plot coherence.
- Cross-paragraph reference resolution.
- Factual consistency.
- Long-range arithmetic, logic, or planning.

These limits are not patches you can apply at $10$M parameters. They are *structural* — character LMs at this scale don't have the capacity for what we'd call "understanding." Scale (Cap 7.11–7.12), better architecture (Cap 12.4 RoPE/RMSNorm/GQA), better data, and alignment (12.5 mini-RLHF) all help; none individually changes the qualitative picture.

## 6. Comparing to nanoGPT's baseline

Re-run the baseline (`nanoGPT/model.py`) for comparison. With the same config and seed, your `TinyGPT` should produce nearly identical loss curves — within $\sim 5\%$ of the baseline val loss. If yours is *substantially worse*, something is wrong with your implementation; debug.

A useful diagnostic: param count. Both implementations should report the same number ($\sim 10.6$M for the default config). If yours is different, you've miscounted weight-tying or layer construction.

Sampling should also be qualitatively similar — same Shakespeare-flavoured pseudo-text, same failure modes.

## 7. Optional experiment: scale comparison

If you have GPU time, try a smaller and a larger variant:

- **Tiny** ($n_\text{layer}=2$, $n_\text{embd}=128$): ~$0.4$M params. Val loss caps around $1.9$. Output is much less coherent — barely word-level.
- **Medium** ($n_\text{layer}=12$, $n_\text{embd}=768$): ~$85$M params. Val loss reaches $\sim 1.3$. Output noticeably more coherent — multi-paragraph structure, occasional plausible narratives.

This is your own miniature scaling law (Cap 7.11). Two data points won't give you a clean curve, but the trend is visible: more parameters → lower loss → better output. The slope flattens; the absolute level keeps improving.

## 9. What we keep going forward

Three things.

1. **The training curve has structure.** Early steep drop (unigram/bigram), slower middle (longer-range syntax and format), final plateau (overfitting). Sampling at intermediate checkpoints reveals the qualitative progression.
2. **Character-LM failure modes are structural.** Repetition loops, character drift, factual inconsistency, lack of plot coherence. These don't go away with more steps at fixed scale; they require scale, architecture, or alignment improvements.
3. **The baseline is now an artefact.** Trained model + samples + loss curve. Cap 12.4 next: upgrade to RoPE and RMSNorm and feel the difference.
