## 2. What to write

A one-page essay (~$500$–$800$ words). Structure suggestion (don't follow rigidly):

1. **What a Transformer is.** A decoder-only autoregressive next-token predictor, decomposed as embedding → residual-stream → repeated read-modify-write blocks → unembed → softmax. Use your own notation; pick what feels natural after a year with this material.
2. **Why attention.** What it buys over recurrence and convolutions. State the QK ↔ OV decomposition; argue why scaled-dot-product is the natural primitive.
3. **Why depth.** Residual stream + pre-norm + many layers. Each layer adds a small contribution; depth enables composition; superposition lets each layer carry many features.
4. **How it is trained.** Next-token prediction at scale; Chinchilla-compute-optimal scaling; mixed-precision and parallelism.
5. **How it is aligned.** SFT → RLHF (or DPO) → constitutional / RLAIF → inference-time selection. The full Chapter 9 pipeline in compressed form.
6. **How it is deployed.** KV cache, FlashAttention, quantisation, continuous batching, speculative decoding, agentic loops. The Chapter 10 stack.
7. **How it is understood.** Residual stream view; circuits; induction heads; superposition; sparse autoencoders; activation steering. The Chapter 11 vocabulary.
8. **What you take away.** Personal note. What surprised you. What you still find unsatisfying. What you want to study next.

That's eight sections in ~$800$ words — $\sim 100$ words each. The constraint forces compression. Don't pad.

## 4. Worked-example skeleton (don't copy; use as scaffolding)

Below is a *minimal* sample skeleton — what one essay *could* look like. Don't copy it; use it as a starting point for the *shape* if you're stuck. The actual prose, claims, and emphases must be yours.

> ## What a Transformer is, after twelve chapters
> 
> A Transformer is an autoregressive model of token sequences. Given a context $x_{<t}$, it produces a distribution $\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{<t})$ over the next token; sampling iterates the distribution to generate full sequences. The architecture is built out of repeated read-modify-write blocks on a shared residual stream $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\text{model}}$: each block applies a layer-normed attention (which mixes information across positions) and a layer-normed feed-forward (which mixes information across features), both written back to the stream additively.
> 
> The attention primitive is scaled dot product: a query at position $t$ attends to keys at every earlier position, weights are softmaxed, and the weighted sum of values is written. The QK matrix selects *what to look at*; the OV matrix decides *what to write*. This factorisation is the algorithmic substrate of the Transformer — circuits like induction heads (which implement in-context pattern matching $AB \ldots A \to B$) are recognisable combinations of QK and OV behaviours.
> 
> Depth is the second key: a model with $L$ layers can compose $L$ stacked feature transformations through the residual stream. Each layer's small contribution can be tracked as an additive term; collectively they implement complex algorithms that single-layer models cannot. Depth is also what enables superposition — the network packs more features than dimensions into its activations, using sparsity to keep interference manageable.
> 
> Training is next-token prediction at scale. Cross-entropy of the predicted softmax against the next token, minimised by AdamW on shuffled subsequences. The Chinchilla scaling law tells us that compute should be split roughly equally between parameters and data; the modern recipe (mixed precision, ZeRO/FSDP, careful schedulers) scales this to the $10^{25}$ FLOPs frontier.
> 
> Alignment is the multi-stage pipeline that turns a pretrained next-token predictor into a usable assistant: supervised fine-tuning on (prompt, response) pairs, then preference-based optimisation (DPO is the clean derivation; PPO is the workhorse) against a reward signal — human preferences, AI preferences, verifiable rewards for reasoning. Test-time scaling — letting the model "think" before answering — compounds with training-time alignment.
> 
> Deployment is the engineering stack: KV cache (the dominant inference memory cost), FlashAttention (I/O-optimal exact attention), quantisation (INT4 for weights, INT8 for cache), continuous batching with paged KV (vLLM), speculative decoding (free wall-clock from a small draft model), and agentic orchestration on top. A frontier inference server is the integration of all of these.
> 
> Interpretability is the youngest layer of the stack. The residual stream is a bus on which features (linear directions) accumulate. Sparse autoencoders recover these features from the model's activations; activation steering uses them as control knobs. We can now read off, partially, what frontier models have learned — and we are very far from doing this completely. Mech-interp is the safety bet.
> 
> What I take away: the Transformer's success comes from one architectural idea (attention) and many small accumulated improvements (RMSNorm, RoPE, SwiGLU, GQA/MLA, etc.). The hard work has shifted from architecture to alignment, from alignment to inference, from inference to interpretation. The next five years' frontier is probably some hybrid of (i) longer-thinking reasoning models, (ii) agentic loops with tools, (iii) better interpretability tools that scale to frontier models. Whether the safety story can keep pace with the capability story is the central open question.

That's $\sim 750$ words. *Don't copy it.* The essay must be in your own voice, your own emphases, your own choices about what to include. If you find yourself paraphrasing this skeleton, throw it away and start fresh.

## 5. Quality bar

How do you know your essay is good?

### 5.1 Specific not generic

A bad sentence: "Transformers use attention to process sequences." A good sentence: "A Transformer's attention head, factored into a QK matrix that selects which token to attend to and an OV matrix that decides what to write, implements one of perhaps a half-dozen identifiable algorithmic primitives — bigram, copy, induction, name-mover — depending on which of these matrices is more structured."

The first sentence could appear in a marketing pitch. The second has been written by someone who has done the work.

### 5.2 Compressed without lossy

You're squeezing $\sim 100{,}000$ words of course material into $\sim 800$. The right ratio is $125\times$ compression. The wrong way is by dropping content; the right way is by *naming the abstractions* (QK + OV, residual stream, KL leash) and letting the names do the work.

### 5.3 Opinionated

This is your essay. You're allowed to say what you think. "Beam search is dead" is acceptable. "RoPE is the most consequential single architecture change since pre-norm" is acceptable. "I think mech-interp will succeed at scale" is acceptable. Defend your opinions briefly.

### 5.4 Honest about what's unsolved

A good essay names what we don't yet understand: the faithfulness of reasoning-model chains, whether agentic capabilities will outpace alignment, what mech-interp can and can't certify. These open questions are part of the story, not bugs in your understanding.
