## 1. Why this session exists

Softmax + cross-entropy is the output head of almost every modern classifier and the entire next-token loss of every LLM. Underneath, it admits a single backward identity:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \hat y - y.
$$

This is the same identity we saw in 1.2 for sigmoid + BCE (the $K = 2$ case). It is the algebraic miracle that makes softmax + cross-entropy the gold-standard pairing — no $\sigma'$ tail, no $K \times K$ matrix to multiply during backward, just a single subtraction. This session is the careful derivation.

---

## 2. Setup

A single training example. Logits $z \in \mathbb{R}^K$, one-hot label $y \in \{0, 1\}^K$ with $\sum_k y_k = 1$. Predicted distribution:

$$
\hat y_k = \operatorname{softmax}(z)_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}.
$$

Cross-entropy loss:

$$
L = -\sum_{k=1}^K y_k\, \log \hat y_k.
$$

Because $y$ is one-hot, $L = -\log \hat y_c$ where $c$ is the index of the true class. Equivalently, $L = -z_c + \log \sum_j e^{z_j}$ (the log-sum-exp form from 0.10 / 1.7).

---

## 3. The Jacobian of softmax

We need $\partial \hat y_k / \partial z_j$ for all $k, j$. Two cases.

**Case $j = k$:**

$$
\frac{\partial \hat y_k}{\partial z_k}
= \frac{\partial}{\partial z_k} \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_l e^{z_l}}
= \frac{e^{z_k} \cdot \sum_l e^{z_l} - e^{z_k} \cdot e^{z_k}}{\bigl(\sum_l e^{z_l}\bigr)^2}
= \hat y_k - \hat y_k^2 = \hat y_k\,(1 - \hat y_k).
$$

**Case $j \ne k$:**

$$
\frac{\partial \hat y_k}{\partial z_j}
= \frac{\partial}{\partial z_j} \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_l e^{z_l}}
= -\frac{e^{z_k}\, e^{z_j}}{\bigl(\sum_l e^{z_l}\bigr)^2}
= -\hat y_k\, \hat y_j.
$$

Combining,

$$
\boxed{\ \frac{\partial \hat y_k}{\partial z_j} \;=\; \hat y_k\,(\delta_{kj} - \hat y_j),\ }
$$

where $\delta_{kj}$ is the Kronecker delta. The Jacobian matrix is

$$
J = \operatorname{diag}(\hat y) - \hat y\, \hat y^\top \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}.
$$

It is symmetric, PSD, and singular — its null space is $\mathbf{1}$, reflecting the simplex constraint $\sum_k \hat y_k = 1$.

---

## 4. The gradient of cross-entropy: derivation 1 (via the Jacobian)

By the chain rule,

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j} = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y_k} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat y_k}{\partial z_j}.
$$

Each factor:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y_k} = -\frac{y_k}{\hat y_k}, \qquad
\frac{\partial \hat y_k}{\partial z_j} = \hat y_k\,(\delta_{kj} - \hat y_j).
$$

Multiply and sum:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j} = -\sum_k \frac{y_k}{\hat y_k} \cdot \hat y_k\,(\delta_{kj} - \hat y_j) = -\sum_k y_k\,(\delta_{kj} - \hat y_j).
$$

The $\hat y_k$ in numerator and denominator cancel. Distribute the sum:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j} = -\sum_k y_k\, \delta_{kj} + \hat y_j\, \sum_k y_k = -y_j + \hat y_j \cdot 1 = \hat y_j - y_j.
$$

Writing this as a vector identity,

$$
\boxed{\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} \;=\; \hat y - y \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^K. \ }
$$

---

## 5. Derivation 2 (via the log-sum-exp form)

Same result, no Jacobian. Start from

$$
L = -z_c + \log \sum_j e^{z_j}.
$$

For the first piece, $\partial(-z_c) / \partial z_j = -\delta_{jc} = -y_j$ (since $y$ is one-hot with $y_c = 1$).

For the second piece,

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial z_j} \log \sum_l e^{z_l} = \frac{e^{z_j}}{\sum_l e^{z_l}} = \hat y_j.
$$

(A side identity worth committing to memory: **the gradient of log-sum-exp is the softmax.**)

Adding,

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j} = -y_j + \hat y_j = \hat y_j - y_j. \quad \blacksquare
$$

The second derivation is shorter because the log-sum-exp form bypasses the softmax Jacobian entirely. Internally, PyTorch's `F.cross_entropy` uses something equivalent.

---

## 6. Gradient w.r.t. the parameters

With logits $z = W x + b$ in $\mathbb{R}^K$, the chain rule extends through the linear layer.

**$\nabla_W L$.** $z_k = w_k^\top x + b_k$, so $\partial z_k / \partial w_k = x^\top$ (and $\partial z_k / \partial w_l = 0$ for $l \ne k$). Therefore

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_k} = (\hat y_k - y_k)\, x^\top,
$$

which stacks into the matrix outer-product

$$
\boxed{\ \nabla_W L \;=\; (\hat y - y)\, x^\top \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{K \times n}.\ }
$$

(Residual column times input row.)

**$\nabla_b L$.** $\partial z_k / \partial b_k = 1$, so

$$
\nabla_b L = \hat y - y.
$$

Averaged over a mini-batch of $m$ examples,

$$
\nabla_W L_{\text{batch}} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m (\hat y_i - y_i)\, x_i^\top, \qquad
\nabla_b L_{\text{batch}} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m (\hat y_i - y_i).
$$

Same "residual × input" pattern as 1.3, just with vector-valued residuals now.

---

## 7. Why this identity is the most-used in deep learning

Three reasons.

1. **It is the backward signal at the loss layer of every classifier.** Every CNN classifier, every Transformer LM head, every reward model in RLHF (Chapter 9) — they all backpropagate $\hat y - y$ out of the softmax + CE head.
2. **It composes cleanly with everything upstream.** Whatever module produced the logits $z$ — a linear layer, a deep stack of attention blocks — receives a "tell me where you went wrong" signal of exactly the right shape (a $K$-dim residual) at exactly the right scale (no $\sigma'$ damping).
3. **It is interpretable.** $\hat y - y$ is the **error in the predicted distribution**. Moving $z$ a step in the direction $-(\hat y - y)$ shifts probability mass from the wrong classes onto the right class. The size of the move is exactly how off the model currently is.

<Aside type="note">
The same identity reappears, in identical form, every time we re-derive a softmax-head loss in this course — there is nothing new in attention's "logit subtraction" backward; it is the same identity in a more elaborate forward graph. For the textbook treatment in our notation, see Bishop, *PRML* (2006) §4.3.4 and Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, *Deep Learning* (2016) §5.5.
</Aside>

---

## 8. Takeaways

- $\partial L_{\text{CE}} / \partial z = \hat y - y$. Memorise this.
- Two derivations: through the softmax Jacobian, or by differentiating the log-sum-exp form.
- Gradient w.r.t. the weight matrix is the outer product $(\hat y - y)\, x^\top$, averaged over the batch.
- Sigmoid + BCE is the $K = 2$ special case of this identity (1.2).
