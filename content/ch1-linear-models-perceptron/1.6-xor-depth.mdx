## 1. Why this session exists

Minsky and Papert's 1969 book *Perceptrons* observed that the simplest non-trivial logical function — exclusive OR — cannot be computed by a single-layer perceptron. The observation effectively killed neural-network research for a decade. The fix (a hidden layer) is mathematically trivial once you know it, but conceptually it is the entire reason the rest of this course exists: **adding depth strictly increases what a linear-threshold network can represent.**

This session does the proof of impossibility and constructs the explicit 2-hidden-unit solution.

---

## 2. XOR

The XOR function on $\{0, 1\}^2$ takes the four input combinations to the values

$$
\text{XOR}(0, 0) = 0,\quad \text{XOR}(0, 1) = 1,\quad \text{XOR}(1, 0) = 1,\quad \text{XOR}(1, 1) = 0.
$$

Geometrically: the two diagonally opposite corners of the unit square share a label; the two off-diagonal corners share the other label.

---

## 3. Impossibility for a single linear unit

**Claim.** No affine function $f(x) = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b$ classifies all four points correctly under the encoding $\{0 \mapsto -1,\ 1 \mapsto +1\}$ via $\operatorname{sign}(f(x))$.

**Proof.** Suppose for contradiction such $(w_1, w_2, b)$ exist. The four required sign conditions read

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{XOR}(0, 0) = 0 \implies\ & b < 0, \\
\text{XOR}(1, 0) = 1 \implies\ & w_1 + b > 0, \\
\text{XOR}(0, 1) = 1 \implies\ & w_2 + b > 0, \\
\text{XOR}(1, 1) = 0 \implies\ & w_1 + w_2 + b < 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Add the middle two:

$$
(w_1 + b) + (w_2 + b) = w_1 + w_2 + 2 b \;>\; 0.
$$

Add the first and the last:

$$
b + (w_1 + w_2 + b) = w_1 + w_2 + 2 b \;<\; 0.
$$

The two sums are *the same number* but are required to be both positive and negative. Contradiction. $\blacksquare$

---

## 4. The solution: one hidden layer

XOR is **not** a half-space. But it *is* the symmetric difference of two half-spaces. We compute it as a small linear combination of thresholded sums.

Let

$$
h_1(x) = \mathbb{1}\bigl[\,x_1 + x_2 - 0.5 > 0\,\bigr], \qquad h_2(x) = \mathbb{1}\bigl[\,x_1 + x_2 - 1.5 > 0\,\bigr].
$$

Read off:

- $h_1(x) = 1$ iff at least one input is on. (Logical OR.)
- $h_2(x) = 1$ iff both inputs are on. (Logical AND.)

Then

$$
\text{XOR}(x_1, x_2) = h_1(x) \,\wedge\, \neg h_2(x) = h_1(x) - h_2(x),
$$

where the subtraction works because $h_1 \ge h_2$ pointwise on $\{0, 1\}^2$.

So XOR is computed by the two-layer network

$$
\begin{aligned}
h_1 &= \mathbb{1}\bigl[\,1 \cdot x_1 + 1 \cdot x_2 - 0.5 > 0\,\bigr] \\
h_2 &= \mathbb{1}\bigl[\,1 \cdot x_1 + 1 \cdot x_2 - 1.5 > 0\,\bigr] \\
o   &= h_1 - h_2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Two hidden units with hard-threshold activations, one output unit with linear activation. Walk the four inputs:

- $(x_1, x_2) = (0, 0)$: $h_1 = 0,\ h_2 = 0,\ o = 0$. ✅
- $(x_1, x_2) = (0, 1)$: $h_1 = 1,\ h_2 = 0,\ o = 1$. ✅
- $(x_1, x_2) = (1, 0)$: $h_1 = 1,\ h_2 = 0,\ o = 1$. ✅
- $(x_1, x_2) = (1, 1)$: $h_1 = 1,\ h_2 = 1,\ o = 0$. ✅

Exactly XOR.

<Aside type="history">
A classical "logic gate" version of this construction uses NAND / AND / OR primitives explicitly; see Minsky & Papert (1969). The version above (two thresholded sums minus each other) is equivalent and is the form that generalises to: *any* Boolean function on $n$ variables is computable by a one-hidden-layer net with at most $2^n$ hidden units. The smoothed (sigmoid / ReLU) version is what we will train in Chapter 2.
</Aside>

---

## 5. What this teaches us about depth

The XOR result is the simplest piece of evidence for two structural truths in deep learning.

1. **Depth ≠ width.** A wide *single-layer* perceptron — no matter how many parameters — can only carve $\mathbb{R}^n$ into two half-spaces. It cannot compute the intersection of two half-spaces, which is what XOR requires.
2. **Composition is the source of expressivity.** Composing two threshold functions yields a *non-convex* decision region. Iterating composition (the MLP of Chapter 2) yields arbitrarily complex piecewise-affine regions; in the limit, MLPs can approximate any continuous function on a compact set — the universal approximation theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1991), which we'll prove in Chapter 3.

The hidden layer is the *new primitive*. It is the difference between fitting a half-space and fitting a polyhedron, and it is the difference between linear regression and "deep learning". Everything else in this course is engineering on top of that insight.

---

## 6. Takeaways

- A single linear classifier cannot solve XOR (the contradiction in §3).
- Two hidden units plus one output unit do solve XOR (the construction in §4).
- Cause: XOR is not a half-space; it is the symmetric difference of two half-spaces.
- This is the prototype demonstration that depth strictly enlarges the function class.
