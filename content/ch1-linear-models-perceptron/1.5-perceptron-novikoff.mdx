## 1. Why this session exists

The **perceptron** (Rosenblatt, 1958) is the first training algorithm for a linear classifier, predating gradient descent on smooth losses. It is also the first algorithm in this course we can prove a clean convergence theorem about. The proof — due to **Novikoff** (1962) — is a one-page induction using the geometric quantities from 1.4. After we have it, every later statement about neural-network training will be measured against this baseline.

---

## 2. The model

Use $\pm 1$ labels: $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$. The classifier:

$$
\hat y(x) = \operatorname{sign}(w^\top x + b).
$$

To simplify notation, *absorb the bias* into the weight vector by augmenting every input with a constant feature:

$$
\tilde x = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1}, \qquad \tilde w = \begin{pmatrix} w \\ b \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1}.
$$

Then $w^\top x + b = \tilde w^\top \tilde x$, and everything works in terms of $\tilde w \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$. For the rest of this session we drop the tildes and pretend the bias is just another weight on a constant-1 feature.

---

## 3. The learning rule

Initialise $w_0 = 0$. Iterate over the data (round-robin or arbitrary order). At each step, given the current $w_k$ and a chosen example $(x_i, y_i)$:

- **Correct case** ($y_i\, w_k^\top x_i > 0$): leave $w$ unchanged.
- **Mistake case** ($y_i\, w_k^\top x_i \le 0$): update

$$
\boxed{\ w_{k+1} \;=\; w_k + y_i\, x_i.\ }
$$

The update pushes $w$ in the direction that improves the score $y_i\, w_k^\top x_i$ for the example that was just misclassified:

$$
y_i\, w_{k+1}^\top x_i = y_i\, w_k^\top x_i + y_i^2\, \lVert x_i \rVert^2 = y_i\, w_k^\top x_i + \lVert x_i \rVert^2.
$$

Each mistake increases that example's score by $\lVert x_i \rVert^2 > 0$.

<Aside type="note">
The perceptron rule looks ad hoc but is exactly the **subgradient method** (0.9) on the convex hinge-style loss

$$
L_{\text{perc}}(w) = \sum_i \max\bigl(0,\, -y_i\, w^\top x_i\bigr).
$$

"Do nothing on correct examples, push by $y_i x_i$ on mistakes" is the negated subgradient of that loss. We'll meet the smoother analogue (hinge + slack) when discussing SVMs in Chapter 3.
</Aside>

## 4. Novikoff's theorem

**Setup.** Suppose the dataset $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^m$ is **linearly separable with geometric margin $\gamma > 0$**, i.e. there exists a unit vector $u \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ (with $\lVert u \rVert = 1$) such that

$$
y_i\, u^\top x_i \;\ge\; \gamma \quad \text{for all } i.
$$

Suppose all inputs are bounded in norm: $\lVert x_i \rVert \le R$ for all $i$.

**Claim.** Starting from $w_0 = 0$ and running the perceptron rule of §3, the total number of mistakes (and hence updates) is at most

$$
\boxed{\ M \;\le\; \frac{R^2}{\gamma^2}. \ }
$$

In particular, the algorithm terminates in finitely many steps.

**Proof.** Let $w_k$ be the weight vector *after the $k$-th mistake*. We track two quantities.

**Bound 1 — alignment with $u$ grows linearly.** Take the inner product of the update $w_{k+1} = w_k + y_i x_i$ with $u$:

$$
u^\top w_{k+1} = u^\top w_k + y_i\, u^\top x_i \ge u^\top w_k + \gamma.
$$

By induction, since $u^\top w_0 = 0$,

$$
u^\top w_k \;\ge\; k\, \gamma.
$$

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $u^\top w_k \le \lVert u \rVert \cdot \lVert w_k \rVert = \lVert w_k \rVert$, hence

$$
\lVert w_k \rVert \;\ge\; k\, \gamma. \qquad (\star)
$$

**Bound 2 — squared norm grows at most linearly.** Expand the squared norm of $w_{k+1}$:

$$
\lVert w_{k+1} \rVert^2 = \lVert w_k \rVert^2 + 2\, y_i\, w_k^\top x_i + \lVert x_i \rVert^2.
$$

Because the update was applied only when $y_i\, w_k^\top x_i \le 0$, the cross term is $\le 0$. And $\lVert x_i \rVert^2 \le R^2$. So

$$
\lVert w_{k+1} \rVert^2 \;\le\; \lVert w_k \rVert^2 + R^2.
$$

By induction from $\lVert w_0 \rVert^2 = 0$,

$$
\lVert w_k \rVert^2 \;\le\; k\, R^2. \qquad (\star\star)
$$

**Combining.** From $(\star)$, $\lVert w_k \rVert^2 \ge k^2 \gamma^2$. From $(\star\star)$, $\lVert w_k \rVert^2 \le k R^2$. So

$$
k^2 \gamma^2 \;\le\; k R^2 \implies k \;\le\; \frac{R^2}{\gamma^2}.
$$

This holds at every $k$ at which a mistake occurs. Hence the total number of mistakes is $M \le R^2 / \gamma^2$. $\blacksquare$

---

## 5. What the bound is — and isn't — telling us

- **Margin matters.** Halving the margin quadruples the worst-case update count. Wider margins → faster convergence.
- **Scale matters only through $R$.** Rescaling all $x_i$ by the same constant $c$ scales both $R$ and $\gamma$ by $c$, leaving $R^2 / \gamma^2$ unchanged.
- **No assumption on data order.** Adversarial orderings can hit the bound; favourable orderings finish faster. The *total* mistake count is invariant under reorderings.
- **Non-separable data.** The theorem fails — there is no $u$ with the required property. The perceptron will oscillate forever. The **pocket** variant (keep the best $w$ seen so far) is the classical workaround; in modern practice we use logistic regression with regularisation, which always has a unique solution.

<Aside type="history">
The original perceptron (Rosenblatt, 1958) used a step activation; modern courses replace the step with the sigmoid (1.2) so the loss becomes smooth and differentiable and we can fall back on standard SGD. For proving *convergence* on the classical hard-threshold model, the perceptron rule remains the simplest non-trivial example available — which is why we keep this proof.
</Aside>

## 6. A worked toy example

Two points in $\mathbb{R}^2$: $x_+ = (1, 0)$ with $y = +1$, $x_- = (-1, 0)$ with $y = -1$. Augment with the constant feature: $\tilde x_+ = (1, 0, 1)$, $\tilde x_- = (-1, 0, 1)$.

$R = \sqrt 2$ and $u = (1, 0, 0)$ achieves $y_i\, u^\top \tilde x_i = 1$ for both points, so $\gamma = 1$. Novikoff bound: $M \le 2 / 1 = 2$.

Start $w_0 = 0$. Both examples score $0$, so both trigger mistakes. Suppose we hit $\tilde x_+$ first:

$$
w_1 = 0 + (+1)\,(1, 0, 1) = (1, 0, 1).
$$

Now check $\tilde x_-$: the signed score is $y\, w_1^\top \tilde x_- = (-1)(-1 + 0 + 1) = 0$, still a mistake. Update:

$$
w_2 = w_1 + (-1)\,(-1, 0, 1) = (2, 0, 0).
$$

Both examples now score $+2 > 0$. Done. Two mistakes total, exactly hitting the Novikoff bound.

---

## 7. Takeaways

- Perceptron rule: $w_{k+1} = w_k + y_i x_i$ on mistakes, no-op on correct examples.
- Novikoff: $M \le R^2 / \gamma^2$ updates on a linearly separable dataset.
- Proof: two inductive inequalities and one Cauchy–Schwarz application.
- Margin is the central quantity; the same theme recurs in SVMs and in PAC-style generalisation bounds.
