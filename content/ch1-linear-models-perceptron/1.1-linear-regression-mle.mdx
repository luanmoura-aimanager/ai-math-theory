## 1. Why this session exists

Linear regression is the simplest non-trivial model in this course, and the one we can take apart most rigorously. Three independent stories converge on it:

- **Optimisation.** Minimise $\lVert y - X w \rVert^2$ (least squares).
- **Probability.** Maximise the likelihood of data under a Gaussian-noise observation model.
- **Geometry.** Orthogonally project $y$ onto the column space of $X$.

This session shows that the first two are *the same problem*, with the same minimiser given in closed form by the normal equations. The geometric picture comes for free.

---

## 2. The probabilistic model

Fix a design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ (rows are data points $x_i^\top$) and a target vector $y \in \mathbb{R}^m$. Assume

$$
y = X w^* + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, \sigma^2 I_m),
$$

with unknown true parameter $w^* \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and known noise variance $\sigma^2 > 0$. Conditional on $X$ and $w^*$,

$$
y \mid X, w^* \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(X w^*,\, \sigma^2 I_m).
$$

The likelihood of $y$ under parameter $w$ is the Gaussian density evaluated at the observed $y$:

$$
p(y \mid X, w) = \frac{1}{(2 \pi \sigma^2)^{m/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \lVert y - X w \rVert^2\right).
$$

(The covariance is $\sigma^2 I_m$, which gives the diagonal Gaussian; the quadratic form in the exponent is the squared Euclidean norm.)

---

## 3. From MLE to least squares

The MLE estimator is $w_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_w p(y \mid X, w)$. Logarithm is monotone, so

$$
w_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_w \log p(y \mid X, w).
$$

Taking the log,

$$
\log p(y \mid X, w) = -\frac{m}{2} \log(2 \pi \sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2 \sigma^2} \lVert y - X w \rVert^2.
$$

The first term is constant in $w$ and drops out. The factor $1 / (2 \sigma^2)$ is positive, so maximising the log-likelihood is equivalent to minimising the residual sum of squares:

$$
\boxed{\ w_{\text{MLE}} \;=\; \arg\min_w\, \lVert y - X w \rVert^2.\ }
$$

Least squares **is** MLE under Gaussian noise. This is the prototype: every standard loss we'll meet (BCE, multi-class CE, Huber, …) is the negative log-likelihood of some observation model.

<Aside type="note">
The minimiser does not depend on $\sigma^2$. Different noise levels give the *same* optimal $w$; they would matter only for posterior uncertainty (Bayesian linear regression, beyond this session).
</Aside>

---

## 4. The normal equations

Let $L(w) = \tfrac{1}{2} \lVert y - X w \rVert^2$. The factor of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is purely cosmetic — it makes the gradient clean.

Expand:

$$
L(w) = \tfrac{1}{2} (y - X w)^\top (y - X w) = \tfrac{1}{2} y^\top y - y^\top X w + \tfrac{1}{2} w^\top X^\top X w.
$$

Apply the matrix-calculus identities from 0.2 (numerator convention):

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\bigl[y^\top X w\bigr] = y^\top X, \qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\bigl[w^\top X^\top X w\bigr] = 2 w^\top X^\top X.
$$

(The second uses $\partial (w^\top A w) / \partial w = w^\top (A + A^\top) = 2 w^\top A$ when $A = X^\top X$ is symmetric.)

So

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = -y^\top X + w^\top X^\top X.
$$

Setting this row vector to zero and transposing,

$$
\boxed{\ X^\top X\, w \;=\; X^\top y.\ }
$$

These are the **normal equations**: a linear system of $n$ equations in $n$ unknowns.

**Existence and uniqueness.** From 0.8 §4 we know $\nabla^2 L = X^\top X \succeq 0$, so $L$ is convex; any stationary point is a global minimum. If $X$ has full column rank, $X^\top X \succ 0$ is invertible and

$$
w^* = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y.
$$

If $X$ is rank-deficient, the normal equations have a whole affine subspace of solutions; the *minimum-norm* solution is given by the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse $X^+ y$.

---

## 5. Geometric interpretation: projection

Let $\hat y := X w^*$. The first normal equation says

$$
X^\top (y - \hat y) = 0,
$$

i.e. the residual $r := y - \hat y$ is orthogonal to every column of $X$. So $\hat y$ is the **orthogonal projection** of $y$ onto $\operatorname{col}(X) \subset \mathbb{R}^m$, with projector matrix

$$
P_X := X (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top.
$$

Two sanity checks: $P_X^2 = P_X$ (idempotent) and $P_X^\top = P_X$ (symmetric) — the defining properties of an orthogonal projector.

<Aside type="note">
This is the standard MLE-under-Gaussian-noise derivation; the canonical textbook treatment is Bishop, *Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning* (2006) §3.1.1, and Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, *Elements of Statistical Learning* (2009) §3.2.
</Aside>

---

## 6. Quick computational note

Direct inversion of $X^\top X$ is numerically poor when $X$ is ill-conditioned (e.g. nearly-collinear features). In practice we either:

1. Factorise $X^\top X = R^\top R$ via **Cholesky** and back-substitute.
2. Compute a **QR decomposition** $X = QR$; then $w^* = R^{-1} Q^\top y$ — much better conditioned.
3. Use the **singular value decomposition** $X = U \Sigma V^\top$; then $w^* = V \Sigma^+ U^\top y$ even when $X$ is rank-deficient.

Numerical linear algebra is its own course; we'll mention these techniques again when Hessian-based optimisers come up in Chapter 3.

---

## 7. Takeaways

- The Gaussian-noise model turns MLE into least squares: $w_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\min \lVert y - X w \rVert^2$.
- The minimiser is unique when $X$ has full column rank: $w^* = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$.
- Fitted values $\hat y = P_X y$ are the orthogonal projection of the targets onto the column space.
- This is the template: each loss function in the rest of the course will be the negative log-likelihood of some observation model.
