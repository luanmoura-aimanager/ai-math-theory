## 1. Why this session exists

The backprop recursion from 2.6,

$$
\delta^{(\ell)} = \bigl((W^{(\ell+1)})^\top \delta^{(\ell+1)}\bigr) \odot g'^{(\ell)}(z^{(\ell)}),
$$

is a *product* over layers. Whatever happens at the top gets multiplied by a chain of Jacobian factors as it propagates to the bottom. If those factors are systematically $< 1$, the gradient signal **vanishes** before reaching the input layers. If they are systematically $> 1$, it **explodes**. Either way, training stalls — vanishing because layer-1 weights stop receiving meaningful updates; exploding because the optimiser overshoots into noise.

This session quantifies the chain and pins down why ReLU saved deep learning circa 2012.<FN ns={[1, 2]} />

---

## 2. The multiplicative chain

Iterating the recursion from layer $L$ down to layer $1$ and ignoring the matrix factors for a moment (treating them as "approximately norm-preserving" — we'll fix this in 2.10),

$$
\lVert \delta^{(1)} \rVert \;\approx\; \lVert \delta^{(L)} \rVert \cdot \prod_{\ell = 1}^{L - 1} \bigl|g'^{(\ell)}\bigl(z^{(\ell)}\bigr)\bigr|_{\text{typical}},
$$

with the product running from layer $1$ to layer $L - 1$ and "typical" meaning the geometric mean of the per-unit derivatives along the path. The size of the gradient at layer 1 is set by this product.

If $|g'| \approx c < 1$ at every layer, then $\lVert \delta^{(1)} \rVert \approx c^{L-1} \lVert \delta^{(L)} \rVert$. For sigmoid, $c \le 1/4$, so depth $L = 10$ kills the gradient by a factor of $(1/4)^9 \approx 4 \times 10^{-6}$. **Vanishing gradient.**

If $|g'| \approx c > 1$, then $c^{L-1}$ blows up. **Exploding gradient.**

The matrix factors $(W^{(\ell+1)})^\top$ can rescue or destroy this in either direction — which is why we'll spend all of 2.10 on initialisation.

---

## 3. Sigmoid: vanishing by construction

Recall from 2.3 that $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z)) \le 1/4$, with equality only at $z = 0$. At typical operating points $z = \pm 2$, $\sigma'(z) \approx 0.10$. So the multiplicative factor at each layer is at most $0.25$, and usually around $0.10$.

For depth $L = 10$, $\lVert \delta^{(1)} \rVert / \lVert \delta^{(L)} \rVert \in (10^{-10},\, 10^{-6})$, depending on operating points. The gradient at layer 1 is *not* getting useful training signal. **This is the entire reason sigmoid hidden layers were abandoned around 2010–2012.**

Tanh is slightly better: $\tanh'(z) \in (0, 1]$, with maximum $1$ at $z = 0$. But for $|z| > 2$ the derivative is again close to $0$, and the chain still shrinks. Tanh deep networks were trainable but slow; the AlexNet jump in 2012 was largely about replacing them with ReLU.

---

## 4. ReLU: vanishing is gone (on the active side)

For ReLU, $\operatorname{ReLU}'(z) \in \{0, 1\}$. On the *active* side ($z > 0$), the multiplicative factor is exactly $1$ — gradient passes through undamped. On the *dead* side ($z < 0$), it is $0$ — gradient is killed completely for that unit.

The key statistical fact: a *path* from layer 1 to the loss is alive iff *every* ReLU along the way is active. If we initialise weights so each ReLU is active with probability $1/2$ (which is what symmetry around zero gives), then a single fixed path of length $L$ is alive with probability $2^{-L}$. **But there are exponentially many paths**, and they share weights — so the effective gradient at layer 1 stays bounded as $L$ grows, *provided* the weight matrices have the right scale.

That "right scale" is the **He initialisation** of 2.10: $\operatorname{Var}(W^{(\ell)}_{i, j}) = 2 / d_{\ell - 1}$. It exactly compensates for the factor of $1/2$ from dead ReLUs.

---

## 5. The exploding side

For unbounded activations or aggressively scaled weights, the per-step product can be $> 1$, and the gradient explodes exponentially. Classical example: linear RNNs (no nonlinearity, weights unbounded) — the backprop product becomes the matrix power $(W^\top)^T$ for $T$ time steps, and the largest eigenvalue of $W$ controls everything.

The clean clinical fix is **gradient clipping**: after computing the gradient, scale it down to a fixed norm threshold $c$,

$$
g \leftarrow g \cdot \min\!\left(1, \frac{c}{\lVert g \rVert}\right).
$$

This caps the maximum step size in parameter space and prevents the optimiser from veering off into infinity. Standard practice in RNNs and Transformers; clip threshold is typically $c = 1.0$.

<Aside type="note">
A subtler form of explosion happens *during the forward pass* in deep Transformers, where residual streams accumulate without bound. The fix is **layer normalisation** (Chapter 6), which keeps activations bounded after each residual step. Norm-based stabilisation of the forward and backward passes is one of the design choices that made very deep Transformers trainable.
</Aside>

---

## 6. What this implies for activation choice

- **Avoid pure sigmoid / tanh in hidden layers.** Their derivatives are bounded above by $1/4$ and $1$ respectively, and saturate fast. The gradient chain shrinks geometrically with depth.
- **Prefer ReLU / GELU / SiLU.** Their derivatives are bounded by $\sim 1$ everywhere on the positive side and don't go far above $1$ on the negative side. The chain is roughly preserved.
- **Use the right initialisation** (2.10) so the matrix factors don't unilaterally rescale the chain.
- **Use residual connections** (Chapters 4, 5) — they bypass the multiplicative chain entirely by adding the input to the output, $a^{(\ell)} = a^{(\ell-1)} + f^{(\ell)}(a^{(\ell-1)})$.

The combination of "good activation + good init + residuals + layernorm" is essentially the entire story of how networks with 50–100+ layers became routine in the 2015–2020 stretch.

---

## 7. Takeaways

- The backprop signal at layer $1$ is a *product* of per-layer Jacobian norms.
- Sigmoid: typical factor $\le 1/4$ → vanishing for $L > 10$.
- ReLU: typical factor $= 1$ on the active side → vanishing solved (modulo dying units).
- Exploding: rare with bounded activations, common in unbounded ones; fix with gradient clipping.
- Modern recipe: ReLU/GELU + He init + residual connections + layernorm.
