## 1. Why this session exists

Chapter 1 built single-layer models. Chapter 2 builds **multilayer** models — every neural network in this course will be an instance of the structure we write down here. The whole engine is one diagram and one composition: stack affine maps and pointwise nonlinearities, alternating, and you have an MLP.

The hard part isn't the definition; it's notation. We're going to fix a single, consistent notation now and use it for the rest of the course.

---

## 2. One-hidden-layer MLP

Inputs $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0}$. Output $\hat y \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L}$ (whatever the task demands: a single logit for binary, $K$ logits for $K$-class).

A **single hidden layer** with $d_1$ units computes

$$
\begin{aligned}
z^{(1)} &= W^{(1)} x + b^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1}, \\
a^{(1)} &= g\bigl(z^{(1)}\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1}, \\
\hat y &= W^{(2)} a^{(1)} + b^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_2},
\end{aligned}
$$

with weight matrices $W^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1 \times d_0}$ and $W^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_2 \times d_1}$, biases $b^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_1}$ and $b^{(2)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_2}$, and a **pointwise activation** $g$ applied entry-by-entry. For classification we then push $\hat y$ through a sigmoid or softmax; for regression we leave it linear.

For multi-class classification specifically, the final output of the network *and* the prediction in probability space are

$$
z^{(L)} \in \mathbb{R}^K \quad \text{(logits)}, \qquad \hat y = \operatorname{softmax}(z^{(L)}) \in \Delta^{K-1}.
$$

---

## 3. The general $L$-layer MLP

Generalising the one-hidden-layer case to $L$ layers:

$$
a^{(0)} := x, \qquad
\begin{cases}
z^{(\ell)} = W^{(\ell)} a^{(\ell-1)} + b^{(\ell)}, \\
a^{(\ell)} = g^{(\ell)}\bigl(z^{(\ell)}\bigr),
\end{cases}
\quad \ell = 1, \dots, L,
$$

with $W^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell \times d_{\ell-1}}$, $b^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}$, and each $g^{(\ell)}$ a pointwise nonlinearity (or the identity for a final regression head). The full network is the **composition**

$$
\boxed{\ f(x) \;=\; g^{(L)}\!\Bigl(W^{(L)}\, g^{(L-1)}\!\bigl(W^{(L-1)} \cdots g^{(1)}(W^{(1)} x + b^{(1)}) \cdots + b^{(L-1)}\bigr) + b^{(L)}\Bigr).\ }
$$

Read it out loud once. Every neural network in this course — convolutional, recurrent, Transformer — is structurally this composition, with extra structure imposed on each $W^{(\ell)}$ (sparsity, weight-sharing, attention).

---

## 4. Notation we'll keep for the rest of the course

- $L$ — number of layers (depth).
- $d_\ell$ — width of layer $\ell$ ($d_0$ is the input dimension, $d_L$ the output dimension).
- $z^{(\ell)} = W^{(\ell)} a^{(\ell-1)} + b^{(\ell)}$ — **pre-activation** at layer $\ell$.
- $a^{(\ell)} = g^{(\ell)}(z^{(\ell)})$ — **activation** at layer $\ell$. $a^{(0)} = x$.
- $W^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell \times d_{\ell-1}}$ — weight matrix mapping layer $\ell - 1$ to layer $\ell$. Rows index outputs, columns index inputs.
- $b^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}$ — bias of layer $\ell$.
- $g^{(\ell)}$ — pointwise activation (sigmoid, ReLU, GELU, identity, …).

The parameters of the network are $\theta = \{W^{(\ell)}, b^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell = 1}^L$, totalling

$$
\#\theta = \sum_{\ell = 1}^L \bigl(d_\ell\, d_{\ell - 1} + d_\ell\bigr) \quad \text{scalars}.
$$

For a moderate net with $L = 6$ and $d_\ell \approx 1000$, this is on the order of $6 \times 10^6$ parameters; for a Transformer LM it grows to $10^{10}$. Same notation throughout.

<Aside type="note">
Some older treatments (e.g., older deep-learning textbooks and the original PDP volumes) use $a^{(j)}_i = g(z^{(j-1)}_i)$, with subscripts shifted by one layer. The convention adopted here aligns the indices: $a^{(\ell)}$ and $z^{(\ell)}$ both refer to layer $\ell$. If you encounter the shifted convention elsewhere, the math is identical — only the index labels move.
</Aside>

## 5. The two "linear" layer types coming later

Every layer in this chapter is **fully connected**: $W^{(\ell)}$ is dense, every output unit sees every input unit. Two cousins we'll meet later:

- **Convolutional** (Chapter 4). $W^{(\ell)}$ is a structured sparse matrix that re-uses the same small filter across spatial positions. Many fewer parameters for the same $d_\ell, d_{\ell-1}$.
- **Self-attention** (Chapter 6). $W^{(\ell)}$ depends on the input itself, $W^{(\ell)} = f(x)$ — a *data-dependent* linear layer. Same composition structure, dynamic weights.

The general MLP is the prototype. The rest are special cases.

---

## 6. The compositional perspective

Three takeaways from the composition view.

1. **Each layer is a learned feature map.** $a^{(\ell)}$ is a representation of the input. Deeper layers tend to be more abstract; we'll formalise this in 2.2 (universal approximation) and in Chapter 5 (embeddings).
2. **The chain rule is the engine.** All of backprop is the chain rule from 0.3 applied to this composition. Nothing new about derivatives; everything new about *organising* the derivative computation efficiently.
3. **Nonlinearity is essential.** Without the $g^{(\ell)}$'s, every $f$ reduces to a single linear map $W = W^{(L)} \cdots W^{(1)}$, and we'd be back to the perceptron of Chapter 1. The composition only buys expressive power when at least one $g^{(\ell)}$ is nonlinear — the XOR result of 1.6 is the prototype.

---

## 7. Takeaways

- An MLP is a composition of affine maps and pointwise nonlinearities.
- Parameters: $\theta = \{W^{(\ell)}, b^{(\ell)}\}$.
- The notation $(z^{(\ell)}, a^{(\ell)}, W^{(\ell)}, b^{(\ell)}, g^{(\ell)})$ carries through the entire course.
- Without nonlinearities, depth gains nothing; XOR is the canonical witness.
