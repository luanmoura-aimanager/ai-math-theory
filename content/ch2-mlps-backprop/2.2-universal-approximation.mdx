## 1. Why this session exists

XOR (1.6) showed that *one* hidden layer is enough to compute a non-linear-separable Boolean function. The natural question — *how powerful is one hidden layer in general?* — was answered by Cybenko (1989) and Hornik (1991)<FN n={2} />: arbitrarily powerful, in a precise approximation sense. This is the **universal approximation theorem** (UAT).

The theorem is important for what it asserts (existence of an approximator) and *equally* important for what it doesn't assert (efficient training, reasonable size, depth efficiency). This session does both halves.

---

## 2. Statement

**Theorem (Cybenko, 1989, sigmoidal case)<FN n={1} />.** Let $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to [0, 1]$ be a continuous **sigmoidal** function: $\sigma(z) \to 1$ as $z \to +\infty$, $\sigma(z) \to 0$ as $z \to -\infty$. Let $K \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ be compact and $f : K \to \mathbb{R}$ continuous. Then for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exist $N \in \mathbb{N}$, weights $W \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$, biases $b \in \mathbb{R}^N$, output weights $v \in \mathbb{R}^N$ such that the **one-hidden-layer network**

$$
F_N(x) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^N v_i\, \sigma(W_i^\top x + b_i)
$$

satisfies

$$
\sup_{x \in K} \bigl|f(x) - F_N(x)\bigr| \;<\; \varepsilon.
$$

**Hornik's extension (1991).** Replace "sigmoidal" by "any non-polynomial continuous activation". The theorem still holds. So ReLU, GELU, tanh, swish — all are universal approximators.

In words: with enough hidden units, a *single* hidden layer can approximate *any* continuous function on *any* bounded region to *any* accuracy.<FN ns={[5, 6, 7]} />

---

## 3. What the theorem says and does not say

Four things to internalise.

**(a) Universality is existential, not constructive.** The theorem promises that *some* $(W, b, v)$ realising $\varepsilon$-approximation exists. It says nothing about how to *find* them. Training, optimisation, generalisation — none of those questions are addressed.

**(b) The width $N$ is uncontrolled.** The theorem says "for every $\varepsilon$, there exists $N$". It does not bound $N$ in terms of $\varepsilon$ or in terms of the complexity of $f$. For pathological $f$ (high oscillation, sharp transitions) $N$ can be astronomically large. **Universality is not efficiency.**

**(c) It is shallow universality.** UAT is a statement about *one* hidden layer. It doesn't say a deep network is needed or even helpful. The reason we use depth in practice is **not** that shallow nets cannot represent the target — they can — but that deep nets often represent natural targets with *exponentially fewer* parameters. This is a separation result (Telgarsky 2016 and follow-ups)<FN n={3} />, not a universality result.

**(d) Continuity matters.** UAT covers continuous targets on compact domains. It says nothing about discontinuous functions, unbounded domains, or functions on discrete spaces. The right tool for piecewise-constant or discrete targets is a different theory (decision trees / boosting).

<Aside type="warning">
A common over-reading: "deep learning works because of UAT". This is wrong. UAT predicts that a shallow net with enough units can fit anything. The empirical surprise of deep learning is that gradient descent on *finite-width* networks finds *generalising* solutions — that is the deep-learning story (Chapter 3, implicit bias, NTK, etc.), and it is **not** what UAT is about.
</Aside>

## 4. Why it's true: intuition only

We won't prove UAT — the original arguments use the Hahn–Banach theorem (Cybenko) and the Stone–Weierstrass theorem (Hornik). The intuition is short.

Pick a sigmoidal $\sigma$. By scaling and shifting, $\sigma(W^\top x + b)$ is a **soft indicator** of a half-space: it ramps from $\approx 0$ on one side of $\{W^\top x + b = 0\}$ to $\approx 1$ on the other, with a sharp transition that gets sharper as we scale $W$.

A *sum* of two such soft indicators — one with positive weight, one with negative weight — gives a **soft bump** that is $\approx 1$ inside a slab and $\approx 0$ outside. Sums of soft bumps approximate any function that is approximately constant on small slabs. Continuous functions are uniformly approximable by such slab-step functions on a compact set (the Stone–Weierstrass step). Hence sums of sigmoids approximate continuous functions. QED, very roughly.

ReLU and other non-polynomial activations admit similar constructions: sums of $\operatorname{ReLU}(w^\top x + b)$ produce piecewise-affine continuous functions, and those are dense in $C(K)$.

## 5. The depth-width trade-off

UAT leaves an obvious follow-up question: *for the same approximation accuracy*, can we use *fewer total parameters* by going deep instead of wide?

Yes. Two concrete results.

- **Telgarsky (2016).** There exist functions on $[0, 1]$ that a depth-$L$ ReLU net realises with $\Theta(L)$ units, but any depth-$\mathcal{O}(1)$ net needs $\Omega(2^L)$ units to approximate within $\varepsilon$. Depth can be **exponentially** parameter-efficient.
- **Eldan & Shamir (2016)<FN n={4} />.** A separation between depth-2 and depth-3 networks in $\mathbb{R}^d$: certain targets require $e^{\Omega(d)}$ hidden units at depth 2 but only polynomial size at depth 3.

These results say: depth is a *parameter-efficient* knob for representing functions that compose simple pieces. Empirically this matches what we observe — deep nets on hierarchical data (images, text) use orders of magnitude fewer parameters than the shallow equivalents.

## 7. Takeaways

- **Universal approximation.** One hidden layer with a non-polynomial continuous activation can approximate any continuous function on a compact set to arbitrary precision.
- **What UAT does not say.** How big the network must be; how to train it; whether deep is better than wide; anything about generalisation from a finite sample.
- **Depth–width trade-off.** Depth can be exponentially parameter-efficient for naturally compositional targets (Telgarsky, Eldan–Shamir).
- Empirically, depth is preferred — UAT is the *floor* of network expressivity, not the ceiling.
